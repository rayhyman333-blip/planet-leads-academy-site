From finding middle C to playing hands-together pieces with expression.

Lesson 1: Posture, Hand Position, and Finding Your Way Around the Keyboard

This lesson teaches you how to sit at the piano and shape your hands so that playing feels natural from the very first note. Everything later in this course - scales, chords, and pieces played with both hands - builds directly on the physical habits you set up today. Good posture and hand position prevent the two problems that stop most beginners: tension and soreness that make practice unpleasant, and sloppy habits that cap how fast and cleanly you can play later. Give this lesson a full practice session on its own. It may feel like you are not really playing music yet, but this foundation work pays off every single time you sit down at the keys.

Start with your seat. Sit on the front half of the bench, not leaning back against anything, with both feet flat on the floor. Your bench height is right when your forearms are roughly level with the keys - if your elbows sit well below your hands you are too low, and if your shoulders creep up toward your ears you are too high. Slide forward or back until your fingertips can rest comfortably on the keys with your arms slightly bent and your shoulders hanging loose. Sit tall through your back without locking it stiff, and lean just slightly forward from the hips. You should feel balanced and free to move, not perched and not slouched.

Now shape your hands. Let your arms hang at your sides for a moment and notice the natural curve of your relaxed fingers - that is the shape you want on the keys, as if you were gently holding a small ball. Play on your fingertips, meaning the firm pad right behind the nail, not the flat middle of the finger. Your thumb plays on its outer edge near the tip and angles slightly toward you. Keep your wrists level with the backs of your hands: not drooping below the keys and not arched high above them. Your knuckles should stay gently rounded, never collapsed flat. Shake your hands out completely loose, then rebuild this shape slowly three times until it feels familiar.

Next, learn the geography of the keyboard. Look at the black keys: they fall into repeating groups of two, then three, then two, then three, all the way across. Every white key named C sits immediately to the left of a group of two black keys - find all of them now. The C nearest the middle of the keyboard is called middle C, and it is your home base for this course. Name the white keys around it: D sits between the two black keys, E sits just to the right of them, F sits just to the left of the group of three, and G, A, and B follow in order across that group. Play each one slowly as you name it, and notice how the two-then-three pattern repeats identically in every octave.

Time for drills. First, the C-finder: starting at the lowest C, point to every C on the keyboard, say its name out loud, and play it. Travel up and back down five times without rushing. Second, the five-finger set: place your right hand with the thumb on middle C and the remaining fingers resting on D, E, F, and G. Play each note in order, one at a time, four times through, listening for an even tone on every key. Third, the reset drill: lift both hands completely off the keys, shake them loose, and rebuild your hand shape ten times in a row. Speed does not matter here - accuracy of the shape is the whole point.

Two common mistakes to watch for. The first is collapsed wrists: beginners often let their wrists sink below the level of the keys, which strains the wrist and weakens the fingers. Freeze mid-practice, glance at your wrists, and lift them back to level. The second is flat fingers: pressing with the flat pad of the finger instead of the fingertip gives a mushy tone and slow, clumsy movement. If your sound is uneven or your fingers feel tangled, check that each fingertip is standing on its key.

Practice: Sit down and rebuild your posture and hand position from scratch. Place your right-hand fingers on C-D-E-F-G around middle C and play the five notes up and down, slowly, eight times through. Before every repetition, run the checklist: feet flat, forearms level, shoulders loose, wrists level, fingers curved. Finish by finding and playing every C on the keyboard twice, naming each one aloud.

Lesson 2: Reading Music - The Staff, Treble Clef, Note Values and Rhythm

This lesson teaches you to read written music: the staff, the treble clef, and how note values and rhythm work. Reading is simply a map - it tells you which key to press, how long to hold it, and when. You do not need to memorize everything today; you need to understand the system and be able to work a short line of music out slowly. Every piece in this course is written down, so the reading skills you build here unlock everything that follows.

The staff is the set of five horizontal lines that music is written on, with four spaces between them. In the treble clef - the curly symbol at the start that wraps around the second line - the lines from bottom to top are E, G, B, D, F, which many players remember with the phrase "Every Good Boy Does Fine." The spaces from bottom to top spell F, A, C, E, which spells the word "face." Notes can also sit on short extra lines above or below the staff, called ledger lines; middle C, for example, sits on a ledger line just below the treble staff. Point to random lines and spaces and name them until the answers come without hesitation.

Now rhythm. A whole note is an open oval held for four beats. A half note is an open oval with a stem, held for two beats. A quarter note is a filled oval with a stem, held for one beat. An eighth note looks like a quarter note with a flag or beam and lasts half a beat - two of them fit into one beat. Rests are the symbols for silence, and they follow the same values: a whole rest hangs from a line and means four beats of silence, a quarter rest means one beat. The time signature at the start of a piece tells you the beat: 4/4 means four beats per measure, with the quarter note getting one beat. Count every rhythm out loud before you ever play it.

Time for drills. First, note-naming: point at a printed staff or draw one yourself, and name thirty notes in a row on lines and spaces, aiming for no pauses. Second, clap-and-count: clap the rhythm "quarter, quarter, half, whole" while counting "1, 2, 3-4, 1-2-3-4" out loud, and repeat it ten times until the counting feels automatic. Third, mixed rhythm reading: clap a line that mixes quarter notes, eighth-note pairs, and half notes, counting steadily, five times through. If you lose the count, slow down rather than stopping - a steady slow beat beats a fast broken one every time.

Extend your reading a little further with ledger lines above the staff. Just as middle C sits on a ledger line below, the notes continue upward: the space above the top line is G, the ledger line above that is A, and the space above that is B. Practice these the same way - point, name, check - adding ten high notes to your thirty-note naming drill. Then try the say-and-play method: look at a note, say its letter name out loud, and only then play it. This two-step process feels slow, but it builds the direct eye-to-key connection that fluent reading requires. After a week of say-and-play, drop the spoken step and let your eyes go straight to your fingers.

Two common mistakes. The first is ignoring rhythm while reading pitches: beginners focus so hard on which note to play that they hold every note for the same length. Always learn the rhythm first - clap it - then add the notes. The second is not counting out loud. Silent counting collapses under pressure; your voice keeps your timing honest. Count every beat, even when a note is held or a rest is silent, until steady counting becomes a habit you no longer have to think about.

Practice: Draw or print a short eight-measure line in 4/4 using only quarter notes, half notes, and whole notes on the treble staff. First clap and count the full rhythm three times. Then name every pitch out

loud from start to finish. Finally, play the line on the piano at a slow, steady tempo, counting aloud as you play, four times through without stopping.

Lesson 3: Five-Finger Patterns and Your First Scale (C Major)

This lesson teaches you five-finger patterns and your first full scale, C major. A scale is the raw material of melody: most tunes you will ever play are built from the notes of a scale, and practicing scales trains your fingers to move in the smooth, even way that good playing requires. By the end of this lesson you will play the C major scale up and down with both hands, one octave, at a steady tempo.

First, finger numbers, because piano music often marks which finger to use: on each hand the thumb is 1, the index finger is 2, the middle finger is 3, the ring finger is 4, and the little finger is 5. A five-finger pattern is exactly what it sounds like - all five fingers resting on five neighboring white keys, played in order. Place your right-hand thumb (finger 1) on middle C and let fingers 2 through 5 fall on D, E, F, and G. Play 1-2-3-4-5-4-3-2-1 slowly, keeping every note even. Repeat with the left hand, where finger 5 (the little finger) starts on the C below middle C.

The C major scale is eight notes: C-D-E-F-G-A-B-C. It uses only white keys, which is why it is the perfect first scale. Going up with the right hand, the fingering is 1-2-3, then the thumb tucks under the hand to play F, and fingers 2-3-4-5 finish the climb: C(1)-D(2)-E(3)-F(1)-G(2)-A(3)-B(4)-C(5). Coming down, reverse it: 5-4-3-2-1-3-2-1, crossing finger 3 over the thumb. The left hand going up is 5-4-3-2-1-3-2-1, and coming down is 1-2-3-1-2-3-4-5. Learn one hand at a time, hands separately, before ever combining them. Play slowly enough that every note sounds clean and connected.

The tricky moment is the thumb tuck: as finger 3 plays E, the thumb must already be moving under the palm toward F, arriving exactly when it is needed - not before, which breaks the line, and not late, which causes a gap. Think of the hand gliding sideways smoothly rather than jumping. Practice the tuck in isolation: play E-F-E-F with fingers 3-1, thumb passing under, twenty times slowly, listening for zero gap between the notes. Then add the surrounding notes until the full octave flows.

Time for drills. First, five-finger patterns: play the C-D-E-F-G pattern up and down with each hand, four times each, at a comfortable tempo. Second, one-octave scales hands separately: right hand up and down, then left hand up and down, three times each, counting "1-2-3-4-5-6-7-8" as you climb. Third, the metronome ladder: set a metronome to a slow pulse, play one note per click, and only raise the speed after three perfect repetitions at the current one. Never raise the tempo to cover up unevenness - fix the unevenness first.

Once the scale is even hands separately, try it hands together. Start both hands on C an octave apart - right thumb on middle C, left little finger on the C below - and climb in parallel motion, both hands ascending together. Keep the same fingerings you learned for each hand; the only new challenge is coordination, so drop the tempo back to slow. Play the hands-together scale up and down four times. Then try contrary motion: both hands start together on middle C and move outward in opposite directions, mirroring each other. Contrary motion is often easier at first because the thumb tucks happen at the same moment in both hands - a satisfying milestone that proves your hands are starting to work as a team.

Two common mistakes. The first is lifting the fingers too high between notes, which wastes motion and breaks the smooth, connected sound scales need. Keep your fingers close to the keys, moving just enough to strike and release. The second is rushing the thumb crossover: the notes before and after

the tuck get squeezed or stretched. If your scale sounds lumpy at the crossover, slow down and give that one spot ten extra repetitions until it evens out.

Practice: Play the C major scale one octave, hands separately, up and down, six times through with each hand. Use a metronome at a slow tempo, one note per click, and do not increase the speed until all six repetitions are even and clean. End with the E-F-E-F thumb-tuck drill, twenty slow repetitions with the right hand.

Lesson 4: Chords - Building Triads, the C-G-Am-F Progression

This lesson teaches you chords: how to build them and how to play the most useful progression in popular music, C-G-Am-F. A chord is three or more notes played at the same time, and chords are the harmony underneath melodies - the sound that makes a song feel full. By the end of this lesson you will play all four chords cleanly and change between them in rhythm, which is the core skill behind accompanying songs on the piano.

A triad is the basic three-note chord: a root note, a third above it, and a fifth above it. Stack every other white key and you get one. A major triad, like C major, stacks a larger-sounding third: C-E-G. A minor triad stacks a smaller third and sounds sadder or darker: A minor is A-C-E. To build any major triad, count four half-steps (a half-step is the move from any key to its nearest neighbor, black or white) from the root to the middle note, then three more half-steps to the top. For a minor triad, it is three half-steps then four. Build C major (C-E-G), G major (G-B-D), A minor (A-C-E), and F major (F-A-C) note by note on the keyboard, saying each chord name as you play it.

Now the progression. In the key of C, the chords C, G, Am, and F appear together in countless songs, and practicing them as a loop trains the exact hand movements real music uses. Play them in the right hand as block chords - all three notes pressed at once: C (C-E-G), G (G-B-D), Am (A-C-E), F (F-A-C). Notice the smooth voice leading: between C and G, some notes stay the same or move by a single step, and the same small movements connect the rest. Keep your wrist loose and let the hand reshape itself for each chord rather than lifting high off the keys.

Time for drills. First, chord spelling: say the three notes of each chord out loud, then play them, cycling C-G-Am-F five times. Second, chord changes in rhythm: play each chord for four beats - C for four, G for four, Am for four, F for four - and repeat the loop eight times, changing exactly on the beat with no pause. Third, the faster-change challenge: play each chord for just two beats, then one beat each, forcing quicker, cleaner changes. If a change is late, slow the tempo instead of accepting the gap.

There is one more trick that makes your chord changes sound professional: inversions. So far you have played every chord in root position, with the root note at the bottom. But C-E-G can be rearranged as E-G-C (first inversion) or G-C-E (second inversion) - same three notes, same chord, different order. Inversions let your hand barely move between chords: try playing C in root position (C-E-G), then G in first inversion (B-D-G), then Am in root position (A-C-E), then F in first inversion (A-C-F). Notice how the hand glides instead of jumping. Practice the C-G-Am-F loop with these smooth inversions four times through, listening to how connected the progression sounds compared to all root positions.

Two common mistakes. The first is accidentally pressing extra notes: a finger drifting onto a neighboring key turns your triad into a muddy cluster. After each chord, listen - a clean triad rings clearly, a mistake sounds cloudy. Lift and reset rather than holding a wrong note. The second is a stiff, pounding touch: beginners often hammer chords because three fingers feel harder to control than one. Chords should sound no louder than your single-note melodies. Practice them softly on purpose until control replaces force.

Practice: Loop C-G-Am-F as block chords in the right hand, four beats per chord, for five full minutes at a steady tempo, counting "1-2-3-4" for each chord. Keep a simple written tally of clean loops versus loops with a mistake, and aim for ten clean loops in a row before you stop.

Lesson 5: The Left Hand - Bass Notes and Simple Accompaniment Patterns

This lesson gives your left hand a real job: playing bass notes and simple accompaniment patterns. Until now your right hand has done most of the work, but in real music the left hand provides the foundation - the low notes that anchor the harmony and the rhythmic patterns that make a piece move. You will also meet the bass clef, the staff your left hand reads, and by the end of the lesson you will play three accompaniment patterns under the C-G-Am-F progression.

The bass clef is the symbol that looks like a backward C with two dots, and it wraps around the fourth line, which is the note F - that line is sometimes called the F line. From bottom to top, the bass clef lines are G-B-D-F-A ("Good Boys Do Fine Always") and the spaces are A-C-E-G ("All Cows Eat Grass"). Middle C sits on a ledger line just above the bass staff. Take ten minutes to name bass-clef notes the same way you learned the treble clef: point, name, and check, thirty notes in a row.

Start with the simplest left-hand job: root notes. Play the root of each chord - C under the C chord, G under G, A under Am, F under F - as single whole notes, one per measure, while your right hand plays the chords. Listen to how the low note grounds the sound. Next, the root-fifth pattern: under each chord, alternate the root and the fifth above it (for C: C then G; for G: G then D; for Am: A then E; for F: F then C), playing half notes. This two-note bounce is the backbone of countless accompaniments. Finally, the broken-chord pattern: play the three chord tones one at a time, low to high and back (C-E-G-E), as quarter notes. Three patterns, one progression - this is real accompanying.

Time for drills. First, left hand alone: play the root-fifth pattern under C-G-Am-F, four beats per chord, six times through, listening for even tone between the two notes. Second, hands together in stages: play the progression with the left hand on roots and the right hand on block chords, four times; then switch the left hand to the root-fifth pattern, four times; then to broken chords, four times. Third, the quiet-left challenge: play the whole loop and deliberately make the left hand softer than the right - the bass should support, never cover, the chords above it.

Add one more pattern to your toolkit: the waltz bass. In 3/4 time - three beats per measure - the classic left-hand pattern is root on beat one, then the fifth on beats two and three: C (beat 1), G (beat 2), G (beat 3). This strong-weak-weak feel is the heartbeat of every waltz. Practice it under each chord of the progression: play C-G-Am-F with the waltz pattern, one measure per chord, six times through, emphasizing beat one slightly. Then try it hands together with simple block chords in the right hand on beats two and three only - the left hand carries beat one alone, which teaches your hands true rhythmic independence.

Two common mistakes. The first is a left hand that plays too loudly: the bass notes are naturally heavier, and your left hand presses harder without realizing it. Consciously lighten the left hand until you can hear the right-hand chords clearly on top. The second is a stiff left wrist from the unfamiliar patterns: if the wrist locks, the pattern becomes mechanical and the timing wobbles. Stop, shake the hand loose, and restart the pattern slower.

Practice: With the left hand alone, play the broken-chord pattern (root-third-fifth-third) under C-G-Am-F, four beats per chord, eight times through at a slow, steady tempo. Then add the right-hand block chords on top and play the full hands-together loop four times. Count every beat out loud until the patterns feel automatic. As a final challenge, play the loop once with the root-fifth pattern and once with

broken chords, alternating, for four full cycles without stopping. Write down which pattern felt weaker and make it the first drill of your next session.

Lesson 6: Hands Together - Your First Complete Piece, Step by Step

This lesson teaches you the method for playing hands together - the skill that turns two separate parts into real music. Your first complete piece is a simple sixteen-measure study in C major: the right hand plays a gentle melody built from the C major scale while the left hand plays the root-fifth accompaniment pattern from Lesson 5. More important than this one piece is the method itself: learn each hand alone, combine in tiny sections, and only then play the whole thing. This method works on every piece you will ever learn.

Step one: master each hand separately. Play the right-hand melody alone until you can play it three times in a row without a mistake at a slow tempo - not approximately, but cleanly. Then do the same with the left-hand accompaniment alone. If either hand is not solid on its own, combining them will collapse, so do not skip this stage. Step two: combine just two measures. Play measures 1 and 2 hands together, very slowly, counting every beat out loud. When those two measures are clean three times in a row, add measures 3 and 4. Build the piece two measures at a time until all sixteen are joined.

Step three: connect the sections. Play measures 1 through 4, then 1 through 8, then 1 through 12, then the full sixteen, always at the same slow tempo. This overlapping method means you never practice the seams badly - the joints between sections get as much attention as the sections themselves. Step four: only when the full piece is clean at the slow tempo do you raise the speed, one small step at a time, using the metronome ladder from Lesson 3. A piece that is clean and slow is music; a piece that is fast and full of stumbles is just noise.

Time for drills. First, the two-measure loop: take the hardest two measures of the piece and play them hands together ten times in a row, slowly, until they feel easy. Second, the rhythm-only run: tap the right-hand rhythm on the closed piano lid with one hand while playing the left-hand part with the other, four times through - this isolates the coordination without pitch worries. Third, the cold-start test: close the music, wait a minute, and play the first four measures from memory twice. If you cannot start cold, the piece is not learned yet.

Apply the three-perfect-repetitions rule to every section: a two-measure chunk only counts as learned when you can play it hands together three times in a row with no mistakes at your practice tempo. Keep a simple tally on paper - three clean passes, then move on. This rule stops the most common practice trap: playing a section until it works once, then assuming it is learned, only to have it fall apart the next day. Three in a row proves the learning is stable. If you fail the third repetition, the count resets to zero - frustrating, but it forces the kind of deep repetition that actually sticks.

Two common mistakes. The first is raising the tempo too soon: beginners combine the hands at performance speed immediately, and the result is a tangle. The rule is simple - if you cannot play it slowly, you cannot play it fast. The second is stopping at every mistake and restarting from the beginning: this trains you to play the opening brilliantly and the ending never. When you stumble, keep going, note the bad spot, and drill that spot alone afterward.

Practice: Using the two-measures-at-a-time method, build the full sixteen-measure study hands together at a slow tempo. Play the complete piece four times through without stopping, counting aloud. Mark the two measures that gave you the most trouble, and drill each one ten extra times hands

together before you finish. Then play the piece one final time at a slightly faster tempo, keeping every note clean. If the faster run introduces mistakes, drop back to the slow tempo - speed is earned, never forced.

Lesson 7: Dynamics, Tempo, and Playing with Expression

This lesson teaches you to play musically: dynamics (loud and soft), tempo markings, and phrasing - the difference between pressing the right keys and making music. Two players can play identical notes and sound completely different, and the difference is expression. By the end of this lesson you will shape a simple piece with planned louds and softs, steady tempo control, and phrasing that breathes.

Start with the dynamic markings. The letter p stands for piano, meaning soft; f stands for forte, meaning loud. The m versions - mp (mezzo-piano, moderately soft) and mf (mezzo-forte, moderately loud) - sit in between. A hairpin opening to the right means crescendo, gradually louder; one closing means diminuendo, gradually softer. These are relative, not absolute: forte in a gentle piece is not the same volume as forte in a stormy one. Practice the dynamic ladder: play the C major scale up and down starting at p and growing to f by the top, then shrinking back to p - four times, making the change perfectly gradual.

Tempo markings tell you the speed and character. Common ones include largo (very slow and broad), andante (at a walking pace), moderato (at a moderate speed), and allegro (fast and lively). Ritardando, often shortened to rit., means gradually slowing down, usually near the end of a piece. Set your metronome to a walking-pace pulse and play a familiar five-finger pattern andante; then try the same pattern allegro and notice how the character changes even though the notes are identical. Never change tempo mid-piece unless the music asks for it - the number one sign of a beginner is a tempo that drifts faster whenever the easy part arrives.

Phrasing ties it together. A phrase is a musical sentence, usually two to four measures, and like a spoken sentence it has a shape: it breathes at the start, rises or grows toward the middle, and settles at the end. Mark the phrases in your sixteen-measure study from Lesson 6 - you will likely find four phrases of four measures each. Play each phrase with a gentle swell: start mp, grow slightly toward the middle of the phrase, and ease back at the phrase ending, lifting your hands slightly at each breath point as if the music were pausing to inhale. Record yourself on your phone and listen back: you will hear the phrasing clearly, or hear exactly where it is missing.

Add articulation to your expressive toolkit: how each individual note begins and ends. Legato means smooth and connected - each note holds until the next begins, like singing. Staccato means short and detached - the finger bounces off the key quickly, marked by a dot under or over the note. Practice both on a five-finger pattern: play C-D-E-F-G legato four times, listening for zero gaps, then staccato four times, listening for crisp, even short notes with silence between them. Then mix them: play your sixteen-measure study with the right-hand melody legato and the left-hand accompaniment slightly detached. The contrast alone makes the piece sound twice as musical, before you change a single dynamic.

Two common mistakes. The first is playing everything at one volume: beginners default to mezzo-forte for an entire piece, which flattens the music. Exaggerate your dynamics in practice - play the softs softer and the louds louder than feels natural - because under pressure everything shrinks toward the middle. The second is ignoring the markings entirely and playing only by feel: feel is wonderful, but only after you have honored what is written. Play the dynamics as marked first, then add your own shaping on top.

Practice: Take the sixteen-measure study from Lesson 6 and write in a dynamic plan: mark each four-measure phrase mp, mf, mp, mf, add one crescendo and one diminuendo, and mark a ritardando in the final two measures. Play the piece four times following your markings exactly, then play it twice more with your own expressive shaping added. Record the last run and listen back for the phrasing.

Lesson 8: Your Practice System + Next Pieces to Learn

This lesson builds your permanent practice system and maps out where to go next. Talent is mostly a myth in music: what separates players who improve from players who stall is not ability but the quality and consistency of their practice. This lesson gives you a repeatable session structure, a weekly plan, and a clear list of next pieces, so you never again sit down wondering what to do.

The session structure has four blocks. Block one is warm-up, five minutes: hand-shape resets, five-finger patterns, and one slow scale, reminding your hands what good feels like. Block two is technique, five to ten minutes: whatever is currently hardest - thumb tucks, chord changes, a new accompaniment pattern - drilled slowly with the metronome. Block three is pieces, ten to fifteen minutes: your current piece, worked with the two-measures-at-a-time method, never just played through on autopilot. Block four is review and fun, five minutes: play something you already know well and enjoy, ending every session on a win. Twenty-five to thirty-five minutes total, and the order matters: technique before pieces, review last.

The weekly plan spreads the work. Practice five or six days a week rather than cramming: thirty focused minutes beats a two-hour weekend marathon because your brain consolidates the learning between sessions, while you sleep. Assign each day a small focus - Monday: scales and the new piece's hard spots; Tuesday: chords and progressions; Wednesday: the piece, sections only; Thursday: review plus dynamics work; Friday: full run-throughs recorded on your phone; weekend: one light session or rest. Keep the written practice log from your materials: date, minutes, what you worked on, and one honest note about what improved.

Where to go next: five directions. One, a second scale - G major, which adds one black key (F sharp) and teaches you the same thumb-tuck in new territory. Two, a minor-key piece: the A minor scale and a simple minor melody, which will make your playing sound instantly more emotional. Three, a waltz in 3/4 time, which teaches you a new time signature and the strong-weak-weak bass pattern. Four, a piece where both hands share melodic lines in places - simple counterpoint that builds real hand independence. Five, a lead-sheet song: melody in the right hand with chord symbols above, where you invent the left hand from the patterns in Lesson 5 - your first step toward playing songs your own way.

Once a month, hold a review session instead of a normal practice. Play through everything you have learned - scales, the progression, your piece - and record it all. Listen back with your practice log open and ask three questions: what is clearly better than last month, what has not moved at all, and what is the single weakest skill right now. Write the answers down, then set exactly one goal for the coming month, such as "play the C major scale hands together at a steady moderate tempo" or "change C-G-Am-F with no gaps." One goal keeps your practice focused; five goals means none of them happen. Date the goal in your log, and check it off the day you achieve it.

Two common mistakes. The first is practicing only what you already play well: it feels good and improves nothing. Spend at least half of every session on the hardest thing, not the favorite thing. The second is the autopilot run-through: playing a piece start to finish five times while thinking about dinner. If your mind wanders, stop, pick the worst measure, and drill it ten times - focused repetitions beat mindless ones by a wide margin.

Practice: Write out your weekly plan using the four-block session structure and the day-by-day focus above. Do one full session today, fill in the practice log honestly, and record a run-through of your

sixteen-measure study. Listen back, write down the single weakest spot, and make it tomorrow's technique block.
