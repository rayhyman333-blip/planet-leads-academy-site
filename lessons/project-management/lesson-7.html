This lesson teaches the monitoring phase: tracking progress against your plan and handling change requests without derailing the project. By the end you will run a weekly status routine, read the warning signs of a slipping project, process changes through a simple approval flow, and re-plan honestly when reality diverges. Plans are predictions; this lesson is what you do when predictions meet reality — which is every week, on every project.

Run status as a weekly routine with three inputs. Task check: go down the task list and mark each task done, in progress (with percent honest — "80% done" for the third week running means it is stuck), or not started. Compare against the schedule: which tasks should be done by now and are not? Focus especially on the critical path from Lesson 3 — a two-day slip on a critical task moves the finish date; the same slip on a task with float does not. Spend check: update the budget table's spent and committed columns and compare to plan. Then produce the one-page status: what got done this week, what is next, what is blocked or at risk, and the project health in one line — "on track," "at risk," or "off track" — with the reason. Share it with the team and stakeholders the same day each week. This routine takes under an hour and is the single highest-value habit in project management: problems found in week three cost a fraction of problems found in week seven.

Learn the warning signs that a project is slipping before the schedule admits it. Tasks "90% done" for weeks — the last 10% is where the hard part hid. Growing silence — owners stop reporting, which usually means bad news they are hoping fixes itself. Shrinking contingency with most of the project remaining — the fuel gauge from Lesson 4 dropping fast early means the estimates were wrong. New tasks appearing that were never in the plan — scope leaking in without the change process. And the busiest warning sign: the team working nights and weekends to "catch up" — heroic effort masks a broken plan for about two weeks, then burns people out. When you spot one, name it in the status honestly: "publicity is two weeks behind because the design took three rounds; this threatens the June 1 flyer deadline." Early honesty gives you options; late honesty gives you a crisis.

Handle change with a process, not with reflexes. Every change request — and they will come — goes through four steps. One, write it down: what exactly is being requested, who asked, when. Two, estimate the impact: what does it cost in money, time, and displaced work? Never approve before estimating — "it's small" is how budgets die. Three, decide: approve (and adjust scope, budget, or schedule explicitly), reject (with the reason tied to goals), or defer (after this project). The decision belongs to the decision-maker named in Lesson 5, not to whoever was asked first. Four, communicate and update: the plan, schedule, and budget change on paper the same day, and everyone affected is told. For the fundraiser: "add a bake sale table" gets estimated (two volunteers, $50 supplies, one week of coordination), decided (approved — funded from contingency, Maya owns it), and the task list and budget update that day. Small process, enormous protection.

When reality diverges seriously — a key vendor cancels, half the timeline is lost — re-plan openly rather than improvising. Re-planning means rebuilding the schedule from today forward with honest estimates, re-scoring risks, and presenting the options: "we can still hit June 14 if we cut the evening program and move $300 from contingency, or we move to June 21 with everything intact — here is my recommendation and why." Bring options with trade-offs, not just problems; recommend one; decide together. Teams forgive bad news delivered with a plan — they do not forgive surprises at the deadline.

Two mistakes to avoid. First, status theater: reporting everything "on track" until it is undeniably off track. Status exists to surface problems early — a yellow "at risk" in week three is professionalism, not failure. Second, the invisible change: letting requests happen informally ("sure, we can add that") without the four steps. Every informal yes is a loan against the schedule that comes due later with interest. Kind, firm, procedural: "great idea — let me estimate it and we'll decide Thursday."

Practice: For your project, (1) create the one-page status template: done / next / blocked-or-at-risk / health line — and fill it with three sample weeks of plausible progress including one task slipping on the critical path; (2) write the status narrative for the slipping week, naming the problem and its impact honestly; (3) take two realistic change requests and run each through the four steps — written request, impact estimate, decision with reasoning, and the exact plan updates you would make; (4) draft a re-plan memo for a serious scenario (lose two weeks): the rebuilt outlook, two options with trade-offs, and your recommendation. Save all of it — the capstone needs your tracking and change approach.
