This final lesson is your capstone: assemble everything from the course into a complete project plan for a sample project, then review it like a professional. By the end you will have a single document containing the goal, scope, task breakdown, schedule, budget, roles, risks, and tracking approach — a genuine project plan — plus a roadmap for what to learn next. This is where eight lessons become one usable skill.

Use the community fundraiser as the sample project if you do not have your own ready: goal — raise $5,000 for playground repairs at a Saturday event on June 14, eight weeks out, with at least 150 attendees. Assemble the plan in order, pulling from your lesson exercises. One, the charter page: the goal statement with its five tests passed, the three supporting goals, and the one-paragraph project description from Lesson 1. Two, the scope statement: deliverables, inclusions, exclusions, and acceptance criteria from Lesson 2 — this is the contract the whole plan hangs on. Three, the work breakdown: your task list of thirty-plus tasks grouped by workstream, each with an owner and a done state, from Lesson 3. Four, the schedule: dependencies mapped, critical path marked, estimates honest, laid backwards from June 14 with buffer before the date. Five, the budget: line items walked from the task list, quotes versus estimates marked, contingency set with its defense, from Lesson 4. Six, the team page: role roster, decision rights, and the communication rhythm from Lesson 5. Seven, the risk register: scored risks with response plans, owners, and triggers from Lesson 6. Eight, the control approach: your status template, the change process steps, and the weekly routine from Lesson 7. Each section should reference the others — the budget's contingency names the risks it covers; the schedule's critical path names appear in the status template's watch list.

Now review the assembled plan like someone whose money is at stake, because that is the skill that separates planners from wishful thinkers. Read it end to end and attack it with five questions. Is the goal measurable and the scope free of weasel words — could two people read the exclusions and agree on what is out? Does every task have exactly one owner, or are there orphans everyone assumes someone else handles? Does the schedule fit the calendar — count the weeks yourself, check the critical path arithmetic, confirm the buffer exists? Does the budget's contingency match the risk register — are the 6-and-9 risks actually covered by the reserve? And what is the single weakest point — the one task, person, or assumption most likely to break — and what is your specific response when it does? Write the answers down; every weakness you find now is a crisis you prevented. A plan that survives this review is a plan you can trust.

Close the project properly in your plan too — Lesson 1's most-skipped phase. Write the closing checklist now, while you are clear-headed: confirm each deliverable against its acceptance criteria, settle every payment and collect receipts, thank the team specifically (name contributions, not generic praise), publish the financial report to stakeholders, and hold a thirty-minute lessons-learned session with three questions — what worked, what did not, what we will do differently. Write the answers into a one-page note filed where the next project can find it. This is how a one-time project becomes lasting capability: the next fundraiser starts from your note instead of from zero.

Where to go next depends on what engaged you. If the people side pulled you — roles, communication, hard conversations — study leadership and facilitation: running workshops, giving feedback, resolving conflict. If the analytical side pulled you — scheduling, budgeting, risk scoring — go deeper into estimation techniques, critical-path software tools, and quantitative risk analysis. If you want credentials, formal certifications exist in project management — research them only after you have run real projects, because the courses assume experience and the exams test judgment more than memory. Whatever direction you take, the foundation does not change: clarify the goal, bound the scope, break down the work, schedule honestly, budget simply, communicate constantly, plan for risk, track weekly, and close deliberately.

Practice: Assemble your capstone document — all eight sections above, in order, for your sample project, each section at least a solid paragraph or its full artifact. Then perform the five-question review in writing, fix what you find, and add the closing checklist with the lessons-learned questions. When the document reads as one coherent plan — goal through closing, every section consistent with the others — you have finished the course. Keep it: it is both your proof of skill and a template for every project you will ever run.
