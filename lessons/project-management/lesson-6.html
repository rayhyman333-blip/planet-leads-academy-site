This lesson teaches risk management: finding what could go wrong before it does and preparing responses while they are cheap. By the end you will run a risk brainstorm, score risks by likelihood and impact, write response plans for the serious ones, and keep a risk register alive through the project. Beginners treat risk management as worrying; professionals treat it as buying insurance with planning instead of money — a few hours of thought that prevent the crises that destroy schedules and budgets.

Identify risks with a structured brainstorm, not anxious rumination. Gather the team (or think it through solo) and walk the project asking "what could go wrong here?" at each workstream, plus four universal prompts: people (what if a key person is unavailable?), suppliers (what if a vendor fails?), weather and environment (for anything outdoors or location-dependent), and dependencies (what are we waiting on that we do not control?). For the fundraiser: rain on the Saturday, the venue double-booking, half the volunteers not showing, the sound system failing, food vendors canceling, donation processing going down. Write each risk as a specific event with a cause — "rain forces cancellation of outdoor activities" beats "bad weather" — because specific risks get specific responses and vague ones get shrugged at. Aim for ten to twenty; fewer means you are not looking hard enough, and the exercise takes one focused hour.

Score each risk on two simple scales from 1 to 3: likelihood (1 = unlikely, 2 = possible, 3 = likely) and impact (1 = minor inconvenience, 2 = serious disruption, 3 = threatens the goal). Multiply them for a score from 1 to 9. This is not precise science — it is a sorting tool. Risks scoring 6 or 9 demand response plans; 3 or 4 get watched; 1 or 2 get noted and ignored. Rain on an outdoor June fundraiser might be likelihood 2, impact 3 (outdoor activities are core) for a 6 — plan needed. The treasurer catching a cold is likelihood 2, impact 1 — noted, not planned. Score quickly and move on; the value is in the ranking, not the arithmetic.

Write responses for the high scorers using four strategies. Avoid: eliminate the risk — book an indoor backup venue and the rain risk to the location disappears. Reduce: lower likelihood or impact — confirm volunteers twice and recruit 20% extras to reduce the no-show impact; rent the sound system from a vendor with on-call support. Transfer: shift the consequence — require food vendors to confirm in writing with a cancellation fee clause. Accept: for risks too small or too expensive to address — note them and move on, like brief passing clouds. Each response names an owner and a trigger: "if rain is forecast 48 hours out, Maya activates the indoor layout." Responses without owners are wishes; triggers without dates get debated while the rain falls.

Keep the risk register alive: a simple table of risk, score, response, owner, and status, reviewed for five minutes at each weekly check-in. Risks evolve — the venue contract signed means that risk retires; a vendor sounding flaky means a risk's likelihood rises from 1 to 3. Add new risks as they appear; the brainstorm was the start, not the whole. And connect the register to your contingency: the budget reserve from Lesson 4 exists precisely for the risks you accepted or could not fully reduce — "we hold $800 contingency against the vendor-cancellation and weather risks" is a sentence that shows the plan is coherent.

Two mistakes to avoid. First, the risk register as a write-once document: risks identified in week one and never reviewed are theater. The five-minute weekly review is the entire difference. Second, planning responses for everything: writing elaborate plans for 1-point risks burns the team's planning energy on trivia. Score honestly, plan the 6s and 9s well, watch the 3s and 4s, and let the 1s and 2s go — risk management is about focus, not completeness.

Practice: For your project, (1) brainstorm at least twelve specific risks using the workstream walk plus the four universal prompts — write each as a specific event with a cause; (2) score each 1–3 on likelihood and impact, compute the score, and sort; (3) write response plans for every risk scoring 6+ using the four strategies, each with a named owner and a trigger; (4) build the risk register table and write the five-minute review script you will use at check-ins ("any scores changed? any new risks? any responses to activate?"); (5) write one sentence linking your register to your contingency reserve. Save the register — the capstone and your status reports will reference it.
