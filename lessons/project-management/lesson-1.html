This lesson defines project management in plain terms and walks you through the five phases every project moves through. By the end you will be able to tell a project apart from ongoing work, name the five phases and what happens in each, and explain what a project manager actually does all day. This is the map of the territory — every later lesson is a deep dive into one region of it.

A project is a temporary effort to create something specific: organizing a fundraiser, moving apartments, launching a website, renovating a kitchen. Three traits define it. It is temporary — it has a beginning and an end, unlike ongoing operations such as "running the shop day to day." It creates something unique — this fundraiser, not fundraisers in general. And it has constraints — limited time, money, and people. Project management is the discipline of delivering that unique result inside those constraints: deciding what will be done, by whom, by when, and for how much, then keeping reality matched to the plan. Notice the definition says nothing about authority or job titles — a volunteer organizing a charity run is doing project management, whether or not anyone calls them a project manager.

Every project, from a birthday party to a building, moves through five phases. Starting: someone decides the project is worth doing — you clarify why it matters, who wants it, and roughly what success looks like. Our running example: the neighborhood association wants to raise $5,000 for playground repairs with a Saturday fundraiser in eight weeks. Planning: you define exactly what is included, break the work into tasks, schedule them, budget the costs, and decide how the team communicates. This phase is where projects are won or lost — an hour of planning here saves many hours of chaos later. Doing: the team executes the tasks — booking the venue, printing flyers, recruiting volunteers. Monitoring: running alongside the doing, you track progress against the plan, spot problems early, and adjust. Closing: you finish the work, confirm everything is delivered, thank the team, and — the step everyone skips — write down what you learned for next time.

The project manager's real job, stripped of mystique, is five ongoing duties. Clarify: make sure everyone understands the goal and their part — most project failures are communication failures wearing other disguises. Plan: build and maintain the schedule, budget, and task list. Track: compare actual progress to the plan weekly and surface gaps early, when they are cheap to fix. Decide: when reality diverges from the plan, choose the response — adjust scope, add time, spend contingency — and communicate the change. Protect: shield the team from distractions and scope creep so they can do the work. None of this requires a title. If you are the person making sure the fundraiser actually happens, you are the project manager, and this course is your toolkit.

Two beginner mistakes start here. First, skipping the starting phase: jumping into tasks before agreeing on why the project exists and what success means. The fundraiser team that never agrees whether the goal is "raise $5,000" or "have a fun community day" will argue about every decision for eight weeks. Write the goal down in one sentence before anything else — Lesson 2 shows you how. Second, treating the phases as a strict one-way sequence: real projects loop back. Planning reveals the budget cannot work, so you return to starting and shrink the goal; doing reveals a task was underestimated, so you re-plan the schedule. The phases are a map, not a prison — expect to revisit them, and build that expectation into how you talk about the project.

Practice: (1) Name your running real-life project (or use the fundraiser) and write one sentence proving it is a project: temporary, unique, constrained. (2) Write one paragraph describing what each of the five phases would contain for your project — what happens in starting, planning, doing, monitoring, and closing. (3) List three duties from the project manager's five that you have already done informally in your life (organizing anything counts) and one sentence each on what happened. Keep this document — it becomes the cover page of your capstone plan.
