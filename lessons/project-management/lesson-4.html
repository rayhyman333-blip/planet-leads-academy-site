This lesson teaches project budgeting: listing every cost, adding sensible contingency, and tracking spending so there are no surprises. By the end you will build a line-item budget, choose a contingency reserve you can defend, and run a simple spend-tracking routine. Budgets fail for boring reasons — forgotten categories, optimistic guesses, and nobody watching — and this lesson's practices prevent all three.

Build the budget as a line-item list, one row per cost, grouped by workstream. For the fundraiser: venue deposit and rental, food vendor fees, flyer design and printing, children's activity supplies, sound equipment rental, donation-processing fees, volunteer refreshments, contingency. The technique that catches forgotten costs is walking the task list from Lesson 3 line by line and asking "does this cost money?" for each — tasks like "distribute flyers" hide printing costs; "children's activities" hides supply costs. Separate one-time costs from per-unit costs: the venue is one price, but food supplies scale with attendees — write per-unit costs as formulas ("$2 per attendee × 150") so the budget updates when the headcount changes. Get real numbers wherever money is significant: one phone call for a quote beats three guesses, and vendors expect it. For small items, honest estimates are fine — mark them as estimates so everyone knows which numbers are firm.

Contingency is not pessimism; it is arithmetic about uncertainty. Set aside a reserve — typically ten to twenty percent of the budget for familiar projects, more for unfamiliar ones — owned by the project manager, not distributed to task owners. The rule: contingency covers identified risks and estimating error, not scope additions (new scope needs new budget through the change process). When a risk from your Lesson 6 register materializes — the sound equipment costs more than quoted — you spend contingency and log it. Report contingency remaining at every status check, like a fuel gauge: "we've used $200 of $800 contingency with five weeks left" tells everyone the project's financial health in one line. A budget with no contingency is a promise that nothing will deviate from the estimate — a promise reality never keeps.

Track spending with a routine, not willpower. Keep one simple table: budgeted, spent, committed (ordered but not yet paid), and remaining per line item, updated weekly. Committed is the column beginners forget — the $400 sound system you ordered but have not paid for is already gone from your budget; tracking only paid invoices makes you feel richer than you are until the bills land together. The weekly review asks three questions: which lines are over pace, what commitments are coming due, and is contingency still adequate for the remaining risks? When a line threatens to overrun, you have the same three levers as scheduling: reduce scope in that area, find a cheaper approach, or move money from an under-spending line — decided openly, not by quietly hoping. For the fundraiser, the treasurer owns this table and reports the one-line health summary at each team check-in.

Two mistakes to avoid. First, the "we'll figure it out" budget: starting spending with costs still listed as "to be decided." Every undecided cost is a surprise waiting to happen — get the quote or set an explicit estimate with the uncertainty noted. Second, confusing the budget with the spending: a budget nobody reviews is decoration. The weekly fifteen-minute review is the entire difference between budgets that work and budgets that do not — put it on the calendar as a recurring appointment in week one.

Practice: For your project, (1) build the full line-item budget by walking your task list and asking "does this cost money?" for each task — include per-unit formulas where costs scale; (2) mark each line as quoted or estimated; (3) set your contingency percentage and write two sentences defending it based on how familiar this project is; (4) create the tracking table with budgeted/spent/committed/remaining columns and enter three sample weeks of plausible spending, including one line going over pace — then write the decision you would make about it using the three levers. Save the budget; the capstone assembles it with everything else.
