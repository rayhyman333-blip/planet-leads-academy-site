This lesson teaches you to turn a scope statement into a trustworthy schedule. By the end you will break work into small tasks, sequence them by dependency, estimate durations honestly, and build a schedule that shows what happens when — including which tasks truly control the finish date. Big projects overwhelm because they are viewed whole; this lesson is the technique for viewing them as manageable pieces.

Break the work down until no task is bigger than a few days of effort and each has a clear done state. Take "publicity" from the fundraiser scope: it decomposes into "design flyer," "print 200 flyers," "distribute to 20 shops," "write three social posts," "publish posts on schedule." Each is assignable to one person, each has an obvious finish line. The test of a good breakdown: every task names a single owner and a verifiable result — "flyers in 20 shops, photo proof" beats "handle publicity." Keep decomposing any task you cannot picture doing: vagueness at this stage becomes delay later. A practical rule: if a task would take one person more than a week, split it; if describing it takes longer than doing it, you have split too far. For the fundraiser, expect thirty to fifty tasks — that is normal and healthy.

Sequence the tasks by dependency: what must finish before what can start? You cannot distribute flyers before they are printed; you cannot print before the design is approved; the venue must be booked before you advertise the location. Lay these out as a simple chain per workstream — publicity chain, venue chain, volunteer chain — then look across chains for the critical path: the longest dependency chain, which sets the project's minimum duration. If the venue chain takes five weeks and everything else takes three, the project takes at least five weeks, and any delay on the venue chain delays everything. Mark critical-path tasks clearly — these get your daily attention, your best people, and your contingency. Non-critical tasks have float: they can slip a little without moving the finish date, which tells you where flexibility lives when problems hit.

Estimate durations honestly, because every schedule is built on guesses and most guesses are optimistic. Three techniques beat gut feel. Ask the doer: the volunteer who has printed flyers before knows it takes three days including the print shop's turnaround — the person doing the work estimates better than the manager. Add explicitly for uncertainty: unfamiliar tasks get a wider estimate ("three to five days") and you schedule the upper end. And track reality: compare estimates to actuals as the project runs, and use what you learn to re-estimate the remaining work — estimation is a skill that improves with feedback. Then build the schedule backwards from the deadline: place the final tasks at the end date and work backwards through dependencies, which reveals immediately whether the plan fits the time available. If it does not fit, you have three honest levers — shrink scope, add people to parallel tasks, or move the date — and Lesson 7's change process governs which you pull. What you must never do is keep the date and the scope and just hope: hope is not a scheduling technique.

Two mistakes dominate scheduling. First, the student-syndrome schedule: tasks planned back-to-back with zero buffer, so the first delay cascades through everything. Protect the critical path with explicit buffer — a few unassigned days before the deadline that get consumed by reality. Second, scheduling people at 100%: nobody does project tasks eight hours a day alongside their life — volunteers have jobs, employees have meetings. Plan people at half to two-thirds availability for project work, and the schedule will survive contact with reality.

Practice: For your project, (1) break the scope into tasks until each is a few days at most with a single owner and a verifiable done state — aim for at least twenty-five tasks; (2) group them into three to five workstreams and map the dependencies within each; (3) identify your critical path and mark which tasks have float; (4) estimate each task using the "ask the doer" principle (write who you would ask and what you would ask them); (5) lay the schedule backwards from your deadline and note honestly whether it fits — if not, write which of the three levers you would pull and why. Save the task list; it becomes your tracking baseline in Lesson 7.
