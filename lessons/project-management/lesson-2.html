This lesson teaches scope: writing down exactly what your project includes — and excludes — plus goals you can actually measure. By the end you will write a scope statement with inclusions, exclusions, and deliverables, and set goals that pass a simple five-part test. Scope is where projects are saved or doomed: most overruns, arguments, and disappointments trace back to people holding different unspoken assumptions about what "the project" covers.

Start with the goal, because scope serves the goal. A useful goal passes five tests: Specific (what exactly?), Measurable (how will we know?), Achievable (can this team do it?), Relevant (does it serve the real purpose?), Time-bound (by when?). "Raise money for the playground" fails four of the five. "Raise $5,000 in donations for playground repairs by the end of the fundraiser on June 14" passes all five — a stranger could verify whether it happened. Write one primary goal plus two or three supporting goals: "at least 150 attendees," "every volunteer shift filled," "event ends by 4 PM with the park clean." Supporting goals prevent the classic trap of hitting the money target by burning out every volunteer — they define what success looks like in full, not just in dollars.

The scope statement turns the goal into boundaries. It has three parts. Deliverables: the tangible things the project produces — "a Saturday fundraiser event, a donation collection process, a financial report." Inclusions: the work you will do — "venue booking, food vendors (three), children's activities, publicity, volunteer coordination, donation handling." Exclusions: what you will NOT do — stated explicitly, because unspoken exclusions are where arguments breed: "no evening entertainment program, no alcohol sales, no playground construction work (funds only)." Acceptance criteria: how you will know each deliverable is done — "venue booked means signed contract and deposit paid; publicity done means flyers in 20 shops plus three social posts." Write exclusions with the same care as inclusions — saying "we are not doing X" in week one prevents the week-six argument about why X is not happening.

Scope creep — the slow growth of "just one more thing" — is the force that destroys plans, and defending scope is a core project manager duty. It never arrives as a villain; it arrives as reasonable requests: "while we're at it, could we also..." Each addition seems small, but five small additions consume the contingency and then the schedule. Your defenses: the written scope statement (point to it — "that's outside what we agreed; here's what adding it costs"), a change process (Lesson 7 teaches it — every addition gets estimated before approval, never after), and the habit of asking "what should we remove to make room for this?" That last question is magic: it forces the requester to confront that additions are trades, not free. Protecting scope is not being difficult — it is keeping the promise the team made about what would be delivered.

Two mistakes to avoid. First, the invisible exclusion: assuming everyone knows something is out of scope without writing it down. You know the fundraiser is not building the playground; does the enthusiastic volunteer who offered to "coordinate construction" know? Write it. Second, goals without numbers: "make it a great event" cannot be planned for or verified. If a goal matters, find its number — attendees, dollars, deadline, satisfaction rating. Numbers turn wishes into targets the team can aim at and you can track.

Practice: For your project, write (1) one primary goal and two supporting goals, each passing all five tests — rewrite until they do; (2) a scope statement with at least five deliverables, eight inclusions, five explicit exclusions, and acceptance criteria for each deliverable; (3) three realistic scope-creep requests someone might make ("could we also...") and your written response to each using the scope statement and the "what should we remove?" question. Read the scope statement aloud to an imaginary team member — if any sentence could mean two things, rewrite it until it cannot.
