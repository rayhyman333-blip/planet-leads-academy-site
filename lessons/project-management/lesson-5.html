This lesson teaches the people side of project management: clarifying who does what and communicating so the team stays aligned without drowning in meetings. By the end you will assign clear roles, set up a communication rhythm, run meetings that respect everyone's time, and handle the two most common people problems. Projects are done by people, and people problems — confusion about ownership, surprises from silence, meetings that waste hours — sink more projects than technical difficulty ever does.

Clarify roles before work starts, because "I thought you were doing that" is the most expensive sentence in project management. For each major deliverable, name exactly one owner — the person accountable for it happening — even when several people contribute. The fundraiser's publicity has one owner; the flyer designer and the distributors contribute to that owner. Write the roster down where everyone can see it: name, role, what they own, how to reach them. Also name the decision-maker for the project (often you) and what decisions team members can make alone — volunteers should not need a meeting to choose flyer colors, but they should not sign contracts either. The test: pick any task and ask "who owns this?" — if the answer is a name within three seconds, your roles are clear; if it is a discussion, they are not.

Set up a communication rhythm with three parts. A weekly check-in (thirty minutes, same time): each workstream owner reports progress, next steps, and blockers — status, not discussion; discussions that need only two people happen after. A shared written status (one page or one message thread): what got done, what is next, what is blocked, updated before the meeting so the meeting itself stays short. And agreed response norms: how quickly messages get answered, which channel is for urgent versus routine, and that blockers get raised immediately rather than saved for the meeting — a blocker reported Tuesday gets fixed Tuesday; one saved for Friday's meeting costs three days. Match the weight to the project: a volunteer fundraiser needs this light structure, not corporate ceremony. The principle is constant though: regular, brief, written-first communication prevents the surprise, and surprises are what destroy trust.

Run meetings people do not hate with four rules. An agenda sent in advance with the decisions needed — no agenda, no meeting. Start and end on time, every time; ending late steals from the next commitment and teaches people your meetings are optional. Decisions get written down with an owner and a date before anyone leaves the room — "we decided X, Maya owns it, done by Friday" — because unwritten decisions evaporate by morning. And anyone who does not need to be there does not get invited; a five-person meeting that needs two people wastes three people's hour. For status meetings specifically, go around the owners with three prompts — done, next, blocked — and park any problem-solving longer than two minutes into a separate huddle with only the involved people.

Two people problems will find you; handle them early and directly. The disengaged contributor: someone who agreed to a role and went quiet. Talk to them privately within a week of noticing — not in the group meeting. Ask what changed; often the task was unclear or life intervened, and re-clarifying or reassigning solves it. What does not work is hoping they re-engage or complaining about them to others. The disagreeing stakeholder: someone with influence who wants a different direction. Hear them out fully first — sometimes they see a real problem — then decide openly against the written scope and goals: "I understand the concern; against our goal of $5,000 by June 14, I recommend we stay the course because..." People accept decisions they disagree with when the reasoning is transparent and the goals are shared; they revolt against unexplained ones.

Two mistakes to avoid. First, managing by broadcast: sending all information to everyone and assuming alignment. Targeted communication — the right update to the right people — beats volume; a volunteer does not need the budget spreadsheet, and the treasurer does not need the flyer drafts. Second, avoiding the hard conversation: the unclear role, the missed commitment, the brewing disagreement. Every hard conversation gets harder with waiting — address it in days, not weeks, privately and specifically, focused on the work rather than the person.

Practice: For your project, (1) write the role roster: every major deliverable with exactly one named owner, plus the decision-maker and what team members may decide alone; (2) design your communication rhythm — weekly check-in time and format, the written status template, and your response norms; (3) draft an agenda for your first team meeting with the decisions it must produce; (4) script two conversations: addressing a contributor who has gone quiet (private, specific, focused on the work) and responding to a stakeholder who wants to add out-of-scope work (heard fully, decided against the written goals). Rehearse both out loud — the words need to exist before the moment does.
