This lesson studies the Quran's major passages and teaches methods for close reading them. By the end you will be able to describe the content and structure of the text's most widely read passages, read a passage at several levels, and explain how context, language, and structure shape meaning. The passages in this lesson are the ones a student will meet most often: in daily prayer, in study, and in scholarly discussion. Knowing them well gives you anchor points for the whole text.

Al-Fatihah, The Opening, is the first surah and the most recited passage in the text. It has seven short verses. The first verse names God as the Most Merciful and the Most Compassionate. The second praises God as Lord of the worlds. The third repeats the names of mercy. The fourth names God as Master of the Day of Judgment. The fifth turns to direct address: You alone we worship, and You alone we ask for help. The sixth asks for guidance to the straight path. The seventh describes that path as the path of those favored, not of those who earned anger or went astray. Study the surah's movement: it begins with description, moves to praise, then to direct address and request. That movement from description to request is a pattern worth noticing in other passages. The surah is recited in every unit of prayer, which is why it is often the first passage a student memorizes.

Al-Ikhlas, Sincerity, is the 112th surah, four verses that state the oneness of God directly: Say, He is God, One; God, the Eternal; He neither begets nor is begotten; and there is nothing comparable to Him. Its brevity is part of its importance: in four lines it gathers the theme of tawhid from Lesson 5 into a single compact statement. The two short surahs that follow it, Al-Falaq, The Daybreak, and An-Nas, The People, are prayers of protection: the first seeks refuge from the evil of created things, darkness, and envy, and the second seeks refuge from the whisperer who tempts the hearts of people. Together the three short surahs at the end of the text are among the most memorized passages, and studying them together shows how the text pairs a statement of belief with prayers that apply it to daily life.

Ayat al-Kursi, the Verse of the Throne, is verse 255 of the second surah, Al-Baqarah, and it is one of the most widely read single verses. It describes God as the Living, the Sustainer, who neither sleeps nor slumbers, to whom belongs all that is in the heavens and the earth, whose knowledge encompasses all things, and whose throne extends over the heavens and the earth. The verse gathers the themes of oneness, knowledge, and power into one long sentence, and its structure is worth studying: it builds through a series of clauses, each adding an attribute, and closes with the statement that God is the Most High, the Most Great. The opening of Al-Baqarah, its first five verses, is another major passage: it describes the book as guidance for those who are God-conscious, who believe in the unseen, establish prayer, and give from what they have been provided. Reading these openings together shows how the longest surah introduces itself.

Close reading begins with the plain meaning: what the words say in their grammar, vocabulary, and immediate context. Paraphrase each verse in your own words before consulting any commentary; if you cannot paraphrase it, you have not yet understood its plain sense. Then use word study: choose a word that repeats in a passage, find its root from Lesson 3, and list other words from that root. The word ayah, for example, means both verse and sign, so a passage using ayah is connecting the text with the natural world. Then study context: the occasions of revelation from Lesson 6, the Meccan or Medinan setting from Lesson 4, and the surah's overall movement. A verse about a specific historical situation reads differently when its situation is known, and the classical commentators insist that interpretation requires knowledge of context, language, and the broader text.

Structure and comparison are the final methods. Sketch the structure of a passage before interpreting it: list its sections in order, note repetitions and contrasts, and identify the turning point. The Fatiha's movement from description to request is a structural observation, not an interpretation, and it is the kind of observation that grounds good interpretation. Compare translations: place two or three renderings of the same passage side by side and note where they differ. Differences reveal ambiguities in the Arabic or different translator philosophies, and each difference is a question worth investigating. Compare passages within the text: the prophet stories are retold in several surahs, and asking why the retellings differ is one of the oldest and most productive questions in the field. And compare commentaries: when two scholars read one verse differently, reconstruct each one's reasoning.

Two mistakes to avoid. First, quoting verses without context to win arguments, with others or with yourself. A verse about a specific historical conflict is not a general rule, and a verse about mercy is not a reason to ignore the text's commands. The classical scholars insisted that interpretation requires knowledge of context, language, and the broader text; humility before the text is part of respect for it. Second, treating one translation as the text: every translation is already an interpretation, making hundreds of small decisions you cannot see. The student who reads only one translation mistakes the translator's choices for the text itself. Compare at least two, and remember that the Arabic, which you can now approach through letters, roots, and vocabulary, is the anchor.

Practice: (1) Choose one major passage from this lesson, Al-Fatihah, Al-Ikhlas, or Ayat al-Kursi, and paraphrase each verse in your own words in your journal, plain-meaning work with no commentaries yet. (2) Choose one repeated word in the passage, trace its root, list three other words from that root, and write a paragraph on what the root contributes to the passage. (3) Sketch the passage's structure in a simple outline, marking repetitions, contrasts, and the turning point. (4) Compare two translations of the same passage, list every difference you find, and write one question about each significant difference.
