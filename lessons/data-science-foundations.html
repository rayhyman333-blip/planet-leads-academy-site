Turn raw data into clear answers, one careful step at a time.

Lesson 1: What Data Science Is

This lesson defines data science in plain terms and shows you how a data project moves from a vague worry to a clear answer. By the end you will be able to explain what data science is, describe the five steps of a data project, tell the four types of data apart on sight, and write a good question that data can actually answer. Everything in this course, cleaning, summarizing, charting, predicting, presenting, sits on top of these foundations, so take the time to get comfortable here.

Data science is the practice of turning raw records into answers that help someone make a decision. Raw records are facts captured in the course of activity: each harvest weighed, each book borrowed, each order placed. A data scientist takes those records and does five things. First, they clarify the question: "How much should we plant next month?" is answerable, while "Are we doing well?" is too vague to work with. Second, they gather the relevant data and check that it is trustworthy. Third, they clean it, because real data is messy. Fourth, they analyze it: count, group, compare, and summarize to find patterns. Fifth, they present the finding clearly, often with a chart, plus a recommendation. Notice what is not on the list: guessing, cherry-picking one favorable number, or dressing up a weak finding with a fancy chart. Credibility in data work comes from honest handling of the data, and Lessons 3 through 5 teach the techniques that keep you honest.

Here is a small sample dataset you will see referenced through the course: a community garden's weekly harvest log. Each row is one week:

Week ending | Kilograms harvested | Beds in use | Volunteers | Weather | Notes 2026-10-03 | 42 | 8 | 6 | sunny | "Extra tomatoes" 2026-10-10 | 31 | 8 | 4 | rainy | "Two beds flooded" 2026-10-17 | 38 | 9 | 5 | cloudy | "" 2026-10-24 | 47 | 9 | 7 | sunny | "New bed planted" 2026-10-31 | 28 | 9 | 3 | rainy | "Several volunteers away"

Kilograms harvested and volunteers are numbers. Beds in use is also a number, a count. Weather is a category. The week-ending date is date data. The notes column is free text. Already you can see real questions this answers: which weather brings bigger harvests, what a typical week looks like, whether volunteer numbers explain the slow weeks. A data scientist would start by asking the garden coordinator which decision the answer feeds, for example how many beds to plant next season, and only then compute. That question-first habit is the single most valuable thing this lesson teaches.

Data comes in four types, and the type decides what you are allowed to do with it. Numerical data is numbers where arithmetic makes sense: kilograms, temperatures, ages, counts. You can add and average these. Categorical data is labels that group things: weather, crop names, bed numbers used as labels, volunteer roles. You can count how many fall in each category, but averaging "sunny" and "rainy" is meaningless. Text data is free-form writing: notes, comments, descriptions. You can search it and count words, but it needs processing before it behaves like the other types. Date and time data marks when things happened: harvest dates, loan dates. You can sort by it, group by month or week, and measure gaps between events. A single dataset usually mixes all four: a garden record has a date, a weather label, a harvest weight, and a note.

Two beginner mistakes start in this lesson. First, treating categories like numbers: assigning 1 to sunny and 2 to rainy and then averaging the weather to 1.4 is nonsense, even though a spreadsheet will happily compute it. Always ask: does arithmetic mean anything here? If not, count instead of averaging. Second, jumping to analysis before defining the question. It is tempting to open a dataset and start calculating everything, but analysis without a question produces trivia. Write the question in one sentence first, "Do rainy weeks harvest less?" and let it choose which numbers you compute. A good question names the thing you want to compare and the decision it will inform. "Do rainy weeks harvest less, so we know whether to add covered beds?" is a question with a decision attached, and that is the standard to aim for.

Practice: Invent a small dataset of your own with ten rows about something you can observe this week: your daily cups of water, minutes spent on chores, pages read, or items in your shopping cart. Include at least one numerical column, one categorical column, and a date column. Then write three one-sentence questions your dataset could answer and three it could not: water data cannot tell you about sleep, for example. Finally, label each column in the garden dataset above with its data type, and explain in one sentence why "beds in use" is numerical while "weather" is categorical, even though both can be written as short values in a table.

Lesson 2: Working with Data Tables

This lesson teaches you to work confidently with data tables: the rows and columns that hold almost all data you will meet. By the end you will read a table correctly, sort and filter it, add calculated columns, and use the core spreadsheet functions for totals, averages, and counts. Tables are the basic unit of data work, and fluency here makes every later lesson faster.

Start with the shape of a table. A table has one header row with short, clear column names, such as week ending, kilograms, beds, volunteers, and weather, and one row per record below it. Each row is one observation: one week, one order, one person. Each column is one measurement or label, the same kind of thing all the way down. A cell is where one row meets one column: a single value. Keep your table tidy: no blank rows in the middle, no merged cells, no titles or notes sitting inside the data area, and one kind of value per column. If a column mixes numbers and text, such as "42" in some cells and "about forty" in others, formulas will fail or silently skip cells. Put titles and notes above the table or on a separate sheet, and keep the data area clean. This discipline feels fussy until the day a careless sort scrambles your data, and sorting is the first skill.

Sorting rearranges rows so a column runs in order. Sort the garden data by kilograms harvested, largest to smallest, and the best and worst weeks jump out at once. The golden rule of sorting is to select all columns before sorting, or to use your program's sort-with-headers feature, so each row stays intact. Sorting one column alone while the others stay put silently destroys your data: the harvest numbers move but the dates do not, and every row becomes a lie. This is the most damaging beginner error in spreadsheet work, and the habit of selecting the whole table first prevents it. Sorting is also a quick way to inspect data: sort a column from smallest to largest and scan the extremes for odd values, such as a negative harvest or a date in the wrong year.

Filtering lets you look at a slice without deleting anything. Turn on filters for your header row, then use the dropdown on the weather column to show only rainy weeks: the spreadsheet hides the other rows and functions such as SUBTOTAL adjust to show only the visible rows. Filtering answers questions like "what is our average harvest on sunny weeks?" in seconds: filter to sunny, then average the kilograms column. A filter never changes the underlying data, it only changes the view, so it is always safe to experiment. Combine filters across columns to narrow further: rainy weeks with fewer than five volunteers. When you are done, clear the filter to bring everything back. Filtering is also how you spot problems: filter a column for blanks and you see at once how many values are missing, a question Lesson 3 takes up in full.

Calculated columns turn raw columns into answers. A formula in a spreadsheet starts with an equals sign and can reference cells: =B2*2 computes double the value in B2, and =B2/C2 divides one column by another. Add a column called kilograms per bed, with the formula =B2/C2 copied down, and you can compare weeks fairly even when the number of beds changed. The functions you will use constantly are SUM for a total, AVERAGE for a mean, COUNT for how many numbers there are, COUNTA for how many non-empty cells there are, and MIN and MAX for the smallest and largest values. So =AVERAGE(B2:B6) gives the average weekly harvest, and =MAX(B2:B6) finds the best week. IF makes decisions: =IF(B2>40,"Big week","Regular week") labels each week, and you can build on that with a second IF for more bands. When you copy a formula down a column, references shift relatively, B2 becomes B3, which is usually what you want. Put a dollar sign before the letter or number to lock a reference, like $B$2, when it must not move, for example a target value stored in one cell that every

row's formula uses.

Two more mistakes to engrave in memory. First, numbers stored as text: values that look like numbers but were pasted as text will not sort or average correctly, and your totals will silently undercount. The symptom is SUM returning zero or AVERAGE ignoring cells; the fix is to convert the column to numbers, which most programs offer as a convert-to-number command or by multiplying by one in a helper column. Second, dividing by zero or by an empty cell: a week with zero beds in use cannot give kilograms per bed, and the formula will show an error. That error is honest feedback, not a bug to hide: it tells you the row needs attention, and Lesson 3 teaches you how to handle it.

Practice: Rebuild the five-week garden dataset from Lesson 1 in a spreadsheet program. Add a sixth and seventh week of your own invention. Then do these four steps: sort by kilograms harvested, largest first, and confirm whole rows moved together; filter to rainy weeks and compute their average kilograms with AVERAGE; add a column with =IF(B2>40,"Big week","Regular week") and copy it down; add a column for kilograms per bed with =B2/C2 and copy it down. At the bottom, compute total kilograms with SUM, average kilograms with AVERAGE, and the best week with MAX. Save this file: you will extend it in Lessons 3, 4, and 5.

Lesson 3: Cleaning Messy Data

This lesson teaches the least glamorous and most important data skill: cleaning messy data. By the end you will run a repeatable cleaning routine, checking for missing values, fixing inconsistent formats, removing duplicates, and validating your work, on any dataset. Real-world data is never as tidy as textbook examples: entries are misspelled, dates arrive in three formats, half the rows are missing something. People who work with data commonly spend most of a project's time cleaning, because every analysis built on dirty data is wrong in ways that look convincing.

Start with a cleaning mindset: never edit the raw data. Make a copy or work on a duplicate sheet, and keep a short log of what you changed: "removed 2 duplicate rows", "filled 1 missing harvest with the weekly average". This log is your proof that the final numbers are honest, and it lets you or anyone else redo the work. Then inspect before you fix: sort each column, scan for odd values, and use filters to spot blanks. A quick scan of a garden log might reveal "sunny", "Sunny", "SUNNY", and "suny" all meaning the same weather, plus two identical weeks and a volunteer column mixing "6" with "six". Each of these is a different defect with a different fix, and the inspection step is what tells you which defects you actually have, instead of guessing.

Missing values are the most common defect, and the fix depends on why the value is missing. A blank harvest on a week the garden was closed for a holiday is not a zero: it is not applicable, and the honest treatment is to leave it blank so averages skip it, as Lesson 2 noted. A blank in a survey response where someone skipped the question is genuinely unknown. Your options are: leave it blank, which is best when few are missing and the analysis tolerates it; fill it with a sensible substitute like the column's average or median, which is acceptable when you disclose it in your log; or drop the row, which is only right when the row is unusable otherwise. What you must never do is silently treat blanks as zeros without thinking: zeros drag averages down and can flip a conclusion. As a rule, if more than a small share of a column is missing, the problem is in how the data was collected, and you should say so in your findings rather than patch over it.

Inconsistent formats are the next defect. Standardize text with find-and-replace and consistent capitalization: pick "sunny" and convert every variant to it. Standardize dates to one format, year-month-day, which also sorts correctly as text. Standardize numbers: strip units and commas into plain numbers in one column, keeping the original if you like. Remove exact duplicates, most spreadsheet programs have a remove-duplicates command, but check first that the rows are truly identical and not two legitimate identical-looking records: two separate weeks with the same harvest are real, while a pasted-twice import is not. Finally, validate: recompute your row counts before and after, so you removed exactly the duplicates and nothing else; spot-check ten random rows against the raw copy; and re-run your key formulas. If the totals changed in a way you cannot explain from your log, something went wrong, so go back and find it before analyzing further.

Impossible values deserve their own check. A harvest of negative kilograms, a week with 400 volunteers when the garden has never had more than ten, or a date in the wrong year are all signals that something was typed wrong. Do not just delete them: check the raw source first, such as the paper log the numbers came from, and fix the value if the source shows the truth. If the source cannot be checked, remove the row and log it, saying why. The same care applies to outliers, values that are unusual but possible. A 90-kilogram week after a new bed opened might be real, while a 900-kilogram week is almost certainly a typing error, an extra zero. Investigate outliers instead of deleting them on

sight, because a real outlier often carries the most interesting story in the data.

Two mistakes define this lesson. First, cleaning without a log: a month later you will not remember whether you deleted those rows because they were duplicates or because they were inconvenient, and neither will anyone checking your work. Second, fixing data by deleting rows that contradict the story you want. Removing a terrible harvest week because it spoils the trend is not cleaning, it is fabrication. The rule is simple: you may only remove or change values for documented data-quality reasons, a duplicate, an impossible value, an obvious typo, never because you dislike the result. When in doubt, keep the row and note the oddity in your findings.

Practice: Take your garden spreadsheet from Lesson 2 and deliberately mess up a copy: misspell one weather entry, enter one date in a different format, duplicate one row, delete one harvest value, and add a stray space to a weather entry. Then clean the copy using this lesson's routine: inspect, log each fix, standardize, dedupe, and validate, and write a five-line cleaning log. Confirm your totals match the original file afterward. Keep the log format; the checklist in your materials packet is built from exactly this routine, and you will use it again in the final project.

Lesson 4: Describing Data with Summaries

This lesson teaches descriptive summaries: the handful of numbers that describe a dataset in plain terms. By the end you will compute and interpret the mean, median, and mode, three kinds of average, measure spread with range and standard deviation, and know which summary fits which situation. These summaries are the vocabulary of data work: nearly every report, dashboard, and conversation about data uses them, and choosing the wrong one is one of the easiest ways to mislead, even by accident.

Start with the three averages, because each answers a slightly different question. The mean is what most people mean by average: add everything up and divide by the count. The garden's harvests over five weeks, 42, 31, 38, 47, and 28 kilograms, sum to 186, divided by 5 gives a mean of 37.2. The median is the middle value when the numbers are lined up in order: 28, 31, 38, 42, 47, so the median is 38. The mode is the most frequent value: with these five distinct numbers there is no mode, but in a season of weather data the mode might be sunny. The crucial difference is that the mean is pulled by extremes while the median is not. Add one bumper week of 120 kilograms and the mean jumps to about 51 while the median barely moves. That is why people who work with data report the median for things with occasional huge values, such as incomes, house prices, and order sizes, and the mean when values are well-behaved. When someone quotes an average without saying which one, ask; the choice can completely change the story.

Spread tells you how much the numbers vary, and a summary without spread is half a story. "Average 37 kilograms a week" could mean every week harvests 35 to 39, or it could mean alternating 20 and 55: same average, wildly different garden. The range is the simplest measure: the largest value minus the smallest, here 47 minus 28, which is 19. The standard deviation is the more useful one: roughly, the typical distance of a week's harvest from the mean. A small standard deviation means consistent weeks; a large one means volatile weeks. You do not need to compute it by hand: every spreadsheet program has a STDEV function, but you need the intuition. Two gardens can share an average while one is predictable and the other is a roller coaster, and the standard deviation is the number that reveals which is which. Percentiles add one more useful lens: the 90th percentile is the value that 90 percent of weeks fall below, handy for questions like "how big a week should we be ready for?"

Choosing the right summary is a small decision tree. Describing a typical value? Use the median if outliers exist, the mean otherwise, and the mode for categories. Describing consistency? Use range for a quick sense and standard deviation for the real picture. Comparing two groups, sunny versus rainy weeks, this month versus last? Compute the same summary for each group side by side and look at the gap. And always sanity-check summaries against the raw data: if the median says 38 but a glance at the column shows mostly 20s, something is off, perhaps a filter is still on, or text-numbers are being ignored. Summaries compress reality; the raw column is the ground truth you check against. Counts deserve the same care: a count of weeks is simple, but a count of distinct values, such as how many different crops were harvested, answers a different question from a count of rows, so name exactly what you counted.

Two mistakes to avoid. First, quoting the mean for skewed data without mentioning it: saying "average harvest is 51 kilograms" when one bumper week did most of the work misleads whoever plans planting from that number. Report the median alongside it, or at least note the outlier. Second, confusing a difference with a cause, which starts here because summaries invite it: rainy weeks averaging fewer

kilograms does not prove rain causes low harvests. Perhaps rainy weeks in your data were also the weeks few volunteers showed up, remember the week of October 31, with only three volunteers. A difference between groups is a clue that deserves investigation, not a conclusion. Write "rainy weeks averaged fewer kilograms in this data", a true statement, rather than "rain causes low harvests", a claim the data alone cannot support. That careful wording is the mark of honest data work.

Practice: Extend your garden spreadsheet to fourteen weeks, inventing seven more rows and keeping the same columns. Compute the mean, median, and mode of kilograms harvested, plus the range and standard deviation using STDEV. Then split the weeks into sunny versus not sunny and compute the mean harvest for each group. Write three honest sentences summarizing what you found, using "in this data" language, not causal claims. Finally, add one extreme week, a 120-kilogram bumper harvest, and watch what happens to the mean versus the median. Write one sentence explaining which summary you would report to the garden coordinator and why.

Lesson 5: Charts That Tell the Truth

This lesson teaches you to turn numbers into pictures that tell the truth quickly. By the end you will choose the right chart for each question, build clean honest charts in a spreadsheet program, and spot the tricks that make charts misleading, including in other people's work. A good chart lets a busy person grasp in five seconds what a table of numbers takes five minutes to reveal. A bad chart does the opposite, and sometimes it misleads without the reader noticing.

Every chart answers one of a few question types, and the question picks the chart. Comparing categories? Use a bar chart: kilograms harvested per crop, volunteers per week. Showing change over time? Use a line chart: weekly harvest across the season, where the eye naturally follows the trend. Showing parts of a whole? Use a pie chart only for a few slices, two to five, such as the share of harvest by crop, or use a stacked bar when there are more. Showing the relationship between two numbers? Use a scatter plot: each week is a dot with volunteers on one axis and kilograms on the other, revealing whether more volunteers go with bigger harvests. Showing a distribution? Use a histogram: how many weeks fell into each harvest band. When in doubt, a bar chart is the safest default: it is the hardest chart to misread. Match the chart to the question before touching any formatting, because a beautiful wrong chart is still wrong.

Honest charts follow a short set of principles. Start bar charts at zero: a bar's length encodes its value, so cutting the axis at 30 makes a 35-to-40 difference look enormous when it is actually small. Label everything: a chart title stating the finding, axis labels with units, such as "Kilograms harvested", and data labels on key points. Keep it simple: one message per chart, no 3D effects, no decorative images, no rainbow of fifteen colors when two will do. Use color to highlight, not decorate: gray for the background data and one strong color for the point you want noticed. Keep the data visible: a chart should never hide the numbers behind so much styling that the reader cannot check your work. These rules are not about taste; each one prevents a specific misunderstanding, and together they make your charts trustworthy by construction.

Learn to spot misleading charts, because you will meet them in reports, news, and sales pitches. The truncated axis is the classic: bars or lines that start at a non-zero value to exaggerate a tiny change. Always check where the axis begins. The cherry-picked timeframe: a line chart starting right after a dip to show amazing growth. Ask what the longer history looks like. The area trick: using pictures, such as baskets or people icons, sized by height when the eye reads area, doubling the visual exaggeration. If the value doubles, the icon's area quadruples. The missing context: "harvest up by half" with no mention that last month was the worst on record. The dual-axis trap: two lines on different scales engineered to look correlated. Your defense is one habit: before reacting to any chart, read the axes, the labels, and the source note. Ten seconds of skepticism defeats most chart tricks, and it is a habit worth building now.

The workflow for building a chart is: question first, then the right data slice, then the right chart type, then honest formatting, then a one-sentence takeaway written beneath it. For example, take the question "are weekends stronger than weekdays?" and translate it to the garden: "do sunny weeks harvest more than rainy weeks?" Slice the data into average harvest grouped by weather. Chart it as a bar chart of the averages, axis starting at zero, titled "Average weekly harvest by weather". Takeaway: "In this data, sunny weeks averaged the highest harvest and rainy weeks the lowest." Notice the takeaway describes the data without claiming why. Build every chart this way and your visuals will be

trustworthy by construction. Also keep the underlying table near the chart, or available on request, so a reader who doubts the picture can check the numbers it came from.

Two mistakes to avoid. First, charting raw unsorted tables: a line chart of dates in random order produces spaghetti that hides the trend, so sort by date first, always. Second, pie charts with too many slices: a twelve-slice pie is unreadable. If you have many categories, use a bar chart sorted largest to smallest, or group the small ones into "Other". If your reader has to work to decode the chart, the chart has failed. A third, quieter mistake is charting a summary without saying what it is: a bar labeled "average" with no note of whether it is a mean or a median leaves the reader guessing, so label the summary type in the title or in a note beneath the chart.

Practice: In your garden spreadsheet, build three charts from your fourteen-week dataset: a line chart of weekly harvest over time, sorted by date, with labeled axes; a bar chart comparing average kilograms on sunny versus rainy versus cloudy weeks, axis starting at zero; and a pie chart of weeks by weather type, keeping it to the weather categories you have. Under each chart write a one-sentence honest takeaway. Then deliberately make a misleading version of the bar chart by starting the axis just below your smallest value, and compare the two side by side. Notice how different the story feels, and write one sentence about why the honest version is the one you would show the garden coordinator.

Lesson 6: An Introduction to Prediction

This lesson introduces prediction in plain terms: using patterns in past data to make a careful forecast about what might happen next. By the end you will understand what a prediction is and is not, build a simple forecast from a trend, test it by holding back recent data, and know the limits that keep predictions honest. Prediction is the part of data science that sounds most magical, so this lesson is deliberately careful: a forecast is a reasoned guess with stated assumptions, not a promise.

A prediction answers a question like "how much will we harvest next week?" or "how many books will be borrowed next month?" It works by finding a pattern in past data and extending it forward. The simplest pattern is a trend: harvests rising by a few kilograms each week suggest next week will be a little higher again. A second pattern is a group average: sunny weeks in your data averaged 44 kilograms, so a sunny week ahead might harvest around that amount. A third pattern is a relationship between two columns: weeks with more volunteers tended to harvest more, so knowing next week's volunteer count gives a clue about its harvest. None of these patterns is a law of nature. They are summaries of what happened before, and they only help if the future resembles the past. That condition is the heart of honest prediction.

Build a simple trend forecast by hand so you can see every step. Take the garden's last six weekly harvests in order: 28, 31, 38, 35, 42, 47 kilograms. The numbers rise overall, with a small dip in the middle. A plain way to forecast is to average the last three weeks, 35, 42, and 47, which gives about 41 kilograms, and to use that as the forecast for next week. A second way is to look at the change from week to week: the jumps are 3, 7, minus 3, 7, and 5, averaging about 4 kilograms a week, so adding 4 to the latest week gives about 51. Those two reasonable methods give different answers, 41 and 51, and that disagreement is informative: it tells you the forecast is uncertain and that the method matters. Report a forecast as a range, "around 41 to 51 kilograms", rather than a single false-precision number. A range admits what you do not know, and a reader who plans planting from it can plan for both ends.

Testing a prediction is what separates guessing from data science. The method is simple and powerful: hold back the most recent data, build your forecast from the earlier data only, and then compare the forecast with the weeks you held back. Suppose you forecast week 14 from weeks 1 through 13 and predicted 40 kilograms, but the actual harvest was 33. The error is 7 kilograms, and the direction matters: you overestimated. Repeat this for several weeks, one at a time, and average the errors to get a sense of how far off your method usually is. If the average error is small compared with the harvests themselves, the method is useful. If the errors are large, or if the method keeps overestimating in rainy weeks and underestimating in sunny weeks, the pattern is telling you something is missing, perhaps weather, and you should add it to the forecast. Never test a prediction on the same data you used to build it: that is like setting an exam from questions you already memorized, and it will flatter the method.

Prediction also has limits you must respect. First, the future may not resemble the past: a new pest, a broken water pipe, or a change in opening hours can break every pattern at once, and no method built on old data can see that coming. Say so in your findings. Second, predictions about people carry extra care: a forecast that a certain group will behave a certain way can become a self-fulfilling label, so report patterns about groups with caution and never present a group average as a fact about one person. Third, a prediction is not a cause: finding that volunteer numbers and harvests rise together does not prove that adding volunteers causes bigger harvests. Perhaps good weather brings both

more volunteers and better growth. Use the language of association, "weeks with more volunteers tended to harvest more", and let a proper test, such as trying extra volunteers for a few weeks and comparing, decide the cause.

Two mistakes dominate this lesson. First, false precision: reporting "47.382 kilograms" when your method is only accurate to within several kilograms. Round the forecast to the precision your data supports, and give a range. Second, forecasting far ahead: a one-week forecast built on recent weeks is reasonable, while a forecast for a year ahead built on the same data is mostly imagination. The further ahead you forecast, the wider the range should be, and the more clearly you should state the assumptions it rests on.

Practice: Using your fourteen-week garden data, build two forecasts for week 15: the average of the last three weeks, and the latest week plus the average weekly change over the last five weeks. Write both forecasts as a range and note which method you trust more and why. Then test your method properly: use weeks 1 through 10 to forecast week 11, compare with the actual week 11 value, and write down the error. Repeat for weeks 12, 13, and 14, each time using only the data before the week you forecast. Compute the average error across the four tests and write one paragraph: is your method good enough to plan planting from, and what would make it better?

Lesson 7: Presenting Your Findings

This lesson teaches you to present findings so other people can understand them and act on them. By the end you will structure a short findings report, write a clear one-page summary, choose a few good charts, and explain limits honestly. Analysis that nobody understands is wasted work: the presentation is where data science earns its keep, and a clear, honest report beats a clever analysis that nobody can follow.

A findings report has a simple structure that works for almost any project. Start with the question, in one sentence, and the decision it will inform. Then describe the data: where it came from, how many rows it has, what time period it covers, and what cleaning you did, in a few lines. Then give the findings, each as a short statement with its evidence: a number, a comparison, or a chart. Then give the recommendation: what should happen next, and what would prove the recommendation wrong. End with limits: what the data cannot tell you, and what extra data would help. That structure, question, data, findings, recommendation, limits, fits on two pages for a small project and scales to longer reports. A reader who only reads the first page should still walk away with the question, the main finding, and the recommendation.

The one-page summary is the most important page you will write. It opens with the question and the answer in plain language: "Sunny weeks harvest more than rainy weeks in this data, by about 12 kilograms a week on average." It follows with two or three supporting numbers, each with its context: the sunny average, the rainy average, and the number of weeks behind each. It includes one chart, the best one, with a title that states the finding. It closes with the recommendation and the limit: "Consider covered beds for rainy weeks, and track the next eight weeks to check whether the pattern holds." Notice what the summary does not do: it does not list every calculation, it does not use jargon without explaining it, and it does not hide uncertainty. If a term must appear, define it in the sentence where it appears: "the median, the middle value when weeks are lined up in order, was 38 kilograms."

Choosing charts for a presentation follows the same rules as Lesson 5, with one addition: fewer is better. A report with one excellent chart is more persuasive than a report with eight mediocre ones. Pick the chart that answers the main question, and add a second only if it answers a different question the reader needs. Every chart gets a title that states the finding, not just the topic: "Sunny weeks averaged the highest harvest" is a title, while "Harvest by weather" is only a label. Put the takeaway in words beneath the chart, so a reader who skims still gets the point. Keep the underlying table available, on a second page or in an appendix, so a skeptical reader can check the numbers. That openness is what makes a report trustworthy: you are showing your work, not asking for blind faith.

Writing about numbers needs care with language. Use "in this data" for every finding, because your data covers a particular garden, a particular season, and a particular set of weeks. Use "averaged" for means and medians, and say which one you used. Use "tended to" for relationships: "weeks with more volunteers tended to harvest more." Avoid causal words like causes, proves, and makes, unless you ran a test designed to show cause, such as changing one thing and comparing. Avoid vague intensifiers: "much bigger" means nothing, while "12 kilograms more on average" means something. When a finding is weak, say so: "the difference was small and could easily be chance" is an honest sentence that protects your credibility far more than a confident overclaim. Readers remember when you were honest about a weak result, and they trust your strong results more because of it.

Two mistakes ruin presentations. First, the data dump: pasting every table and chart you made into the report and letting the reader sort it out. Your job is to choose, and choosing is the work. Cut anything that does not answer the question or support the recommendation, and move the rest to an appendix if you must keep it. Second, the buried lead: putting the answer on page four after pages of method. State the answer first, then the evidence. Busy readers decide in the first paragraph whether to keep reading, so the first paragraph must carry the finding and the recommendation.

Practice: Write a one-page findings report about the garden data using the structure in this lesson: question, data, findings, recommendation, limits. Use your fourteen-week dataset and your sunny-versus-rainy comparison from Lesson 4, and include one bar chart with a finding-stating title and a one-sentence takeaway beneath it. Then show the report to someone who has not seen your data and ask them to tell you, in their own words, what the question was, what the answer was, and what you recommend. If they cannot answer all three, revise the report: the test is not whether you understand it, but whether a fresh reader does. Keep the revised report; it is the template for your final project.

Lesson 8: End-to-End Project — From Question to Recommendation

This final lesson walks a complete data project from question to recommendation, using every skill in the course, and then maps where your learning goes next. By the end you will see how the pieces connect in a realistic project, know how to structure your own analyses, and have a clear next-steps roadmap. This is the lesson that turns eight separate skills into one professional workflow, and the project you build here is the first full case study in your portfolio.

The case: the garden coordinator asks, "Should we add two more beds next season?" That is a good question: specific, tied to a decision, and answerable with data. Step one is planning: what data would answer it? Weekly harvests, beds in use, volunteer numbers, and weather, all of which the garden log already holds, plus the cost of two new beds and the time needed to tend them. Step two is cleaning: you scan the log and find the problems Lesson 3 trained you for, three dates typed in a different format, one week where harvest was entered with a unit word attached, two duplicate rows from a double entry, and two weeks with blank harvests that turn out to be weeks the garden was closed for a holiday, blanks, not zeros. You log every fix. Step three is analysis. The average harvest per bed is steady at about 4.5 kilograms a week across the season, and the median tells the same story, so the per-bed yield is real and not driven by one bumper week. Weather splits show the pattern holds on rainy weeks too, though at a lower level. A scatter plot of volunteers against harvest shows a gentle upward relationship, so you note that extra beds will need extra hands, or the yield per bed may fall.

Step four is the part beginners skip: checking the finding before presenting it. You ask the hard questions. Is fourteen weeks enough? It covers one season, autumn, but not spring or summer, so you note that limit openly. Could something else explain the steady yield? The beds were tended by the same small group all season, so a different group next season might tend them differently, and you cannot prove cause, so you report the pattern without claiming why, exactly as Lesson 4 taught. What would it cost? Two beds, soil, and seed run to a modest sum, and at the season's average yield the extra harvest would cover that cost in a few weeks of picking, but you present this as an estimate with stated assumptions, not a promise. Step five is the presentation: a one-page summary with three visuals, a per-bed yield bar chart, a season trend line, and a cost-versus-harvest comparison, each with a one-sentence takeaway, plus your recommendation: add one bed first, track the same metrics for a full season, and decide on the second bed with real data. Notice the recommendation includes how to verify it: good analysis always says what would prove it wrong.

That workflow, question, plan, clean, analyze, check, present, verify, is the template for every data project you will ever do. Write it on a card and keep it visible. The checking step is what separates a data scientist from a spreadsheet operator: anyone can compute an average, but the professional asks "what could make this wrong?" and answers honestly in the report. Your materials packet's checklist is built around this workflow, and you will use it on every project from now on. The other habit to keep is the cleaning log: it is the audit trail that lets anyone, including future you, trust the numbers and redo the work.

Where to go next depends on what pulled you. If databases excited you, go deeper into querying: selecting, filtering, grouping, and joining tables are the next tier, and any beginner guide to database queries plus practice datasets will carry you. If statistics grabbed you, the next concepts are correlation, basic probability, and testing whether a difference is real, and introductory statistics books written for

non-mathematicians are the right level. If charts and presentation were the thrill, study information design: how layout, color, and typography guide attention, and how to build a simple dashboard that updates itself. If you want it all to scale, learning a programming language for data work lets you automate cleaning and analysis on datasets far beyond spreadsheet size. Whichever path you take, the method stays the same: small real datasets, question first, clean carefully, check honestly, present clearly.

Practice: Run your own end-to-end mini project. Pick a question about your own life that data could answer, for example "do I spend more on weekdays or weekends?" or "which days am I most productive?" Collect two weeks of simple data in a log, date plus two or three columns, clean it with your checklist, compute one comparison with summaries, build one honest chart, make one careful forecast for the next week with a range, and write a one-page summary with a recommendation, including one paragraph on what could make your finding wrong and what data would check it. Save the spreadsheet and the summary together. This is the first full case study in your portfolio, and it demonstrates the complete workflow to anyone who sees it.
