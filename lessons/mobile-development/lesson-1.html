This lesson teaches you what a mobile app actually is and the two great strategies for building one. By the end you will explain what happens between tapping an icon and seeing a screen, compare native and cross-platform development honestly, and choose the right approach for a given app idea. This choice shapes everything downstream — cost, feel, and which devices you reach — so it comes first.

A mobile app is a program installed on the device that runs with direct access to the hardware: the screen, the touch sensor, the camera, the location chip, storage, and the network radio. When you tap an icon, the operating system loads the app into memory, shows its first screen, and hands it control of the display until you leave. Unlike a website, an app can work with no internet connection (if designed to), can send you notifications, can run partly in the background, and lives in the device's built-in app store, which handles discovery, installation, updates, and payment. Users judge apps by brutal standards formed over years: they must open fast, respond to every tap instantly, never lose data, and look native to the device — a sluggish or confusing app gets deleted in seconds.

There are two dominant mobile platforms: Apple's mobile platform (running on iOS devices) and Google's Android platform (running on Android devices). Together they cover effectively the entire market, and they differ in design language, conventions, and development tools. This is where the native vs. cross-platform decision enters.

Native development means building separately for each platform with its official tools and programming language: one codebase for iOS devices, a different codebase for Android devices. The result is the best possible fit — full access to every device feature the day it releases, the smoothest performance, and an interface that feels exactly like the rest of the device. The cost is duplication: two codebases to write, two to maintain, two skill sets (or two teams), and every feature built twice. Native fits apps where feel and performance are the product — games, camera apps, audio tools — and teams that can afford the investment.

Cross-platform development means writing one shared codebase with a toolkit that produces apps for both platforms. One team, one codebase, one feature built once — dramatically cheaper and faster, especially for small teams. The trade-offs: you wait on the toolkit for brand-new platform features, highly demanding graphics or hardware work may run less smoothly, and the interface can feel slightly "translated" rather than native unless you take care. Cross-platform fits content apps, business tools, forms-and-lists apps, and prototypes — which is most apps most teams actually build. A common pragmatic path: prototype cross-platform to prove the idea, then go native later only if the app's success justifies it.

Two mistakes beginners make here. First, choosing by hype instead of by fit: asking "which is better" instead of "which fits my app, team, and budget". A solo founder building a habit tracker and a studio building a video editor need different answers. Second, underestimating maintenance: building the app is the cheap part; updating it for years across operating-system releases is the real cost. The approach that is cheapest to maintain for your situation usually wins.

Practice: Open your app journal and pick three apps you use daily. For each, write: which platform(s) it runs on, three device features it uses (camera, location, notifications, offline storage, payments), and whether you think it is native or cross-platform — with one reason for your guess. Then write one paragraph: if you were building a simple expense-tracking app as a solo beginner, which approach would you choose and why? There is no single right answer; there is only a reasoned one.
