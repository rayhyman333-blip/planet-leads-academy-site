This lesson teaches you how apps are organized: screens as the unit of structure, navigation patterns that connect them, and flows that carry users through tasks. By the end you will map an app's screens, choose the right navigation pattern, and design the list-to-detail flow that powers most apps. Good structure is invisible — users feel it as "this app just makes sense" — and it is designed before a single screen is built.

Think of an app as a small building. Screens are the rooms; navigation is the hallways, doors, and signs. Every screen has one job (Lesson 2), a name you can say out loud ("the inbox", "the checkout screen"), and a clear way in and a clear way out. Before designing anything, list every screen your app needs — for a simple notes app: notes list, note editor, settings, maybe search results. That list is your blueprint; if a screen cannot justify its existence in one sentence, cut it or merge it.

Three navigation patterns cover nearly all apps. Tab navigation: a persistent bottom bar with three to five top-level destinations (Home, Search, Notifications, Profile). Use it when your app has a few equal, always-available sections — social apps, music apps, store apps. Stack navigation: screens pile up like cards; each new screen slides in with a back button returning to the previous one. Use it for drilling deeper — list to detail to sub-detail. Drawer/side-menu navigation: a hidden panel sliding from the edge with many destinations. Use it when you have more sections than fit in tabs, accepting that hidden navigation gets used less. Most real apps combine them: tabs for the top level, stacks within each tab. A hard rule: never nest the same pattern confusingly (tabs inside tabs), and the system back control must always do something predictable.

The list-to-detail flow is the most common structure in existence: a list of items (emails, products, songs, messages), tapping one opens its detail screen, and actions happen there. Master this and you can structure half the apps in the store. Related patterns: master-detail on larger screens (list and detail side by side), wizards for multi-step tasks (each step one screen, with back/next and a progress indicator), and modal screens for focused interruptions (compose, filters, confirmations) that dismiss back to exactly where you were.

Onboarding — the first-run experience — deserves special care because you only get one first impression. Show value before asking for anything: let users see or try the core experience before demanding an account, permissions, or a tutorial. Ask for permissions (location, notifications, camera) in context, at the moment they are needed, with a one-line explanation of why — a location prompt at launch with no explanation gets denied; the same prompt when the user taps "find nearby" gets accepted. Skip the five-screen tutorial carousel; nobody reads it. Teach with the interface itself: empty states that explain what to do ("No notes yet — tap + to create one") outperform lectures.

Two mistakes to avoid. First, dead ends: every screen needs a visible way back or forward — a modal with no dismiss button, a flow with no cancel, traps users and breeds one-star reviews. Second, navigation amnesia: when the app restarts or the user switches away and back, return them to where they were (or to a sensible home), never to a blank or broken state. State restoration is unglamorous and deeply appreciated.

Practice: In your journal, choose an app idea (yours or a simple one: a recipe box, a workout log, a plant-watering reminder). List every screen with its one-sentence job. Draw the navigation map as boxes and arrows: which pattern connects what, where stacks drill down, where modals interrupt. Mark the first-run path: what does a new user see, in order, before they reach the core value — and cut anything that delays it. If your map has more than about ten screens for a simple idea, simplify before proceeding to Lesson 4.
