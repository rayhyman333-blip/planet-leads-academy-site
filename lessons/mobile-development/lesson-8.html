This final lesson takes you to the finish line: the complete publishing checklist for releasing an app, your capstone app plan assembled from the whole course, and a map of where to go next. By the end you will know every step between "the app works on my device" and "anyone can install it", and you will hold a launch-ready plan for your own idea.

Publishing means submitting your app to the built-in app stores on each platform you support. Each store reviews submissions — a human and automated review that typically takes from a day to a week — checking for crashes, broken links, accurate descriptions, privacy compliance, and content rules. Plan for at least one rejection: reviewers reject for missing privacy policies, filler text, crashes on their test devices, or misleading descriptions. A rejection is normal, fixable, and usually specific — treat the first submission as a draft, not a verdict.

Run this publishing checklist in order. Identity: final app name (unique in each store, spelled consistently everywhere), app icon in all required sizes (simple, recognizable at tiny sizes, no text smaller than legible), and screenshots for each required screen size showing real content — never filler or mock data. Description: an honest, plain-language listing — what the app does, who it is for, key features — with no invented claims, no fake reviews, no misleading screenshots. Privacy: a published privacy policy URL (what you collect, why, how to delete — from Lesson 5), accurate answers to each store's privacy questionnaire, and permissions justified in context. Legal and content: age rating answered honestly, no copyrighted material you do not own, no hidden features. Technical: the release build tested from the test matrix (Lesson 7), version number set, crash reporting enabled so you learn about real-world failures, and a rollback plan if the release breaks. Business: pricing decided (free, paid, or in-app purchases), tax and banking forms completed in the developer accounts, and support contact information that reaches a human.

The review wait is working time, not idle time: prepare your launch announcement, write your help/FAQ page answering the five questions beta testers asked most, and line up your first ten users personally. On launch day, monitor crash reports hourly, respond to every early review, and fix critical crashes within days — the first version's stability sets your rating trajectory, and ratings compound.

Your capstone: assemble the complete app plan from your journal into one document. It must contain: the app's one-sentence purpose and target user (Lesson 1 thinking); three annotated screen sketches showing touch targets and each screen's one job (Lesson 2); the screen list and navigation map (Lesson 3); the input inventory with validation rules and error messages (Lesson 4); the data inventory with device/cloud decisions and offline behavior (Lesson 5); the network request list with loading, error, and offline states (Lesson 6); the test matrix (Lesson 7); and the publishing checklist above, filled in with your real answers. This document is your blueprint — hand it to any developer and they can build from it, or follow it yourself when you learn to build.

Where to go next depends on what excited you. If design thrilled you, study interface design deeply: typography, color, motion, and accessibility. If building thrilled you, learn a programming language and one platform's official tools, then build the smallest real version of your capstone. If the business thrilled you, study app store optimization, pricing, and analytics — how apps actually find users and earn money. Whatever you choose, the method stays the same: small scope, real users, test on devices, iterate. You have the complete foundation; the rest is practice.

Practice: Assemble your capstone document this week, one section per day from your journal. Then fill the publishing checklist with real answers for your app — real name candidates, real screenshot list, real privacy policy sentences. Finally, write the one-paragraph story of your app: what it does, who it is for, and why it deserves to exist. Read it aloud. If it convinces you, it will convince a reviewer, a tester, and a user. You have finished the course — now go build.
