This lesson teaches you to design interfaces for phones: small screens, fat fingers, and distracted users. By the end you will size touch targets correctly, choose readable type, lay out thumb-friendly screens, and apply the one-task-per-screen rule. Most failed apps do not fail on technology — they fail because real thumbs could not use them comfortably.

Start with the finger. Touch targets — every button, link, and tappable row — must be at least fingertip-sized. Both major platforms publish minimum-size guidance (roughly in the mid-40s of their respective point/pixel units), and the practical rule is simpler: if you have to aim carefully, it is too small. Give tappable elements generous padding rather than tiny precise hit areas, and separate neighboring targets with clear space so taps do not trigger the wrong one. This matters most for destructive or irreversible actions (delete, send, pay): make those deliberate, with a confirmation step, never a hair-trigger.

Typography on phones follows the same principle: bigger than you think. Body text should be comfortably readable at arm's length without zooming — when in doubt, go one size larger than your instinct. Keep line lengths short (the narrow screen does this for you), use generous line spacing, and maintain strong contrast between text and background. Never use color alone to convey meaning (red vs. green for status fails color-blind users) — pair color with an icon or label. Test your design decisions in sunlight, not just in a dim room: washed-out screens expose every contrast sin.

Layout for the thumb. Phone users hold the device one-handed, thumb sweeping the lower two-thirds of the screen — the top corners are the hardest to reach. Put primary actions (the main button, the compose control) where thumbs live: bottom of the screen, centered or right-ish. Put destructive or rarely-used actions up top or behind menus. Navigation belongs at the bottom on phones (tab bars), not at the top — this is a platform convention users expect, and violating it makes your app feel foreign. Content flows in a single vertical column; horizontal scrolling is for galleries, not for reading.

The one-task-per-screen rule is the deepest idea in this lesson. A phone screen fits one job: one form, one list, one article, one decision. When a screen tries to do three things, users do zero. Break flows into steps — address entry becomes three short screens instead of one monster form — and show progress ("Step 2 of 3") so the sequence feels finite. Each screen needs one clear primary action, visually dominant, with secondary actions quieter. If you cannot name a screen's single job in five words, split it.

Respect the user's context in every design decision. Phone sessions are short and interrupted — design for glanceability: the most important information visible without scrolling, actions completable in under a minute, and progress saved automatically so an interruption costs nothing. Animation should be subtle and purposeful: a quick transition that shows where a screen came from aids orientation, but bouncy, slow, or decorative motion just delays the user. When in doubt, make it faster and quieter — the best mobile interface is the one the user barely notices.

Two mistakes dominate mobile design. First, shrinking a desktop layout instead of designing for the phone: tiny text, cramped controls, and hover-dependent interactions (there is no hover on touchscreens) all scream "ported, not designed". Second, the cluttered first screen: beginners show every feature at once. Show the core task immediately; tuck the rest into navigation. A new user should understand what to do within five seconds of opening the app.

Practice: In your journal, audit one app you dislike using. List five specific design violations from this lesson (too-small targets, unreachable primary action, multiple tasks per screen, poor contrast, missing progress indicator). Then sketch — on paper — a better version of its worst screen: one task, thumb-reachable primary action, readable type. Label the touch targets and the screen's one job. Finally, hold your phone one-handed and note which screen regions your thumb reaches easily; mark those zones on your sketch. You just did real UX research.
