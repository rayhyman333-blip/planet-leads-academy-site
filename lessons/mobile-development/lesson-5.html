This lesson teaches you how apps remember things: the kinds of data apps store, where on-device storage fits, and the privacy responsibilities that come with keeping user data. By the end you will decide what your app stores locally versus in the cloud, design for the offline case, and handle sensitive data responsibly. An app that forgets is a toy; an app that remembers is a tool.

Apps store several kinds of data. Settings and preferences (theme choice, notification toggles, the last-used filter) — small, simple, read often. User content (notes, photos, workout logs, saved items) — the valuable stuff the user created. Cached content (downloaded articles, images, map tiles) — copies kept for speed and offline use. Credentials and tokens (the proof that the user is logged in) — small but security-critical. Each kind wants different treatment, but the principle is one: store on the device whatever the user needs instantly and offline; sync to the cloud whatever must survive a lost phone or be shared across devices.

On-device storage comes in tiers. Simple key-value storage suits settings and tiny flags ("hasSeenOnboarding = true"). Structured local databases suit real user content — thousands of notes, transactions, or records that need searching, sorting, and relationships. Files suit blobs: photos, audio, downloaded documents. The operating system gives each app its own private storage area other apps cannot read — that sandbox is your first layer of protection, and you should keep private data inside it rather than in shared locations. Design your data model early (Lesson 3's screens imply it: a notes app stores notes with titles, bodies, timestamps), because changing it after launch means migrating every existing user's data.

Offline-first thinking separates good apps from fragile ones. Assume the network will fail — in elevators, rural areas, dead zones — and decide per feature what happens. Reading: show the last synced data with a clear "updated 2 hours ago" stamp rather than an error wall. Writing: save locally immediately (the user sees instant success), then sync in the background and resolve conflicts sensibly (newest wins, or ask the user for real conflicts). Never make the user wait on the network for something the device already knows. And always distinguish "saved on this device" from "backed up": users who lose a phone discover the difference painfully — say plainly in your interface what is backed up and what is not.

Privacy is not a feature; it is the foundation. Collect the minimum data your app needs — every field you store is a field you must protect. Sensitive data (passwords, tokens, health or financial details) must be stored encrypted in the operating system's secure storage, never in plain files or simple settings. Ask permission before accessing contacts, photos, location, or anything personal, explain why in one sentence, and respect "no" gracefully with reduced functionality, not a dead end. Write a plain-language privacy policy before launch (Lesson 8 requires one): what you collect, why, where it lives, and how to delete it. Regulations in many regions give users deletion rights — build the "delete my data" path from the start, not as an afterthought.

Two mistakes to avoid. First, treating the device as a safe: storing passwords or tokens in plain text because "it's on the user's phone". Phones get lost, sold, and backed up to clouds — encrypt secrets properly. Second, the sync ambush: silently uploading the user's address book or photos "to improve the experience". Anything uploaded must be consented, disclosed, and deletable — trust, once lost, never returns.

Practice: In your journal, write your app idea's data inventory: every piece of data it keeps, classified as settings, user content, cache, or credentials. For each, decide: device only, cloud only, or both — with one reason. Sketch the offline behavior of your three most important screens: what shows with no connection, and what happens to user actions. Finally, draft the three sentences of your future privacy policy: what you collect, why, and how a user deletes it. If any sentence feels hard to write honestly, your data plan needs simplifying.
