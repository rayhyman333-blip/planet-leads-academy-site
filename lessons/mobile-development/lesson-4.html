This lesson teaches you to handle input gracefully on touch devices: choosing the right input method, validating without frustrating, and writing error messages that help. By the end you will pick keyboards, pickers, and toggles appropriately; design forms people actually finish; and handle the edge cases beginners forget. Typing on glass is the most painful thing users do — your job is to minimize it and forgive its errors.

Minimize typing first. Every field you delete is a field that cannot be filled wrong. Prefer choosing over typing wherever possible: a date picker instead of a typed date, a predefined list instead of a free-text category, a stepper (+/−) for quantities, toggles for yes/no instead of text. When typing is unavoidable, show the right keyboard: the email keyboard (with @ and .) for email fields, the numeric keypad for amounts and codes, the phone pad for phone numbers. Pre-fill whatever you already know, offer sensible defaults, and remember previous entries. Autocorrect and autocapitalization should be disabled for emails, usernames, codes, and addresses — "correcting" a street name into a different word is a classic mobile bug.

When forms are necessary, design them for completion. One column, top-aligned labels (never use the hint text inside the field as the label — it vanishes when typing starts, punishing anyone who looks away), logical grouping with section headers, and a single prominent submit action. Validate as the user goes, but kindly: flag an error after the user leaves a field, not while they are mid-keystroke; clear the error the moment it is fixed. Mark required fields explicitly (an asterisk with a legend, or the word "required") rather than surprising users at submit. For long forms, show progress and save drafts — losing twenty fields to a phone call is how apps get deleted.

Error messages are a design surface, not an afterthought. A good error message says what happened, why, and how to fix it, in plain words, next to the field: "Enter a valid email, like name@example.com" beats "Invalid input". Never blame the user ("You entered an incorrect value"), never show raw technical codes, and never clear the whole form on error — preserve everything valid and highlight only what needs fixing. Confirmations matter too: after success, say what happened ("Reminder saved for 8:00 AM") rather than a bare "Done".

Handle the edge cases beginners forget. No connection: forms must not silently swallow submissions — queue them and confirm when sent, or clearly say sending failed and keep the data. Interruptions: a phone call, a dead battery, an accidental swipe — the form's contents should survive all of them. Accessibility: every input needs a real label (screen readers cannot see placeholders), error messages must be announced, and the whole form must work with large text sizes. Timeouts: if submission takes more than a moment, show progress and never let the user tap "submit" twice into a duplicate charge or double post — disable the button while sending.

Two mistakes to watch for. First, the hostile submit: validating everything only at the end with one generic "form has errors" message and no indication of where. Users should never have to hunt for their own mistake. Second, cleverness over clarity: custom date pickers, novel toggle gestures, and artistic keyboards all test worse than the platform's standard controls. Standard controls are free, accessible, and already learned by every user — spend your originality on your app's purpose, not its text fields.

Practice: In your journal, design the signup-and-profile flow for your Lesson 3 app idea: list every field, then cut at least a third of them (justify each survivor). For each remaining field, specify: input method (which keyboard/picker/toggle), validation rule, and the exact error message text. Sketch the form's three states on paper: empty, with one error shown, and success confirmation. Then test a real app's form against this lesson and note two things it does well and two it does badly — critical reading of other apps is how the patterns stick.
