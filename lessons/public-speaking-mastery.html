Full Lessons — Public Speaking Mastery

Full lesson text, Academy-built. This is the complete 8-lesson course text for this course. Prices and course links are on the course page.

Speak clearly and confidently in front of any room, with practical techniques you can practise and a routine that makes you better every time.

Lesson 1: Beating Nerves

This lesson teaches you what nerves actually are and how to work with them, because nerves are where most speakers get stuck — and where most speaking advice goes wrong. The first thing to understand is that nerves are normal, physical, and shared by almost every speaker you have ever admired. A racing heart, shaky hands, a dry mouth, a blank mind in the first minute: these are your body preparing for something important, the same response it gives before a big game or an exam. Nerves are not evidence that you are bad at speaking. They are evidence that you care about doing well, and they fade faster than you expect once you start talking. The goal of this lesson is not to eliminate nerves — that is not possible, and trying makes them worse — but to reduce them to a manageable level and to keep speaking well while they are present.

Start with preparation, because most nerves are really under-preparation wearing a disguise. When you know your first minute word for word, you have something solid to stand on while the nerves settle. When you know your structure — the three points in their order — you cannot truly get lost; you can always find the next point. When you have practised out loud three times, your mouth knows the words even when your mind goes blank. Prepare in this order: decide your one clear message, outline your three points, write your opening and closing in full, then practise the whole talk out loud, standing up, at least three times. Time yourself with a clock. Most nervous speakers are surprised by how much calmer they feel after the third full run-through, because the talk has stopped being an unknown and become something their body has already done.

On the day, use your body to calm your body. Slow breathing is the most reliable tool you have: breathe in for a slow count of four, hold for a count of four, breathe out for a slow count of six, and repeat four times before you stand up. Long out-breaths slow your heart rate directly, and you can do this in a hallway or a bathroom without anyone noticing. Arrive early and stand where you will speak. Look at the chairs, touch the table, test the clicker or the microphone if there is one. Familiar ground feels less threatening than strange ground. Drink water, eat lightly, and avoid a heavy meal right before you speak. Wear clothes you have already worn and feel comfortable in — a new outfit that pinches or slips will occupy your mind at the worst moment.

Reframe the audience in your mind. Nervous speakers imagine a hostile crowd waiting to judge them. Real audiences are almost always on your side: they came to learn something or to support you, and they want you to do well, because your success makes their time worthwhile. Think of the room as a group of individuals having a conversation with you, not as a single judging eye. Find two or three friendly faces in different parts of the room before you begin, and speak to them in turn during the talk. It feels like a conversation, and the audience sees a speaker who is connected to real people. And accept that the audience cannot see most of what you feel. Your hands may shake slightly; from the third row, you look still. Your heart may race; your voice sounds steady. The gap between how nervous you feel inside and how nervous you look outside is far larger than you think.

Two mistakes to avoid. The first is memorising the whole talk word for word. A memorised script sounds stiff, and one forgotten line can derail the entire talk because you have no way back except the exact next word. Memorise only your opening lines and your closing lines; for the rest, know your points and speak them in your own words each time. The second mistake is apologising for your nerves or your talk at the start: "Sorry, I am really nervous," or "This probably will not be very good." An apology tells the audience to lower their expectations and to look for flaws. Skip it entirely. Start with your opening, and let the talk speak for itself.

Practice: (1) Write down your three worst nervous symptoms — the ones that bother you most — and next to each, write the matching countermeasure from this lesson: preparation for a blank mind, slow breathing for a racing heart, arriving early for unfamiliar-room dread. (2) Practise the slow breathing pattern four times, right now, and note how you feel afterwards. (3) Choose your running-example talk and write out your first minute word for word, then say it out loud three times, standing up. (4) Write a one-paragraph plan for the day of the talk: when you will arrive, what you will check, when you will breathe, and which friendly faces you will look for. Keep this plan with your talk notes.

Lesson 2: Structuring a Talk

This lesson teaches you to structure a talk so listeners can follow it without effort. A good talk has one clear message, a beginning, a middle, and an ending that lands — and the listener should be able to repeat the message afterwards in one sentence. Structure is not decoration; it is the thing that turns a pile of information into something a human brain can hold. Audiences forget most of what they hear within days, but they remember a talk whose message was clear and whose shape was easy to follow. Your job in this lesson is to build that shape for your own talk.

Start with the message, not with the material. Before you write a single slide or paragraph, finish this sentence in one line: "After my talk, the audience will understand — or believe, or do — this one thing." That is your message, and everything else in the talk must serve it. A talk about a neighbourhood recycling project might have the message: "Our street can cut its rubbish in half with three simple changes." A work presentation might have the message: "The new rota will give every team member two weekends off a month." If you cannot state your message in one sentence, you do not have a talk yet — you have a topic. Keep the message in front of you while you build the talk, and cut anything that does not serve it, however interesting it is. A talk is not a list of everything you know; it is the shortest route to one idea.

Build the middle from three points, no more. Three is the number a listener can hold in their head: a beginning, a middle point, and a final point, each supporting the message. For the recycling talk, the three points might be: sort correctly, compost food scraps, and share the collection rota. For the work rota talk: the problem with the current rota, how the new rota works, and what happens in the first month. Each point gets one supporting piece of evidence — a concrete example, a simple comparison, or a short story — and nothing else. If a point needs two pieces of evidence, it is really two points, and you should split it or choose the stronger one. Write each point as a full sentence, not a single word: "Sorting correctly doubles what gets recycled" is a point a listener can remember; "Sorting" is not.

Write the beginning and the ending last, even though the audience hears them first and last. The beginning must do two jobs in the first minute: earn attention and tell the audience what the talk is about and why it matters to them. Lesson 3 covers openings in detail. The ending must do two jobs as well: repeat the message in fresh words and give the audience one clear next step — something to do, think about, or discuss. Never end by trailing off, saying "so, yeah, that's it," or apologising. Plan your final sentence word for word, and then stop. A talk that ends cleanly feels twice as good as a talk that dribbles away, even if the middle was identical. The last thing the audience hears is what they carry out of the room.

Signpost the structure as you speak, because listeners cannot see your outline. Use short bridge sentences between points: "That was the problem. Now here is how the new rota fixes it." Use simple numbered language when it helps: "First, sorting. Second, composting. Third, sharing the rota." Tell the audience where you are and where you are going — it sounds obvious, and it is exactly what keeps a room with you for ten minutes. Without signposts, even a well-organised talk feels like a wandering one, because the listener has no map.

Two mistakes to avoid. The first is the information dump: packing in everything you know because you are afraid of leaving something out. The audience cannot absorb ten points, so they absorb none — and they leave remembering that the talk was long. Trust the three-point structure and leave the rest for questions. The second mistake is a talk with no ending: the speaker runs out of time, rushes the

conclusion, and stops mid-thought. Rehearse the ending separately and protect time for it — if you must cut something, cut a middle example, never the ending.

Practice: Build the structure of your running-example talk. (1) Write your one-sentence message, finishing the sentence "After my talk, the audience will understand, believe, or do this one thing." (2) Write your three points as full sentences, each with one supporting example. (3) Write a one-sentence bridge between point one and point two, and between point two and point three. (4) Write your final sentence word for word, including the one clear next step you want the audience to take. (5) Say the whole structure out loud — message, three points, closing — in under two minutes. If you cannot, something is too complicated; simplify it.

Lesson 3: Opening Strong

This lesson teaches you to open strong, because the first minute of a talk decides whether the room listens or drifts. Listeners make a fast, mostly unconscious judgement in the opening moments: is this speaker worth my attention? That judgement is not about your talent — it is about whether you seem prepared, whether you know what you are talking about, and whether the talk promises something useful. A strong opening answers three silent questions in the listener's mind: who are you, what is this about, and why should I care? You have about sixty seconds to answer them, and this lesson gives you the tools to do it well.

The strongest openings are specific, not general. Compare two openings for a talk about a neighbourhood recycling project. Weak: "Hello everyone, today I am going to talk about recycling, which is an important topic for all of us." That opening tells the audience nothing they did not know and gives them no reason to listen. Strong: "Last month, our street threw away three truckloads of rubbish. Half of it could have been recycled. Today I will show you the three changes that would cut our rubbish in half." The strong version is concrete — three truckloads, half, three changes — and it makes a promise the listener can hold on to. Your opening should contain a concrete detail, a clear statement of the talk's subject, and a reason it matters to this audience. Write your opening in full and practise it word for word, so the first minute is the most confident minute of your talk.

Here are four opening patterns that work reliably, and you can choose the one that fits your topic. The first is a surprising fact or a concrete number that reframes the problem — the truckloads example above. The second is a short story: thirty seconds about a real moment that shows the problem, such as the morning you found the shared bins overflowing again. The third is a question that puts the listener inside the topic: "How many of you spent last weekend sorting rubbish you were not sure belonged in the bin?" A question works because the listener answers it in their head and is suddenly participating. The fourth is a simple, direct statement of the message: "I am here to show you how our team can finish the project two weeks early." All four patterns share one trait: they are about the audience's world, not about you. Openings that start with your biography, your credentials, or an apology waste the minute when attention is highest.

What you do physically in the opening matters as much as your words. Walk to your spot, plant your feet, look at the room, pause for two seconds, and then begin. That pause feels enormous to you and looks like confidence to the audience. Speak your first sentence more slowly than feels natural — nervous speakers rush the opening, and a slow first sentence sets the pace for the whole talk. Make eye contact with one friendly face for your first sentence, then another for your second. Do not start talking while you are still walking, shuffling papers, or finding your first slide — the audience reads that as unpreparedness before you have said a word.

Two mistakes to avoid. The first is the long, rambling introduction about yourself: your job history, how you got interested in the topic, a joke that needs explaining. The audience needs one sentence about who you are and why you are the person speaking — no more. The second mistake is opening with an apology or a disclaimer: "I am not really a public speaker," or "This will probably be boring." An apology trains the audience to expect a bad talk and to look for its flaws. If the talk is good, no apology is needed; if it is not, an apology does not save it. Begin with your material, not with your doubts.

Practice: Write three different openings for your running-example talk, one for each of three patterns: a concrete fact or number, a short story of thirty seconds, and a question for the audience. Keep each

opening under one hundred words. Read all three out loud, standing up, and pick the one that feels most natural for you and most relevant to your audience. Then practise that opening five times: walk to your spot, pause for two seconds, look at an imaginary friendly face, and deliver the opening slowly. Time yourself — the opening should land inside one minute. Write the winning opening at the top of your talk notes in full.

Lesson 4: Voice and Body Language

This lesson teaches you to use your voice and your body to support your words, because how you say something shapes how it is heard. A talk with strong content and a flat, rushed delivery loses the room; a talk with simple content and a clear, varied delivery holds it. The good news is that voice and body are trainable skills, not talents you are born with. Small, specific changes — pausing more, slowing down, standing still, looking at real faces — produce large, visible improvements, and you can practise every one of them alone in a room.

Start with pace. Nervous speakers rush, and rushing does two kinds of damage: the audience cannot keep up, and the speaker runs out of breath and sounds panicked. The fix is simple and feels strange at first: speak more slowly than you think you should. A comfortable speaking pace for most talks is around 130 to 150 words a minute — about the pace of a calm conversation, not a newsreader in a hurry. Practise with a timer: read a page of your talk aloud and count the words, then divide by the minutes. If you are far above that range, mark pause points in your notes — a line break, a slash, a reminder to breathe — at the end of each main point. Pauses are the most underused tool in speaking. A two-second pause after an important sentence lets it land, gives the audience a moment to think, and gives you a moment to breathe. Silence feels awkward to the speaker and powerful to the listener.

Next, volume and variety. Project your voice to the back of the room, not to the front row: imagine speaking to the person furthest away, and keep your volume steady so the last row hears you as clearly as the first. Vary your tone to match your meaning — a little higher and quicker for something exciting, lower and slower for something serious. Monotone delivery is not a voice problem; it is a meaning problem: the speaker is not letting the words matter. Mark two or three moments in your talk where the meaning changes — a surprising turn, a serious point, a hopeful ending — and practise giving each one a different energy. You do not need to perform; you need to sound like you mean what you are saying.

Your body speaks before your words do. Stand with your feet planted shoulder-width apart, weight even, shoulders relaxed. This grounded stance looks confident and stops the nervous swaying and pacing that distract audiences. Use gestures that match your words: count points on your fingers when you list three things, open your hands when you describe something large, point briefly when you refer to a slide. Keep gestures above waist height and below shoulder height, and let your hands rest naturally at your sides between gestures — hands that flap constantly are as distracting as hands that never move. Move with purpose: if you step closer to the audience for an important point, that movement means something. Random wandering means nothing and pulls attention away from your words.

Eye contact is the single most powerful body skill, and it is simpler than people think. Do not scan the room like a lighthouse, and do not stare at your notes, your slides, or the floor. Instead, speak one thought to one person, then move to another person in a different part of the room for the next thought. Each person feels spoken to for a few seconds, and the whole room feels included over the course of the talk. If eye contact feels hard, look at foreheads or at the space between people — from the audience's view, it looks the same. Avoid the common trap of speaking only to the friendliest face or only to the person in charge; spread your attention across the room, including the back rows, who are the easiest to lose.

Two mistakes to avoid. The first is filler sounds — repeated "um," "like," "you know" — which creep in when the speaker is thinking aloud. The cure is not willpower but pauses: replace the filler with a short silence. Record yourself for two minutes and count your fillers; most speakers are shocked, and the count drops fast once you practise pausing instead. The second mistake is reading your talk from a script or from your slides. A speaker reading aloud sounds distant and loses eye contact entirely. Use brief notes — a card with your three points and your opening and closing lines — and speak from those.

Practice: (1) Record yourself speaking for two minutes on any topic, then listen back and count your filler sounds, note your pace, and notice whether your voice varies. (2) Take one paragraph of your running-example talk and mark it up: add pause marks at the end of each main point, underline the two sentences that deserve extra weight, and note where you will look in the room. (3) Practise that paragraph three times standing up: feet planted, voice projected to the far wall, one thought to one imaginary person at a time. (4) Write a one-paragraph plan for your body during the full talk: where you will stand, when you will move, and which gestures fit your three points.

Lesson 5: Slides That Help Instead of Hurt

This lesson teaches you to design slides that help the audience and never replace you as the speaker. Slides are a visual aid, not a script: their job is to show what words cannot show easily — a picture, a diagram, a short list — while you do the talking. Most bad slides fail in the same way: they contain the whole talk in small text, so the audience reads instead of listening, and the speaker reads aloud what the audience has already read. This lesson gives you the rules for slides that support you, and a simple method for building them.

The first rule is that you are the presentation and the slides are the aid. If your slides disappeared, your talk should still work, because your structure, your examples, and your message live in your notes and in your head — not on the screen. Test every slide with one question: does this help the audience understand or remember something my words alone cannot deliver? A photo of the overflowing bins helps; a paragraph about the bins does not. A simple diagram of the new rota helps; a wall of bullet points repeating your speech does not. If a slide fails the test, delete it. Most talks need far fewer slides than speakers think: one slide per main point, plus an opening slide and a closing slide, is a good starting point for a ten-minute talk.

The second rule is about words on slides: fewer, bigger, simpler. Aim for no more than six words per line and six lines per slide — and fewer is better. Use short phrases, not sentences, because the audience cannot read sentences and listen to you at the same time; they will choose reading, and you will lose them. Use a large, plain type size that the back row can read, and high contrast between text and background — dark text on a light background, or light text on a dark background. Avoid decorative fonts, tiny footnotes, and dense tables. If you have a table or a detailed figure that matters, simplify it to the one row or the one comparison the audience needs, and keep the full version as a handout or a follow-up document.

The third rule is about images and design. One strong image per slide beats a collage of small ones. Choose images that show something real: the place, the people, the object, the result. Keep the design consistent — the same colours, the same layout, the same style of headings on every slide — so the audience stops noticing the design and focuses on the content. Leave generous empty space around your content; crowded slides feel stressful to look at. If you use charts, keep them simple: one idea per chart, clear labels, and no more than three or four data points. A chart the audience cannot understand in five seconds is a chart that should not be on a slide.

Rehearse with your slides, not just with your notes. Run through the talk standing up, advancing the slides as you will on the day, and check three things: that each slide appears at the moment you refer to it, that you never turn your back to read the screen, and that you know what to do if the slides fail. The backup plan is simple: your notes carry the talk, so a failed projector changes nothing except the visuals. Arrive early and test the setup — the display, the remote, the font sizes from the back of the room. And never apologise for your slides or read them aloud. If you find yourself reading a slide word for word, that slide has too many words on it: cut the words until you can talk about the slide instead of reading it.

Two mistakes to avoid. The first is the slide deck as a script: dozens of dense slides that the speaker reads aloud while the audience zones out. If your slides contain full sentences, move those sentences into your speaker notes and reduce the slide to its key phrase or image. The second mistake is distracting design: animations on every slide, bright colours, clip art, and sound effects that pull

attention from your message. A slide should be boring to look at and useful to understand. Save the energy for your delivery.

Practice: Build or rebuild the slides for your running-example talk. (1) List the slides you actually need — opening, one per main point, closing — and write one sentence for each explaining what it helps the audience see. (2) For each slide, write the content as short phrases only, no full sentences, and note the image or diagram it needs. (3) Check each slide against the rules: six words per line or fewer, large plain type, high contrast, one idea per slide. (4) Rehearse the talk with the slides once, standing up, and note any moment you turned to read the screen or lost track of where you were. (5) Write your backup plan in two sentences: what you will do if the display fails, and where your notes carry the talk without slides.

Lesson 6: Handling Questions

This lesson teaches you to handle questions well, including hard ones, hostile ones, and ones you cannot answer. The question session is not an exam — it is the part of the talk where the audience gets to think with you, and it often determines what they remember. A speaker who handles questions calmly and honestly leaves a stronger impression than a speaker who gave a flawless talk and then panicked at the first difficult question. The skills in this lesson are simple, but they need practice, because questions are the one part of a talk you cannot fully script.

Start with the basics of receiving a question. When someone asks, stop, look at them, and listen to the whole question before you answer. Do not interrupt, do not guess the ending, and do not start answering while they are still talking — the audience notices, and it reads as dismissive. If the room is large, repeat the question briefly in your own words, so everyone can hear it and so you can check you understood it: "You are asking whether the new rota will affect holiday cover — is that right?" Repeating the question also buys you a few seconds to think, which is a legitimate technique, not a trick. If the question is long or unclear, ask for clarification before answering: "Could you say a little more about which part you mean?" A good answer to the right question beats a fast answer to the wrong one.

Answer directly, briefly, and honestly. A good answer is usually three to five sentences: the direct answer first, one supporting reason or example, and then a stop. Speakers get into trouble by answering at length, circling the question, or giving a mini-talk in response to every question. If you do not know the answer, say so plainly — "I do not know that yet, and I will find out and let you know" — and mean it. Audiences respect an honest "I don't know" far more than a confident invention, and inventing an answer destroys your credibility if anyone in the room knows the truth. Write down the question and the promise before you leave the room, and follow up: a speaker who follows up after saying "I will find out" earns trust that no perfect answer can buy.

Handle difficult questioners with calm and structure. If someone asks a hostile or loaded question, do not match their tone. Pause, restate the question neutrally, answer the substance, and move on: "That is a fair concern about cost. Here is how the costs work." If someone asks three questions at once, answer the first and invite them to repeat the others. If someone wants to debate rather than ask, acknowledge their view briefly and offer to continue the conversation after the session — "That is a useful challenge, and I would like to hear more after we finish, so everyone gets their questions in." If someone is simply wrong about a fact, correct them gently with the evidence, not with a put-down: "The figure I have is different — here is where it comes from." Your job in the question session is to stay fair, steady, and generous, even when the question is not.

Plan for the question session before the talk begins. Think of the five hardest questions your audience might ask, and prepare a short answer for each — this is the best rehearsal you can do, because it forces you to find the weak spots in your own argument. Decide in advance how you will invite questions: "I would love your questions now," with a pause long enough for hands to go up, works better than rushing on. If no one asks, have a question ready for yourself: "One question people often ask is..." and answer it — this breaks the silence and usually starts the flow. End the session cleanly: after the last question, thank the audience and repeat your message in one sentence, so the talk ends on your point rather than on a random question.

Two mistakes to avoid. The first is bluffing: inventing an answer to a question you do not know. It feels safer in the moment and is far more dangerous afterwards, because a wrong answer discovered later undoes the trust your whole talk built. The second mistake is taking a question personally. A challenging question is usually about the topic, not about you — the questioner is testing the idea, and a calm, honest response makes the idea stronger.

Practice: (1) Write the five hardest questions your audience could ask about your running-example talk, and write a short answer for each — direct answer first, one supporting reason, then stop. (2) Practise answering two of them out loud, with a friend or alone, timing yourself: aim for under one minute per answer. (3) Write your "I don't know" sentence in your own words, and practise saying it calmly — it should sound confident, not defeated. (4) Prepare your self-question: the question people often ask, with your answer. (5) Write your closing line for the end of the question session: a thank-you and your message in one sentence.

Lesson 7: Storytelling

This lesson teaches storytelling: how to tell short, honest stories that make your ideas stick in the listener's mind. Facts tell, but stories hold. An audience may forget your three points within a week, but they will remember the story of the morning the bins overflowed, the colleague who stayed late to fix the rota, or the first customer who came back. Stories work because human brains are built to follow people through trouble to a resolution — and because a story carries emotion, which is what makes a memory last. You do not need to be a natural storyteller. A good talk story is short, specific, and true, and this lesson gives you a simple structure for building one.

A talk story has four parts, and it should take sixty to ninety seconds to tell. First, the setting: one sentence placing us in a real moment — "It was a Tuesday morning, and the shared bins behind our building were overflowing again." Second, the person and the problem: who was there and what went wrong — "Our caretaker had already emptied them twice that week, and residents were leaving bags on the pavement." Third, the turning point: what changed — "So we tried a simple change: a shared rota and a sorting guide on every floor." Fourth, the result and the meaning: what happened and why it matters for your message — "Within a month, the overflow stopped, and the rota is the first of the three changes I want to show you today." Notice the shape: a real moment, a real problem, a change, a result. If your story has no problem and no change, it is not a story — it is a description, and it will not hold attention.

Choose stories that are true and that you have the right to tell. The best stories come from your own experience: something you saw, did, or learned. If a story involves another person, keep it respectful and anonymous unless you have their permission to name them — change identifying details and focus on the situation, not the person. Avoid stories that make you the hero in every scene; audiences trust a speaker who admits a mistake or a false start far more than one who is flawless in every tale. A story about getting something wrong and learning from it is often the strongest story in a talk, because it shows honesty and gives the audience permission to be imperfect too. Keep your stories short: one story per talk is often enough, two at most, and each under ninety seconds.

Tell the story, do not summarise it. The difference is in the concrete detail: the overflowing bins, the Tuesday morning, the caretaker's name, the bags on the pavement. Concrete details let the listener picture the scene, and a pictured scene is a remembered scene. Speak the story more slowly than the rest of your talk, pause before the turning point, and let the result land before you move on. Use the present tense for the vivid moment — "I am standing in front of the bins" — and the past tense for the setup. Practise the story out loud at least three times, because a story told for the first time on stage always wanders. Time it: if it runs past ninety seconds, cut the least important detail, not the turning point or the result.

Connect the story to your message explicitly. A story that ends without a link to the point leaves the audience entertained and confused. After the result, add one bridge sentence: "That morning is why the first change matters," or "That is what the new rota fixes." The bridge tells the listener what to take from the story and where the talk is going next. Place your story where it does the most work: in the opening, to earn attention; at the start of a main point, to show why it matters; or near the ending, to leave the audience with a feeling they will carry out of the room.

Two mistakes to avoid. The first is the long, wandering story that loses the point: five minutes of detail, no turning point, no result, and an audience that has forgotten why you started. Keep stories short,

structured, and tied to the message. The second mistake is the invented or borrowed story presented as your own experience. If you did not live it, do not tell it as if you did — say where it comes from, or choose a true story from your own life. Honesty is not only the right choice; it is also the safer one, because a borrowed story told badly is easy to spot.

Practice: (1) Think of three moments from your own experience that relate to your running-example talk: a problem you saw, a mistake you made, or a change that worked. Write each in two sentences. (2) Pick the strongest one and build it into the four-part structure: setting, person and problem, turning point, result and meaning. Keep it under ninety seconds when spoken. (3) Write the bridge sentence that connects the story to your message. (4) Tell the story out loud three times, standing up, pausing before the turning point. (5) Ask a listener, if you can, what they remember from the story a day later — that tells you whether it stuck.

Lesson 8: A Practice and Feedback Routine

This final lesson builds your practice and feedback routine: a repeatable method for rehearsing any talk and getting better every time you give one. Talent is not the difference between good speakers and poor ones; practice is. Good speakers rehearse out loud, seek honest feedback, and change one thing at a time. This lesson gives you a routine you can use for every talk you give from now on, so your tenth talk is visibly better than your first — and so you never again walk into a room having only read your notes silently.

Rehearse in three stages, each with a different purpose. Stage one is the table read: sit with your notes and read the talk aloud once, slowly, to find the sentences that do not work when spoken. Written sentences are often too long for speech — if you run out of breath halfway through a sentence, split it in two. Fix awkward phrases, cut anything that sounds stiff, and make sure your opening and closing are exactly as you want them. Stage two is the standing run-through: stand up, use your notes or cards, advance your slides if you have them, and give the whole talk out loud at least three times. Time each run. The first run will feel rough; the third will feel like a talk. Stage three is the dress rehearsal: give the talk to one or two friendly listeners, in the clothes you will wear, standing where you will stand, with the same equipment. Ask them to time you and to note the moments they lost track or felt confused. A dress rehearsal the day before is worth more than a week of silent reading.

Seek feedback that is specific and usable. Vague praise — "that was great" — feels good and teaches nothing. Ask your listeners for three specific things: one moment that worked and why, one moment that confused them or lost their attention, and one thing they would change. Write their answers down in your feedback log, in their words. Then decide what to change — one or two things, not everything. The most useful feedback is often about the audience's experience, not your performance: "I lost the thread in the second point" tells you to add a signpost; "the opening felt long" tells you to cut the introduction. Thank your listeners for honesty, especially when it stings, because honest feedback is rare and valuable, and people stop giving it to speakers who react badly.

Review your own talks with the same honesty. After every real talk, take ten minutes to write down four things: what went well, what went wrong, what surprised you, and what you will change next time. Note the practical details too — the room, the timing, the equipment, the audience's mood — because those details explain a lot of what happened. If you can, record yourself occasionally and listen back with a checklist: pace, pauses, fillers, eye contact, slides. You will notice things no audience member would tell you, and the recording does not flatter you — which is exactly why it helps. Keep the notes in one place, your practice and feedback log, so you can see your progress over months. Improvement in speaking is slow and visible in the log long before it feels visible in the moment.

Build the routine into your calendar for every future talk. A simple schedule works: seven days before, finish the structure and the opening; five days before, do the table read and fix the language; three days before, do three standing run-throughs and time them; the day before, do the dress rehearsal with listeners and collect feedback; the morning of the talk, do one slow run of the opening and closing only, then stop. Do not rehearse frantically in the final hour — over-rehearsing right before you speak makes you tired and tight. Trust the preparation you have done, breathe, and go and speak. After the talk, do your ten-minute review the same day, while the memory is fresh.

Two mistakes to avoid. The first is practising silently: reading your notes in your head and calling it rehearsal. Silent reading does not train your mouth, your breath, or your timing, and the first time you

speak the words aloud should not be in front of the audience. The second mistake is collecting feedback and ignoring it. Feedback you do not act on is wasted, and listeners who see no change stop offering it. Change one thing per talk, note the result, and keep going. That is the whole secret of improvement: one talk, one change, repeated.

Practice: Build your routine and use it on your running-example talk. (1) Schedule your three rehearsal stages with dates: table read, three standing run-throughs, dress rehearsal with listeners. (2) Do the table read now, reading the talk aloud and fixing any sentence you cannot say in one breath. (3) Do one standing run-through, timing it, and write down the three moments that felt weakest. (4) Ask one listener for the three specific feedback items — one moment that worked, one that confused them, one thing to change — and record them in your feedback log. (5) Write your ten-minute post-talk review template in advance, so you can fill it in on the day. Keep the log: it is the record of how you became a better speaker.
