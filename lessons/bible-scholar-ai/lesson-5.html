This lesson teaches the central figure of the New Testament: Jesus of Nazareth as the Gospels present him — his life's shape, his distinctive teaching method in parables, and the core content of his message. Christians hold Jesus to be both fully human and fully divine, the Messiah promised in the Old Testament, and understanding how the text presents him is essential to the whole course.

The Gospels present Jesus's life in a clear arc. Born into a Jewish family under Roman rule, he is baptized by John, tempted in the wilderness, and begins a public ministry of teaching, healing, and gathering disciples in Galilee. Opposition grows as his claims and his table-fellowship with outsiders offend religious leaders. He journeys to Jerusalem, is betrayed, tried, and crucified — and the text presents him risen on the third day, appearing to his followers before ascending. Luke's Gospel especially emphasizes the marginalized people Jesus welcomes: tax collectors, Samaritans, women, the poor. The shape of the story matters: the text presents a ministry of compassion climaxing in self-giving death, not a career of accumulating power.

Parables are Jesus's signature teaching tool — short stories drawn from ordinary life that carry a surprising turn. The Good Samaritan (Luke 10) answers 'who is my neighbor?' by making the hero a member of a despised group. The Prodigal Son (Luke 15) presents a father's lavish welcome that scandalizes the dutiful older brother. The Sower (Mark 4) describes how the same seed produces different results depending on the soil. A reliable rule: each parable typically makes one main point, and the surprise or reversal is usually where that point lives. Ask what the original listeners would have found shocking, and you are close to the meaning.

His core teachings cluster around the kingdom of God. The Gospels also present Jesus making striking claims about his own identity, and these deserve direct attention. In John's Gospel he uses a series of "I am" sayings — the bread of life, the light of the world, the good shepherd, the resurrection and the life — each echoing the divine name revealed to Moses. His miracles function the same way: the text presents healings, nature miracles, and exorcisms not as random wonders but as signs displaying the kingdom's arrival, with each Gospel selecting which signs to include. His conflicts with religious leaders, meanwhile, reveal what the text presents as his central controversy: not whether God matters, but what God is like and who belongs at his table. Reading these claims in context keeps you from reducing Jesus to either a mere moralist or an abstract doctrine — the Gospels present a person whose words and works make a unified claim that his first followers found impossible to ignore. The Sermon on the Mount (Matthew 5–7) presents kingdom ethics: inner righteousness over mere rule-keeping, love of enemies, trust in the Father's provision, and the Golden Rule. His teaching on prayer gives the Lord's Prayer as a model of simple, God-centered petition. His teaching on wealth warns that divided loyalty between God and money is impossible. And his summary of the law — love God wholly, love neighbor as self — gathers the entire Old Testament moral vision, as the text presents it, into two commands.

Two pitfalls need care. First, sentimentalizing Jesus into only a gentle teacher: the Gospels present both tenderness toward the broken and sharp confrontation with hypocrisy and injustice — read the woes against the Pharisees in Matthew 23 alongside the welcome of children in Mark 10. Second, decoding every parable detail as a hidden symbol: the Prodigal Son's ring and robe are story texture, not a code where each item equals a doctrine. Let the story's main reversal carry the meaning.

Practice: Read the parable of the Good Samaritan (Luke 10:25–37) twice. First, write down the one main point as you understand it. Second, list everything in the story that would have surprised a first-century Jewish listener — the priest and Levite passing by, the Samaritan as hero, the open-ended expense. Third, write two sentences applying the parable's point to one concrete relationship in your life this month. Compare your main point with the surprise list: does your summary account for what is most shocking?
