This lesson teaches three themes that run through the entire Bible and hold its diverse books together: covenant, grace, and the kingdom. Grasping these gives you a framework for connecting passages that otherwise seem unrelated.

Covenant is the Bible's word for a binding relationship initiated by God. The text presents a sequence of them: with Noah (creation preserved), with Abraham (a people and a blessing for the nations), with Israel at Sinai (the law as the terms of national life with God), with David (an everlasting dynasty), and finally the new covenant promised by Jeremiah and presented in the New Testament as established through Jesus. Each covenant has promises, obligations, and signs — circumcision, the Sabbath, the shared meal. Seeing this pattern transforms scattered stories into chapters of one relationship history. When the prophets accuse Israel of breaking covenant, for instance, they are invoking the specific Sinai terms from Lesson 2, not inventing new complaints.

Grace is the theme of God's unmerited favor. The text presents grace not as a New Testament invention but as the character of God from the beginning: he clothes Adam and Eve after the fall, chooses Abraham without stated merit, and delivers Israel from Egypt before giving the law — rescue precedes requirements. Christians hold that this pattern reaches its fullest expression in the New Testament's presentation of forgiveness and new life through Christ as a gift rather than wages. A helpful test case is the parable of the workers in the vineyard (Matthew 20): the landowner's generosity offends the workers' sense of fairness, which is precisely the text's point about grace — it does not follow human accounting.

The kingdom is the Bible's vision of God's reign. In the Old Testament it appears as God's rule over creation, focused through Israel's monarchy and promised in fullness through David's line. Jesus's central message, as the Gospels present it, is that the kingdom of God has drawn near. Christians have classically described this as 'already but not yet': the text presents the kingdom as inaugurated in Jesus's ministry, death, and resurrection, yet awaiting its consummation in the renewed creation of Revelation. This tension explains why the New Testament speaks of both present victory and ongoing suffering.

These three themes converge in the New Testament's presentation of Jesus, which is why they repay study together. The text presents Jesus as establishing the new covenant Jeremiah promised — at the Last Supper he speaks of the cup as the new covenant in his blood. It presents him as the embodiment of grace: John's Gospel says he came full of grace and truth, and the narratives show him welcoming precisely the people religious respectability excluded. And it presents him as the bringer of the kingdom, announcing its arrival, demonstrating it in healings the text presents as signs of the reign breaking in, and promising its completion. Seeing the convergence protects you from one-theme reading — grace without kingdom becomes mere comfort, kingdom without grace becomes mere demand, and covenant without either becomes mere contract. The themes interpret each other: covenant tells you grace has a shape, grace tells you covenant is gift before it is task, and kingdom tells you both are heading somewhere.

Two pitfalls cluster around these themes. First, pitting grace against obedience as enemies: the text presents them as partners — grace initiates the relationship, and obedience is the relationship's lived shape, as James insists when he writes that faith without works is dead. Second, reducing 'kingdom' to either a purely future heaven or a purely political program. The text presents it as God's comprehensive reign breaking into the present and completed in the future; either half alone distorts the picture.

Reflection: Choose one theme — covenant, grace, or kingdom — and trace it through three passages, one from the Old Testament and two from the New (use cross-references or a concordance). Write a paragraph describing how the theme develops across the three texts. Note one way the theme challenges a simplified version of it you may have held before this lesson.
