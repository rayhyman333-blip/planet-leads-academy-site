This lesson teaches how to build a sustainable practice of prayer and devotion — the personal side of Bible study. Information without transformation leaves the reader unchanged, and Christians have historically treated prayer and Scripture reading as two halves of one conversation: God speaks in the text, and the reader responds in prayer.

Start with a realistic shape. A devotional time needs only three elements: reading, reflection, and prayer. Read a short passage slowly — ten to twenty verses is plenty. Reflect by asking two questions: what does this show me about God, and what does this show me about myself? Then pray in response to what you read rather than switching to an unrelated list. If the psalm speaks of God's shelter, pray your actual anxieties into that image. Fifteen focused minutes practiced daily will form you more than occasional two-hour sessions that collapse within a week.

The text itself models many forms of prayer. The Lord's Prayer (Matthew 6:9–13) gives a pattern: honor God's name, seek his kingdom, ask for daily needs, confess faults, request guidance. The Psalms model lament — honest complaint brought to God, as in Psalm 13's 'how long, O Lord?' — which the text presents as legitimate prayer, not weak faith. Paul's letters model intercession and thanksgiving for others. And the Bible's own prayers can be prayed back: reading a psalm aloud slowly, personalizing its pronouns, is an ancient practice Christians call praying the Scriptures.

Two rhythms keep devotion alive over months. First, keep a prayer journal: date your entries, write requests and reflections briefly, and periodically review past pages. The journal in your materials provides the template. Reviewing teaches you what God has done and what patterns recur in your own heart — many readers are surprised how their anxieties rhyme month to month. Second, practice silence. After reading and praying, sit quietly for two or three minutes. The text presents God as speaking in a gentle whisper as well as in thunder; beginners often discover that stillness, not more words, is what their practice was missing.

Two further practices deepen prayer beyond the daily quiet time. First, pray with others. The text presents prayer as both solitary — Jesus withdrawing to lonely places — and communal, from Israel's psalms sung together to the early church praying in one accord. A weekly rhythm of praying aloud with even one trusted person breaks the isolation in which doubts grow and gives you words when your own run dry. Second, consider fasting as prayer's companion: the text presents fasting not as a technique for manipulating God but as a way of sharpening hunger for him, setting aside a good gift for a season to say that God matters more. Start small — one skipped meal with prayer in its place — and keep it private rather than performative, as Jesus's own teaching on the subject instructs. Both practices stretch the devotional life beyond the notebook into the body and the community, where much formation actually happens.

Two pitfalls deserve honesty. First, treating devotion as a performance to maintain a streak: missed days are not moral failures, and guilt-driven reading rarely nourishes. If you miss a morning, simply begin again the next day — the text presents God's mercies as new every morning, and your practice can be too. Second, using prayer only as a wish list. Petition is biblical and good, but a devotional life of only asking never matures into adoration, confession, and listening. Check your recent prayers: when did you last spend a whole session on thanksgiving or silence?

Practice: Using the prayer journal template, complete one full devotional session today: read Psalm 23 slowly twice, write three sentences on what it shows you about God and two on what it shows you about yourself, then write a short prayer responding to the psalm in your own words. End with two minutes of silence. Date the entry. Repeat the same shape tomorrow with a different psalm, and notice what changes on the second day.
