This lesson teaches you to adjust your reading strategy to the Bible's literary genres. A psalm is not making the same kind of claim as a Pauline argument, and much confusion comes from reading every text with the same expectations. Four genres cover most of what you will meet: narrative, poetry, letters, and prophecy.

Narrative — the stories of Genesis, the Gospels, Acts — teaches mainly through plot and character rather than direct statements. The key question is what the narrator wants you to see: pay attention to dialogue, repeated patterns, and the story's resolution. When Abraham is asked to offer Isaac (Genesis 22), the text presents the event's meaning through the angel's final speech about blessing, not through a moral printed at the end. Read narratives for the theological point the story as a whole makes, and be cautious about treating every character's behavior as exemplary — the text presents Jacob's deception and David's adultery as failures inside the story, not models.

Poetry — Psalms, much of the prophets, Song of Songs — works through imagery, emotion, and parallelism rather than linear argument. Hebrew poetry typically pairs lines that echo, contrast, or extend each other: 'The heavens declare the glory of God; the skies proclaim the work of his hands' (Psalm 19:1) says one thing in two images. Read poetry slowly, let metaphors do their work, and do not press figures of speech into literal claims — when the psalmist says God hides him under his wings, the text presents a metaphor of protection, not ornithology. Poetry also gives you language for prayer, which connects directly to Lesson 7.

Letters — the epistles — are situational correspondence. Always ask: who wrote to whom, about what problem, and with what desired response? Paul's command about head coverings in 1 Corinthians 11, for example, addresses a specific congregation's confusion about worship customs; readers disagree on how far the principle extends, but all careful readers start with Corinth's situation. Trace the author's argument paragraph by paragraph — words like 'therefore' signal that a conclusion is being drawn from what came before, and missing the connection mangles the meaning.

Prophecy — Isaiah, Jeremiah, the minor prophets — combines warning, comfort, and future hope, usually in poetic form. The text presents prophets as covenant prosecutors and comforters speaking to their own generation first; their oracles against ancient nations addressed real political crises. Read the historical setting before asking about later fulfillments, and let the prophet's own metaphors set the terms rather than importing modern images.

Two more genres appear often enough to name. Old Testament law — the commandments and regulations of Exodus through Deuteronomy — addressed Israel as a covenant nation, blending moral principles the text presents as enduring with civil and ceremonial rules tied to that era; ask what each law taught about God's character and about loving one's neighbor, not only what to copy literally. Apocalyptic — Daniel and Revelation — uses vivid symbolic visions to comfort the oppressed with the assurance that history is heading toward God's justice; read its images as evocative symbols drawn from earlier Scripture rather than as newspaper predictions. With six genres in your toolkit — narrative, poetry, letters, prophecy, law, and apocalyptic — you can now name what kind of text is in front of you before deciding how to read it, which is half the battle.

Two pitfalls span all genres. First, genre blindness: treating a proverb ('train up a child in the way he should go') as an ironclad promise, or a parable as a news report. Proverbs state general wisdom, not guarantees — the text presents them as observations about how life usually works. Second, flattening everything to commands: much of the Bible describes, laments, celebrates, or argues before it ever instructs, and skipping to 'what should I do?' misses what the text is actually doing.

Practice: Take the genre study guide worksheet from your materials. Choose one short text from each genre — for example, Genesis 22 (narrative), Psalm 23 (poetry), Philippians 4:4–9 (letter), Isaiah 40:1–11 (prophecy). For each, write: (1) the genre, (2) one feature of that genre you can see in the text, (3) one sentence on how the genre shapes what the passage means. Finish by writing which genre you find hardest to read well, and one strategy from this lesson you will use with it.
