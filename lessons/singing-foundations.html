Find your voice and sing with confidence, one steady breath at a time.

Lesson 1: Breathing and Posture for Singing

This lesson teaches you how to breathe and stand for singing. Everything later in this course - holding long notes, singing in tune, shaping clear words - builds directly on the physical habits you set up today. Good breathing and posture prevent the two problems that stop most beginners: running out of air halfway through a phrase, and tension in the neck and shoulders that makes the voice sound tight and tired. Give this lesson a full practice session on its own. It may feel like you are not really singing yet, but this foundation work pays off every single time you open your mouth to sing.

Start with posture. Stand with your feet about shoulder-width apart, your weight balanced evenly between both feet, and your knees soft - not locked straight and not bent deeply. Let your arms hang relaxed at your sides. Imagine a gentle thread pulling the top of your head upward, lengthening your spine, while your shoulders stay down and loose. Your chin should be level, neither tucked down toward your chest nor lifted high toward the ceiling. Your chest stays open and lifted, but not puffed out or stiff. Sit tall in the same way if you prefer to practice sitting: both feet flat on the floor, back straight but not rigid, arms relaxed. Good posture is not a military pose - it is a balanced, easy stance that lets your breath move freely.

Now the breath. Place one hand on your belly, just below your ribs, and one hand on your chest. Breathe in slowly through your nose and notice which hand moves: for singing, the lower hand should move outward while the upper hand stays almost still. This is low breathing, sometimes called belly breathing, and it gives you the steady air supply singing needs. Your belly expands as you breathe in, and it draws gently back in as you breathe out. Practice this for two minutes: breathe in for four slow counts, feeling the belly expand, then breathe out for four slow counts on a soft "sss" sound, feeling the belly draw in steadily. The hiss should be even and unbroken - if it sputters or fades, you are letting the air out in bursts instead of a steady stream.

The next skill is managing the air as you sing. Singing uses far less air than most beginners expect; the goal is a slow, controlled release, not a big blast. Try this: breathe in low for four counts, then sing a single comfortable note on "ah" for as long as you can, keeping the sound steady and even. Count how many seconds you last, and repeat the exercise five times, trying to keep the tone from wobbling or fading at the end. Most beginners find the note gets shaky near the end - that is the moment the breath support runs out, and it improves with practice. The trick is to start the note gently, with no push or burst, and to keep the belly engaged as the note goes on, as if you were slowly squeezing air out of a tube.

Time for drills. First, the belly-breath check: five slow breaths with hands on belly and chest, watching the lower hand move and the upper hand stay still. Second, the hiss exercise: breathe in for four counts, hiss out on "sss" for eight counts, keeping the sound perfectly even - repeat six times, then try hissing for twelve counts. Third, the long-tone exercise: breathe in low, sing one comfortable note on "ah" for as long as you can, five times, writing down how many seconds each attempt lasts so you can watch the number grow over the coming weeks.

Two common mistakes to watch for. The first is chest breathing: the shoulders rise and the chest heaves on every breath, which gives you a shallow, shaky air supply and a tight throat. If your shoulders move when you breathe in, stop and reset with your hands on your belly until the lower hand is doing the work. The second is pushing: forcing a big burst of air at the start of a note, which makes the sound blast and then fade. Singing is a steady stream, not a sneeze - start each note gently and let the breath flow out evenly.

Practice: Stand in your singing posture and do the belly-breath check five times. Then do the hiss exercise six times, aiming for eight even counts each time. Finish with five long tones on "ah," one comfortable note each, writing down the length of each one. Before every exercise, run the checklist: feet balanced, knees soft, shoulders down, chin level, belly breathing. End the session by singing a simple, familiar tune once through, standing tall and breathing low, just to feel the difference good posture makes.

Lesson 2: Finding Your Range

This lesson teaches you to find your vocal range: the span of notes you can sing comfortably, from your lowest easy note to your highest easy note. Knowing your range is practical, not a label - it tells you which songs will sit comfortably for your voice, which notes to avoid forcing, and where your voice naturally feels at home. By the end of this lesson you will know your comfortable range and you will have a simple map of it to use when choosing songs.

First, find your lowest comfortable note. Sit or stand in your singing posture, breathe in low, and hum gently on "mmm" starting on a note that feels easy and relaxed - for most people this is somewhere in the middle of their speaking voice. Now slide the hum slowly downward, one small step at a time, like walking down a staircase. Keep going until the sound starts to feel gravelly, weak, or strained, then step back up one note - that note is the bottom of your comfortable range. Write it down. Do not push below it: the lowest notes are not a contest, and forcing them only adds tension. Repeat the slide three times to check that you land on the same bottom note each time.

Now find your highest comfortable note. Start again on an easy middle note and slide the hum slowly upward, one small step at a time. The voice will feel lighter as you climb, and at some point the sound will start to feel thin, tight, or hard to control - step back down one note, and that is the top of your comfortable range for now. Write it down beside the low note. Your range today is simply the distance between those two notes, and it is a starting point, not a fixed limit: ranges grow slowly over months of gentle practice, and they grow by expanding a little at a time, never by forcing.

Between the bottom and the top, your voice has different feels, often called registers. The lower part of your range usually feels warm and speech-like - this is your chest voice, the sound you use when you talk. The upper part usually feels lighter and floatier - this is your head voice, the sound that feels as if it resonates higher up. In the middle there is a passage area, sometimes called the break, where the voice wants to shift from one feel to the other and the sound can wobble or crack. That wobble is normal and every singer has it. The goal of this lesson is not to erase the break but to know where it is: slide slowly through it on a hum, notice where the shift happens, and write that spot down too.

Time for drills. First, the downward slide: hum from an easy middle note down to your lowest comfortable note, three times, checking that you stop at the same note. Second, the upward slide: hum from the same middle note up to your highest comfortable note, three times, stopping at the same top note. Third, the siren: slide your voice slowly up and down across your whole range like a siren, on a gentle "oo" sound, three times, moving smoothly through the passage area without stopping or jumping. The siren teaches your voice to glide through the break instead of cracking at it.

Choosing songs by range is the practical payoff. Before you sing any song, check its lowest and highest notes against your range map. If the song's highest note sits above your comfortable top, the song will feel like a strain every time you reach it - choose a lower key, or choose a different song. If the song sits entirely in the middle of your range, it will feel easy and sound relaxed. As a rule for this course, pick songs whose notes stay inside your comfortable range with at least one note of room at the top and the bottom, so you never have to sing at the very edge of what you can do.

Two common mistakes. The first is confusing your range with your speaking voice: the notes you speak on are usually in the lower-middle of your range, and many beginners assume their singing range ends where their speaking voice ends. It does not - the hum slides in this lesson will show you notes above

and below your speaking pitch. The second is forcing the top: pushing hard to reach a high note, which makes the throat tight and the note sharp and thin. High notes come from steady breath and a relaxed throat, not from effort - if a note feels like work, you are above your comfortable range for now.

Practice: Find your lowest and highest comfortable notes using the hum slides, and write both down on the range worksheet, with the passage area marked. Then sing the siren exercise three times on "oo," sliding smoothly across your whole range. Finish by singing a simple, familiar tune and checking whether its notes sit inside your range - write down the highest and lowest notes of the tune and compare them with your map.

Lesson 3: Pitch and Ear Training

This lesson teaches you to match pitch accurately and to hear musical distances - intervals - by ear. Pitch is the foundation of singing in tune: if you can hear a note clearly and reproduce it with your voice, you can sing any melody. Ear training is simply the habit of listening carefully before you sing, and it improves faster than most beginners expect. By the end of this lesson you will match single notes reliably and recognize the most common intervals by ear.

Start with single-note matching. Play a note on a piano, keyboard, or tuning app - choose a note in the comfortable middle of your range. Listen to it for a few seconds, then hum the same note on "mmm." Listen to yourself: does your hum sit exactly on the played note, or does it drift above or below it? If you are unsure, play the note again while you hum, and listen for the beating or wobbling sound that happens when two notes are close but not identical - when the wobble disappears, the notes match. Practice this with ten different notes, one at a time, taking your time on each. The goal is not speed but accuracy: one matched note is worth more than ten guessed ones.

Now learn to hear intervals, the distance between two notes. The smallest step is a half step, the move from any note to its nearest neighbor. Two half steps make a whole step. The most useful intervals to learn first are the ones that appear constantly in melodies: the major second (a whole step up), the minor third (three half steps), the major third (four half steps), the perfect fourth (five half steps), the perfect fifth (seven half steps), and the octave (twelve half steps, the same note higher). Play each interval on the keyboard - lower note first, then upper note - and sing both notes back. Give each interval a mental picture: the perfect fifth sounds open and strong, like the start of a fanfare; the major third sounds bright and cheerful; the minor third sounds softer and sadder.

The echo exercise is the fastest way to build your ear. Play a short pattern of three notes on the keyboard, listen carefully, then sing the pattern back without the keyboard. Start with simple patterns that move by step - up one, down one, up one - and gradually add small jumps. Do twenty echo patterns per session, and keep a tally of how many you get exactly right. When you miss one, play it again slowly, sing each note separately, and then sing the whole pattern again. The echo exercise works because it forces you to hold a sound in your mind and reproduce it - exactly what singing a melody requires.

Add the slide-and-match drill for notes that feel hard to reach. If a target note is above your comfortable starting point, do not jump straight to it: slide your voice slowly upward from a note you can match, and stop when you hear the slide meet the target note. The slide gives your ear time to compare the moving pitch with the target, and it teaches your voice to find notes by listening rather than by guessing. Practice five slide-and-match exercises per session, choosing target notes a little above and a little below your starting note each time.

Two common mistakes. The first is singing before listening: beginners hear a note and immediately blurt out a guess, and the guess is usually wrong. Build the habit of a silent beat - hear the note, hold it in your mind for a second, then sing. That one-second pause doubles your accuracy. The second is drifting flat on long notes: the pitch sags as the breath runs out. Keep the breath support engaged through the whole note, and imagine the note staying lifted, like a balloon you are holding up, rather than letting it sink.

Time for drills. First, single-note matching: ten notes, played then hummed, checking for the wobble that signals a mismatch. Second, interval singing: play and sing each of the six intervals above, five times each, naming the interval as you sing it. Third, the echo exercise: twenty three-note patterns, sung back from memory, with a tally of exact matches.

Practice: Do ten single-note matches, then the six intervals five times each, then twenty echo patterns, writing down your score. Finish by singing a simple, familiar tune slowly, stopping on each note to check whether it matches the melody you hear in your head - if a note feels wrong, play it on the keyboard and match it before moving on.

Lesson 4: Warming Up Safely

This lesson teaches you to warm up safely: gentle exercises that prepare your voice for singing without strain. A warm-up is not a performance - it is a slow, easy stretch for the voice, like walking before running. Singers who warm up regularly find their voices respond faster, stay in tune more easily, and feel less tired after practice. By the end of this lesson you will have a ten-minute warm-up routine you can use before every practice session and every song.

Start with the body, not the voice. Roll your shoulders slowly backward five times, then forward five times, letting them drop down and away from your ears. Turn your head gently from side to side, then tilt it slowly toward each shoulder, holding each stretch for a slow count of five - never force the stretch, and never roll your head in a full circle. Shake your hands out, then do five slow belly breaths from Lesson 1, feeling the lower hand move while the shoulders stay still. This two-minute body warm-up removes the tension that makes the voice sound tight, and it signals to your brain that singing time has started.

Now the voice, starting with the gentlest sound of all: the hum. Hum on "mmm" at a comfortable middle pitch for ten seconds, feeling a light buzzing in your lips and face. Slide the hum slowly up a few notes and back down, like a small wave, five times. Humming is gentle because the lips are closed and the sound is soft - it wakes the voice up without asking it to work hard. Next, lip trills: blow air through loosely closed lips so they flutter, like a horse, while sliding the pitch slowly up and down. Lip trills are one of the safest warm-ups because the fluttering lips keep the airflow steady and the throat relaxed. Do lip trills for two minutes, sliding through the middle of your range.

Add sirens on "oo" and "ee." A siren is a slow slide from the bottom of your comfortable range to the top and back down again, taking about ten seconds each way. Sing the siren on "oo" three times, then on "ee" three times, keeping the volume soft and the slide smooth - no jumps, no pushing, no holding your breath. The siren stretches the voice gently across its whole range and smooths the passage area where the voice shifts between its lower and upper feels. If the siren cracks or wobbles at the passage area, slow it down and make it softer - the goal is a smooth glide, not a perfect sound.

Finish the warm-up with gentle scales. Sing a five-note pattern - up five notes and back down - on "ah," starting on a comfortable middle note, then move the whole pattern up one step and repeat, climbing slowly through the middle of your range. Keep the volume moderate and the sound easy; if a note feels tight, stop climbing and come back down. Do five of these patterns, then sing the same five-note pattern on "ee" and on "oo," noticing how each vowel feels different in your mouth. End the warm-up with one minute of silence - no talking, no singing - letting the voice settle before you begin your practice.

Two common mistakes. The first is warming up too hard: beginners treat the warm-up like a performance, singing loudly and pushing for high notes, which tires the voice before the real practice begins. A warm-up should feel easy - if you are working hard, you are doing it wrong. The second is skipping the warm-up entirely on days when time is short. Even three minutes of humming and lip trills is better than none, and it protects your voice from the strain of starting cold. If your voice feels tired, hoarse, or sore, stop singing and rest it - rest is part of good practice, not a failure of it.

Time for drills. First, the body warm-up: shoulder rolls, gentle head turns, and five belly breaths, two minutes. Second, hums and lip trills: five hum slides and two minutes of lip trills, all soft. Third, sirens

and scales: three sirens on "oo," three on "ee," and five five-note scale patterns on "ah," all at a comfortable volume.

Practice: Do the full ten-minute warm-up exactly as written - body, hums, lip trills, sirens, scales - before your next three practice sessions. Write down how your voice feels after the warm-up each time, using one honest sentence, and notice whether the feeling improves across the three days.

Lesson 5: Vowels and Diction - Singing Words Clearly

This lesson teaches you to shape vowels and pronounce words clearly while singing. In speech, vowels carry the sound and consonants carry the meaning; in singing, the same is true, but the balance shifts - vowels need to stay open and ringing so the note can sustain, while consonants need to be quick and crisp so the words stay understandable. By the end of this lesson you will sing the five main vowels with an open, relaxed mouth, and pronounce consonants without tightening your jaw.

Start with the five vowels: ah, eh, ee, oh, oo. Sing each one on a single comfortable note, holding it for four slow counts, and notice the shape of your mouth. For "ah," the mouth opens wide and tall, the tongue lies flat, and the jaw drops loosely. For "eh," the mouth opens halfway, the tongue lifts slightly. For "ee," the mouth is narrower, the lips spread gently, and the tongue rises toward the roof of the mouth. For "oh," the lips round into a soft circle and the mouth opens tall. For "oo," the lips round more tightly and the mouth opens smaller. Practice singing all five vowels in a row on the same note, slowly, feeling the mouth reshape for each one. The sound should stay steady and free - if a vowel sounds pinched or thin, the mouth is probably too closed or the jaw is tight.

Now the vowel ladder. Sing the five vowels in order - ah, eh, ee, oh, oo - on a single note, one vowel per beat, four times through. Then sing the ladder on a five-note scale, one vowel per note, climbing and descending. The ladder teaches your mouth to move cleanly between vowel shapes without disturbing the tone. A common trouble spot is the shift from "ee" to "oh": the mouth has to open and round quickly, and beginners often keep the "ee" shape too long, which makes the "oh" sound muddy. Slow the ladder down at that transition until the shift is clean, then speed it back up.

Consonants are the other half of diction. Unlike vowels, which you sustain, consonants are quick events: they start or end a sound and then get out of the way. Practice the consonant drill: sing "mah, nah, lah, dah, bah" on a single note, one syllable per beat, feeling each consonant land crisply at the front of the mouth and then release immediately into the vowel. The tongue and lips do the consonant work - the jaw stays relaxed and does not clamp down. Try the same drill with "tah, kah, sah, zah," noticing how each consonant uses a different part of the mouth. Then sing a line of a familiar song slowly, exaggerating the consonants slightly - not shouting them, just making them clear - and listen for whether every word is understandable.

The jaw deserves special attention because it is where most diction tension lives. Place your fingertips lightly on your jaw joints, just in front of your ears, and sing a line of a song: if you feel the jaw clenching or pushing forward, stop and let it hang loose. A useful check is the two-finger test: you should be able to fit two fingers stacked vertically between your upper and lower teeth while singing "ah" - if you cannot, your jaw is too closed. Practice singing "ah" with the jaw dropped and loose, and notice how much bigger and freer the sound becomes. Relaxed jaw, open mouth, clear consonants: those three habits are the whole of diction.

Two common mistakes. The first is mumbling: vowels that are too closed and consonants that are too soft, so the words blur together and the listener cannot follow the song. If you cannot understand your own words on a recording, the diction needs work - open the vowels and sharpen the consonants. The second is over-enunciating: biting off every consonant so hard that the singing sounds like spelling, with the flow broken by constant interruptions. Consonants should be clear but quick, like a tap on the shoulder, not a shove.

Time for drills. First, the vowel ladder: all five vowels on one note, then on a five-note scale, four times through. Second, the consonant drill: "mah, nah, lah, dah, bah" and "tah, kah, sah, zah" on a single note, four times each. Third, the jaw check: sing a line of a song with fingertips on your jaw joints, stopping and resetting whenever you feel tension.

Practice: Sing the vowel ladder four times, then the consonant drills four times each, then sing one verse of a familiar song slowly, focusing only on diction - open vowels, quick consonants, relaxed jaw. Record it and listen back, writing down one word that was unclear and one vowel that sounded pinched, and repeat that line five times with the fixes in place.

Lesson 6: Singing a Simple Song from Start to Finish

This lesson teaches you to sing a complete song from start to finish: learning it in sections, mapping your breaths, and putting the sections together into a performance you can be proud of. Singing a whole song is a milestone - it is the moment all the separate skills in this course become music. By the end of this lesson you will sing a simple song cleanly, in tune, with clear words and planned breathing.

Choose a simple song that fits your range. Pick one with a narrow span of notes - no big jumps, no notes at the very edge of your range - and with words you understand and like. A short folk song, a simple hymn, or a well-known children's melody all work well. Before you sing it, learn the melody by ear: play it on the keyboard or listen to it carefully, humming along until you can hum the whole tune without help. Then learn the words separately: speak them aloud slowly, as if you were reading a poem, noticing where the natural pauses fall. Melody and words learned separately are much easier to combine than melody and words learned together.

Now build the song in sections. Divide it into phrases - short musical sentences, usually two to four measures long - and learn one phrase at a time. Sing the first phrase slowly, checking each note against the melody you know, and repeat it until it is comfortable. Then learn the second phrase the same way. Then sing phrases one and two together. Add one phrase at a time until the whole song is joined. This section-by-section method works on every song you will ever learn, and it stops the most common practice trap: singing the whole song badly from the start and never fixing the weak spots.

Next, map your breaths. A breath map is simply a plan for where you breathe: mark a breath mark in the words at the end of each phrase, and practice breathing exactly there - nowhere else. Breathe in low and quickly at each mark, and sing through the whole phrase on one breath. If a phrase is too long for one breath, split it at a natural pause in the words and add a breath there. Practice the song with the breath map three times, breathing only at the marks. Planned breathing makes the singing sound calm and controlled; random gasps in the middle of a phrase make it sound panicked. Write the breath marks on your song worksheet so you can see them every time you practice.

Add phrasing and dynamics. A phrase is a musical sentence, and like a spoken sentence it has a shape: it begins gently, grows slightly toward the middle, and settles at the end. Sing each phrase with that shape - start at a comfortable medium volume, let the sound grow a little toward the most important word in the phrase, and ease back as the phrase ends. The most important word is usually the word that carries the meaning of the line - sing it with a touch more warmth and clarity, and let the other words support it. Practice the song once with exaggerated phrasing - big swells and clear phrase endings - then once with natural phrasing, and notice how the exaggerated version teaches your voice the shape that the natural version keeps.

Two common mistakes. The first is rushing the tempo: beginners speed up whenever the easy part arrives, and the song falls apart at the hard part. Choose a tempo slow enough for the hardest phrase, and keep it steady from the first note to the last - use a gentle tap of your foot to hold the beat. The second is stopping at every mistake and restarting from the beginning: this trains you to sing the opening brilliantly and the ending never. When you stumble, keep going, note the bad spot, and drill that spot alone afterward.

Time for drills. First, the phrase builder: learn the song one phrase at a time, singing each phrase three times cleanly before adding the next. Second, the breath-map run: sing the whole song breathing only

at the marks, three times through. Third, the phrasing run: sing the song once with exaggerated phrasing and once naturally, recording both and listening for the shape of each phrase.

Practice: Choose a simple song inside your range and learn it phrase by phrase until you can sing it through without stopping. Write its breath map on the song worksheet, then sing the whole song four times: twice slowly with the breath map, once at a comfortable tempo with phrasing, and once recorded. Listen back to the recording and write down the one phrase that needs the most work, then drill that phrase ten times before you finish.

Lesson 7: Harmony Basics - Singing with Others

This lesson teaches you harmony basics: singing a second part above or below a melody while someone else sings the main tune. Harmony is what makes group singing sound rich - two voices moving together, each on its own line, blending into a sound neither could make alone. By the end of this lesson you will sing a simple harmony part, hold it against the melody, and blend your voice with a partner.

Start by understanding what harmony is. A melody is a single line of notes. A harmony part is a second line that moves along with the melody, usually a third or a sixth above or below it - those are the intervals you learned in Lesson 3, and they sound warm and consonant together. When two voices sing a third apart, they move in parallel, like two friends walking side by side: when the melody goes up one step, the harmony goes up one step too. The simplest harmony to learn is the third above the melody: take any melody, and for each note, sing the note a third higher. Practice this on a simple tune you know well, singing the harmony line alone first, without the melody, until it feels like a tune in its own right.

The hard part of harmony is holding your line. When another voice sings the melody beside you, your ear wants to slide onto their notes - especially when the two lines come close together. The skill is to listen to the melody without joining it. Practice with a partner, or with a recording of the melody playing quietly: sing your harmony line while the melody plays, and if you slip onto the melody, stop, find your starting note again, and rejoin. A useful trick is to sing your harmony line while playing the melody on the keyboard at the same time - the keyboard holds the melody steady, and you learn to trust your own line against it. Do this for five minutes a day and the slipping stops surprisingly fast.

Blending is the second skill. Two voices blend when they match in volume, vowel shape, and timing: neither voice sticks out, and the two sounds melt into one. Practice blending by singing the same note as your partner on "ah," matching their volume exactly - not louder, not softer - and matching the shape of the vowel. Then sing a simple scale together in unison, listening for the moment the two voices fuse into a single sound. When you can blend in unison, try blending in harmony: sing the third above while your partner sings the melody, keeping your volume equal and your vowels matched. If one voice is much louder, the harmony disappears under it - the quieter voice is the one to bring up, gently, until the two are balanced.

Learn two harmony patterns you can use anywhere. The first is the third above: sing every melody note a third higher, which gives a bright, cheerful harmony. The second is the third below: sing every melody note a third lower, which gives a warmer, darker harmony. Practice both on the same simple tune, one after the other, and notice how different the song feels with each. Then try a call-and-response pattern: your partner sings a phrase, you echo it back, then you both sing the next phrase together in harmony. Call and response teaches you to listen and answer, which is the heart of singing with others.

Two common mistakes. The first is singing the harmony too loudly: beginners push their part to prove they are holding it, and the balance breaks. Harmony is a team sound - your part should be exactly as loud as the melody, no more. The second is watching the other singer's mouth instead of listening: your eyes cannot tell you the pitch. Close your eyes, listen to your own line inside your head, and trust your ear.

Time for drills. First, the harmony line alone: sing the third-above harmony to a simple tune by yourself, three times, until it feels like its own melody. Second, the hold-your-line drill: sing the harmony while the melody plays on the keyboard, five minutes, restarting whenever you slip onto the melody. Third, the blend exercise: sing a single note in unison with a partner or a recording, matching volume and vowel, then sing a scale together in unison, then in thirds.

Practice: Learn the third-above harmony to a simple song you know, singing the harmony line alone three times. Then sing it with the melody playing on the keyboard or sung by a partner, three times through, keeping your volume equal to the melody. Finish by singing the third-below harmony to the same song once, and write down which harmony felt easier to hold.

Lesson 8: Your Daily Practice Routine + What to Learn Next

This lesson builds your permanent daily practice routine and maps out where to go next. What separates singers who improve from singers who stall is not talent but the quality and consistency of their practice. This lesson gives you a repeatable session structure, a weekly plan, and a clear list of next steps, so you never again sit down wondering what to do.

The session structure has four blocks in about twenty minutes. Block one is warm-up, five minutes: the body warm-up, hums, lip trills, and sirens from Lesson 4, every single session without exception. Block two is technique, five minutes: rotate daily through pitch matching, interval singing, vowel ladders, and range slides, working on whatever is weakest. Block three is songs, ten minutes: your current song, worked phrase by phrase with the breath map, never just sung through on autopilot. Block four is fun, five minutes: sing anything you enjoy, even imperfectly, and end every session on a song you love. The order is fixed: warm-up, technique, songs, fun.

The weekly plan spreads the work. Practice five or six days a week rather than cramming: twenty focused minutes beats a two-hour weekend marathon because your brain consolidates the learning between sessions, while you sleep. Assign each day a small focus - Monday: pitch and ear training; Tuesday: vowels and diction; Wednesday: range slides and sirens; Thursday: harmony with a partner or recording; Friday: song work, recorded on your phone; weekend: one light session or rest. Keep the written practice log from your materials: date, minutes, what you worked on, and one honest note about what improved. Review the log every Sunday and set one specific goal for the coming week.

Where to go next: five directions. One, a second song in a different style - a slower ballad, where the challenge is sustaining long phrases and singing quietly with control. Two, a wider range: gentle slides that expand your comfortable range by one note at a time, over months, never by forcing. Three, a new time feel: learn to sing a song in 3/4 time, where the phrases breathe in threes instead of fours. Four, a harmony song with a partner: choose a song with a written harmony part and learn it together, trading melody and harmony between verses. Five, reading music: learn the treble clef and note values, so you can learn songs from written music instead of by ear alone. Learn these one at a time, one per few weeks, with the section method from Lesson 6.

Once a month, hold a review session instead of a normal practice. Sing through everything you have learned - your warm-up, your intervals, your vowels, and your songs - and record it all. Listen back with your practice log open and ask three questions: what is clearly better than last month, what has not moved at all, and what is the single weakest skill right now. Write the answers down, then set exactly one goal for the coming month, such as "match ten notes in a row without a miss" or "sing my song through with the breath map and no extra breaths." One goal keeps your practice focused; five goals means none of them happen. Date the goal in your log, and check it off the day you achieve it.

Two common mistakes. The first is practicing only what you already sing well: it feels good and improves nothing. Spend at least half of every session on the hardest thing, not the favorite thing. The second is the autopilot run-through: singing a song start to finish five times while thinking about dinner. If your mind wanders, stop, pick the weakest phrase, and drill it ten times - focused repetitions beat mindless ones by a wide margin. And remember the safety rule that runs through this whole course: if your voice feels tired, hoarse, or sore, stop and rest. A rested voice comes back stronger; a pushed voice does not.
