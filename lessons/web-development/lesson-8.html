This final lesson takes your site live: how hosting works, how to publish with a free hosting service, and how to verify your site on other devices. Then you will plan and build a capstone personal site, and get a map of where to go next. A site on your own computer is a draft; a published site is a portfolio piece you can share.

How hosting works: a hosting service stores your files on its servers and serves them to visitors at a public address. For the static sites you build in this course (HTML, CSS, and JavaScript with no server-side code), free static hosting is plentiful: you create an account with a hosting service, upload your files or connect a folder, and receive a public web address, usually a subdomain of the service. The typical flow: sign up, create a new site, drag your my-site folder into the browser upload area (or follow the service's upload steps), wait a minute, and open your new address on your phone to verify. Your pages must use relative links (about.html, styles.css — not file paths from your computer) so they work on any server; if you followed this course, they already do.

Before publishing, run the pre-flight checklist: every page has a <title> and the viewport meta tag; all images load (no broken icons); navigation links work from every page; the site looks right at phone width and desktop width; the contact form validates; there are no console errors (open developer tools and check). Fix everything locally first — debugging is ten times easier on your own machine than on a live address.

The capstone: a personal portfolio site of at least three pages — Home, Projects, Contact — built from everything in this course. Home: your name, a one-line summary, a photo or graphic with alt text, and navigation. Projects: at least three cards (flexbox grid from Lesson 4) describing things you built in this course, each with what it does and what you learned. Contact: your validated form from Lesson 7 plus a simple way to reach you. Requirements: shared stylesheet, semantic structure, responsive at phone and desktop widths, one JavaScript interaction beyond the form (theme toggle, reading-list widget, or tip calculator embedded as a project), zero console errors, and your own words throughout — no filler text anywhere. Publish it, open the public address on your phone, and send it to one person. That moment — someone else loading your site on their device — is the real graduation.

Where to go next depends on what excited you. If design thrilled you, study typography, color theory, and CSS animations and transitions. If interactivity thrilled you, deepen your JavaScript: arrays and objects in depth, fetching data from web services, and organizing larger scripts. If building thrilled you, learn a version-control workflow and how teams collaborate on code, then explore server-side programming so your forms can actually send email and store data. Whatever you choose, the method stays the same: small project, break into steps, test each step, read errors calmly. You have the complete foundation; the rest is practice.

Practice: Run the pre-flight checklist on your current my-site and fix every issue. Then build the capstone portfolio site to the requirements above — new folder, clean start, best work. Publish it with a free hosting service, verify the public address on your phone and one other device, and write a one-paragraph reflection: which lesson's skill do you reach for most, and what will you build next? Keep that paragraph with your project files — it is the first entry in your developer journal.
