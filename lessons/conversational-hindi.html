Full Lessons — Conversational Hindi

Full lesson text, Academy-built. This is the complete 8-lesson course text for this course. Prices and course links are on the course page.

Speak practical everyday Hindi from day one: Devanagari sounds, core grammar, and real conversations.

Lesson 1: Devanagari Sounds and Script Basics

This lesson teaches you to pronounce Hindi clearly from the start, and introduces the Devanagari script so you can recognize the letters you will see on signs, menus, and screens. Your goal in this lesson is not a perfect accent — it is clarity. If your vowels are clean and you can make the retroflex sounds, Hindi speakers will understand you even while you are still learning words. Every Hindi word in this course is written in transliteration — Roman letters — with an English translation, so you can speak from the first page. The Devanagari letters are shown alongside so you can start recognizing them, but you never need them to say a word.

Start with the vowels, which are pure and steady, unlike English vowels that glide. A sounds like the u in but: namaste (nah-mah-STAY, hello), kam (kahm, work). AA is the same sound held longer, like the a in father: naam (naahm, name), baat (baaht, talk). I sounds like the i in bit: din (din, day), dil (dil, heart). II is longer, like ee in see: neend (neend, sleep), jeena (JEE-nah, to live). U sounds like the oo in put: tum (toom, you), sun (soon, listen). UU is longer, like oo in food: door (door, far), bhool (bhool, mistake). E sounds like the ay in day: mera (MAY-rah, my), dekh (daykh, look). AI is a glide, like the i in bike: kaisa (KAY-sah, how), paisa (PAY-sah, money). O sounds like the o in go: mera dost (my friend), bol (bohl, speak). AU is a glide, like ow in cow: aur (owr, and/more), kaun (kown, who). The difference between short and long vowels matters: kam (work) versus kaam (work, with a long aa, also means work but spelled differently) — and din (day) versus deen (religion) are different words entirely. Practice each pair until your ear hears the length difference.

Now the consonants, which Devanagari organizes by where they are made in the mouth — a brilliantly logical system. The throat group (ka, kha, ga, gha): kaam (work), khaana (food), ghar (house), ghaas (grass). The palate group (cha, chha, ja, jha): chai (tea), chha (shade), jaana (to go), jhaad (broom). The retroflex group is the famous one: the tongue tip curls back to touch the roof of the mouth — ta, tha, da, dha, na with a dot underneath in transliteration. Practice: daal (lentils), thanda (cold), ladka (boy), bada (big). English speakers often say these as dental sounds; curling the tongue back even slightly makes a real difference. The dental group (ta, tha, da, dha, na) is made with the tongue on the teeth: tum (you), din (day), naam (name). The lip group (pa, pha, ba, bha, ma): paani (water), phal (fruit), baat (talk), bhaai (brother), mera (my). Then ya, ra, la, va, and the sibilants sha, sa, ha: shaam (evening), saal (year), haan (yes).

Three sound rules will save you constant confusion. First, the aspirated consonants: kha, gha, chha, tha, pha, bha are the same as ka, ga, cha, ta, pa, ba but with a strong puff of air. Hold your hand in front of your mouth: khaana (food) should blow air on your hand, kaam (work) should not. The difference matters: kaal means time or death depending on context, but khaal means skin. Second, the schwa: the short a at the end of a written word is usually dropped in speech. Namaste is written with a final a but said nah-mah-STAY, and Ram is written Raama but said Raam. Third, the nasal sounds: a dot above a vowel or the letters n and m make the vowel nasal — main (I) is said with the vowel through the nose, and gaan (to go) ends with a nasal glide.

The Devanagari script itself is simpler than it looks. It is written left to right, and each letter is a syllable: a consonant carries an inherent a sound unless a vowel sign changes it. The vowels have their own standalone letters and small marks (matras) that attach to consonants: ka becomes ki with the i mark, ku with the u mark, ke with the e mark. Consonants can join into combined letters (conjuncts) when two consonants meet with no vowel between them, as in namaste, where the s and t join. You do not need to write Devanagari to speak Hindi, but recognizing the letters helps you read signs, menus, and messages. Learn to recognize the ten most common letters first: ka, na, ma, ra, ta, ha, sa, pa, ba, ya — they appear in almost every word.

Here is a short dialogue using only sounds from this lesson. Read it aloud slowly:

Anita: Namaste! Aap ka naam kya hai? (nah-mah-STAY, aap kah naahm kyAH hay) — Hello! What is your name? Rahul: Namaste. Mera naam Rahul hai. Aap ka? (MAY-rah naahm RAH-hool hay, aap kah) — Hello. My name is Rahul. And yours? Anita: Mera naam Anita hai. Aap se milkar khushi hui. (MAY-rah naahm ah-NEE-tah hay, aap say mil-KAR KHOO-shee hoo-EE) — My name is Anita. Pleased to meet you. Rahul: Mujhe bhi. Aap Hindi bolte hain? (moo-JAY bhee, aap HIN-dee BOHL-tay hayn) — Me too. Do you speak Hindi? Anita: Haan, thoda. (haahn, THOH-dah) — Yes, a little.

Say the whole dialogue five times today; it is your first Hindi conversation.

Practice: Write out these ten words, mark the long vowels and retroflex consonants, and say each aloud three times: namaste, khaana, ghar, daal, paani, chai, ladka, thanda, mera, shaam. Next, pair up kam/kaam, din/deen, and sun/soon and say each pair until the short-versus-long difference is obvious. Finally, read the dialogue above aloud five times, then cover the pronunciation hints and read it again — you should be able to produce the sounds from the transliteration alone.

Lesson 2: Greetings, Introductions, and Survival Phrases

This lesson gives you your first real social toolkit: greeting people politely, introducing yourself, and surviving conversations when you do not understand something. These phrases are the highest-value sentences in the language because they unlock every interaction — ordering food, asking for help, meeting people. Learn them cold before you worry about grammar, because even a flawless sentence is useless if you cannot open and close a conversation gracefully. Hindi speakers are warm and encouraging with learners, and even a few Hindi sentences will change how people respond to you.

Start with greetings. Namaste (nah-mah-STAY) is the standard hello and goodbye, used any time of day and in any setting. Namaskar (nah-mah-SKAAR) is a slightly more formal version. Suprabhat (soo-prah-BHAAT) means good morning, shubh ratri (shoobh RAAT-ree) means good night, and phir milenge (phir mi-LEN-gay) means we will meet again — a warm goodbye. Alvida (ahl-vi-DAH) is a more formal farewell. For introductions: Mera naam... hai (My name is...). Aap ka naam kya hai? asks someone's name formally; Tumhara naam kya hai? is the informal version for friends and peers. Aap se milkar khushi hui (Pleased to meet you) and Mujhe bhi (Me too) complete the introduction. Kahan se hain aap? (Where are you from?) follows naturally.

Now the small-talk core. Aap kaise hain? (How are you? — formal) is the standard question; Tum kaise ho? is the informal version. Common answers: Main theek hoon (I am fine), Bahut achha (Very good), Sab theek hai (Everything is fine), Main thaka hua hoon (I am tired — masculine) or Main thaki hui hoon (I am tired — feminine). Aur aap? (And you?) keeps the conversation balanced. Kripya is please, dhanyavaad is thank you, bahut dhanyavaad is thank you very much, and koi baat nahi (no problem / it does not matter) is you are welcome. A frequent beginner mistake is answering Aap kaise hain? with a long life story; a short answer plus Aur aap? gives you twice the practice and sounds natural. Note the respectful word ji, added after a name or yes/no: Haan ji (Yes, respectfully), Rahul ji (Mr. Rahul) — it softens everything and shows good manners.

Survival phrases are your safety net — memorize these before anything else. Mujhe samajh nahi aaya (I did not understand). Kya aap dobara keh sakte hain? (Can you say it again?). Dheere boliye, kripya (Speak slowly, please). Kya aap angrezi bolte hain? (Do you speak English?). Maaf kijiye (Excuse me / Sorry). Mujhe madad chahiye (I need help). Iska matlab kya hai? (What does this mean?). Main thoda Hindi bolta hoon (I speak a little Hindi — masculine) or Main thoda Hindi bolti hoon (feminine). These phrases will rescue you in any Hindi-speaking place, and people respect learners who ask for clarification instead of nodding blankly.

Watch for this social mistake: Hindi runs on three levels of you — aap (formal, respectful), tum (informal, friends and peers), and tu (very informal, close friends, children, or someone younger). Use aap with strangers, shopkeepers, elders, and in any formal setting; switch to tum only when the other person does or with clear peers and friends. Using tu with a stranger is not a grammar failure — it is a social one, and it can come across as rude or condescending. When in doubt, start with aap. A second classic trap: saying Main bhooka hoon is fine for I am hungry, but feelings of heat and cold use a different pattern — Mujhe garmi lag rahi hai (I am feeling hot, literally heat is touching me) and Mujhe thand lag rahi hai (I am feeling cold) — which you will meet properly in Lesson 3.

Full dialogue — a first meeting. Read aloud with the translations:

Priya: Namaste! Aap ka naam kya hai? — Hello! What is your name? Arjun: Namaste. Mera naam Arjun hai. Aur aap ka? — Hello. My name is Arjun. And yours? Priya: Mera naam Priya hai. Aap se milkar khushi hui. — My name is Priya. Pleased to meet you. Arjun: Mujhe bhi, Priya ji. Aap kahan se hain? — Me too, Priya. Where are you from? Priya: Main London se hoon. Aur aap? — I am from London. And you? Arjun: Main Delhi se hoon. Kya aap Hindi bolti hain? — I am from Delhi. Do you speak Hindi? Priya: Thoda. Dheere boliye, kripya. — A little. Speak slowly, please. Arjun: Zaroor. Aap kaise hain aaj? — Of course. How are you today? Priya: Main theek hoon, dhanyavaad. Aur aap? — I am fine, thank you. And you? Arjun: Main bhi theek hoon. Phir milenge! — I am fine too. See you again!

Practice: Write a self-introduction of five lines (greeting, name, where you are from, one feeling word, goodbye) and memorize it — this is your personal script for the next month. Then practice the survival phrases: cover the Hindi column, read the English, and say the Hindi from memory until you can do all eight without peeking. Finally, role-play the dialogue above twice, switching roles, saying every line aloud with the retroflex sounds and long vowels from Lesson 1.

Lesson 3: Hona, Gender, and the Present Tense

This lesson tackles the most important verb in Hindi — hona (to be) — plus the present tense and the gender system, which lets you describe what you do every day. Hona appears in nearly every sentence, and the present tense is built from a verb stem plus a hona form, so mastering this one pattern unlocks hundreds of sentences. Hindi has two genders — masculine and feminine — and every noun, adjective, and verb ending must agree with the gender of its subject. It feels strange at first, but the patterns are regular and you will learn them by example.

HONA (to be) — memorize this table completely: main hoon (I am), tum ho (you are, informal), aap hain (you are, formal), vah hai (he/she/it is), ham hain (we are), ve hain (they are). Use it for identity, origin, description, profession, and location: Main American hoon (I am American), Vah doctor hai (She is a doctor), Ham Delhi mein hain (We are in Delhi), Ve khush hain (They are happy). Note that Hindi word order is subject-object-verb, so the verb comes last: Main Delhi mein hoon, not Main hoon Delhi mein. Also note that vah is used for both he and she — gender shows up in the verb and adjective endings instead. Aap hain is used with aap even for one person, because respect is grammatically plural in Hindi.

GENDER is the heart of Hindi grammar. Most masculine nouns end in -a: ladka (boy), ghar (house — an exception ending in a consonant but still masculine), kitaab is feminine despite ending in b. Most feminine nouns end in -i: ladki (girl), kursi (chair), nadi (river). Adjectives agree: achha ladka (good boy), achhi ladki (good girl), achhe ladke (good boys), bada ghar (big house), badi kursi (big chair). The masculine plural ending is -e, the feminine plural is usually -iyan or stays the same: ladkiyan (girls). When in doubt about a noun's gender, learn it with the word — say ek ladka, ek ladki — and the gender will stick with the vocabulary. Two high-value patterns use hona with feelings: Mujhe bhookh lagi hai (I am hungry — literally hunger has come to me) and Mujhe pyaas lagi hai (I am thirsty). Learn these as fixed phrases today, along with Mujhe garmi lag rahi hai (I feel hot) and Mujhe thand lag rahi hai (I feel cold).

Now the present tense. Take the verb stem, add -ta for masculine singular, -ti for feminine singular, -te for masculine plural or formal, and then the matching form of hona. Karna (to do): main karta hoon (m, I do), main karti hoon (f, I do), tum karte ho, aap karte hain, vah karta hai (m) / karti hai (f), ham karte hain, ve karte hain. Bolna (to speak): Main Hindi bolta hoon (m, I speak Hindi), Main Hindi bolti hoon (f). Jaana (to go): Main bazaar jaata hoon (m, I go to the market). Rahna (to live/stay): Main Delhi mein rehta hoon (m, I live in Delhi). Kaam karna (to work): Main ek bank mein kaam karta hoon (m, I work in a bank). The pattern never changes — stem plus ta/ti/te plus hona — which makes Hindi verbs wonderfully regular once you know the gender of the subject.

Essential verbs to learn as vocabulary: jaana (to go): main jaata hoon. Aana (to come): main aata hoon. Khaana (to eat): main khaata hoon. Peena (to drink): main peeta hoon. Dekhna (to see/watch): main dekhta hoon. Padhna (to read/study): main padhta hoon. Likhna (to write): main likhta hoon. Sona (to sleep): main sota hoon. Chahna (to want): main chahta hoon. Sakna (to be able to): main kar sakta hoon (m, I can do it), main kar sakti hoon (f). These appear in nearly every conversation, so they repay memorization immediately. Note that sakna follows another verb: Main Hindi bol sakta hoon (m, I can speak Hindi).

Two beginner mistakes to avoid. First, forgetting gender agreement: saying main karta hoon when you are a woman — it must be main karti hoon. The ending must match the speaker, not the listener.

Second, putting the verb in the English position: Hindi is subject-object-verb, so Main khaana khaata hoon (I food eat) is correct, while Main khaata hoon khaana is wrong. Time words come early in the sentence: Main aaj bazaar jaata hoon (Today I go to the market), Main kal Delhi jaata hoon (Tomorrow I go to Delhi).

Dialogue — two coworkers at lunch:

Vikram: Aap kahan kaam karte hain? — Where do you work? Meera: Main ek bank mein kaam karti hoon. Main cashier hoon. Aur aap? — I work at a bank. I am a cashier. And you? Vikram: Main teacher hoon. Main ek school mein kaam karta hoon. — I am a teacher. I work at a school. Meera: Kya aap aaj thake hue hain? — Are you tired today? Vikram: Nahi, main theek hoon. Kya aap mere saath khaana khaana chahti hain? — No, I am fine. Do you want to eat lunch with me? Meera: Haan, zaroor. Ham restaurant jaate hain? — Yes, of course. Shall we go to the restaurant? Vikram: Chaliye. Mujhe bhookh lagi hai. — Let us go. I am hungry.

Practice: Conjugate in writing: karna, bolna, jaana, khaana, hona — masculine and feminine singular, all persons. Then write six sentences about yourself: two with hona (identity/origin/profession), two with feeling phrases (bhookh, pyaas, garmi, thand), two with present-tense action verbs. Say each sentence aloud, checking the gender endings. Finally, translate to Hindi: She is a doctor. She works in a hospital. She is tired today. She wants tea. Check: Vah doctor hai. Vah hospital mein kaam karti hai. Vah aaj thaki hui hai. Vah chai chahti hai.

Lesson 4: Numbers, Time, Days — The Everyday Vocabulary Core

This lesson builds your everyday number vocabulary: counting, telling time, and using days, months, and dates. Numbers are pure utility — prices, phone numbers, addresses, schedules — and telling the time unlocks appointments, opening hours, and travel. Hindi numbers from one to twenty are irregular and must be memorized, but after twenty the pattern becomes regular, and after one hundred it is simple. This lesson is built for repetition and out-loud drilling, because numbers only become useful when they are automatic.

Numbers 0–20 (memorize these exactly): 0 shunya, 1 ek, 2 do, 3 teen, 4 chaar, 5 paanch, 6 chhah, 7 saat, 8 aath, 9 nau, 10 das, 11 gyaarah, 12 baarah, 13 terah, 14 chaudah, 15 pandrah, 16 solah, 17 satrah, 18 athaarah, 19 unnees, 20 bees. Tens: 30 tees, 40 chaalees, 50 pachaas, 60 saath, 70 sattar, 80 assi, 90 nabbe. 100 = sau, 200 = do sau, 1,000 = hazaar, 100,000 = lakh, 10,000,000 = crore. Numbers between tens follow a pattern: 21 = ikkees, 22 = baaees, 25 = pachchees, 33 = taintees, 45 = paintaalees, 99 = ninyanve. The in-between numbers are the hardest part of Hindi counting, so drill them in groups: all the twenties together, then the thirties, then the forties. Phone numbers are read digit by digit: nau aath saat shunya for 9870. Note that Hindi speakers in India often switch to English numbers for large figures in business, but market prices, times, and ages are always in Hindi.

Telling time: Samay kya hai? or Kitne baje hain? (What time is it?). Ek bajaa hai (It is one o'clock), Do baje hain (It is two o'clock), Teen baje hain (It is three). Minutes use special words: Sava do (2:15 — a quarter past two), Dhai baje (2:30 — two and a half), Paune teen (2:45 — a quarter to three). So the pattern is: sava + hour for quarter past, dhaai/saadhe + hour for half past, paune + next hour for quarter to. Saadhe teen is 3:30, paune chaar is 3:45. For appointments: Meri meeting teen baje hai (My meeting is at three o'clock). Dopahar is midday/afternoon, shaam is evening, raat is night, subah is morning. Hindi speakers often use the 12-hour clock in speech and add subah, dopahar, shaam, or raat to make the time clear: Teen baje shaam ko (Three in the evening).

Days of the week: Somvaar (Monday), Mangalvaar (Tuesday), Budhvaar (Wednesday), Guruvaar or Brihaspativaar (Thursday), Shukravaar (Friday), Shanivaar (Saturday), Ravivaar (Sunday). All end in -vaar, which means day — a handy memory hook. Months: the English month names are widely used in India — January, February, March — and you can use them freely. Traditional Hindi month names exist (Chaitra, Vaishakh) but are used mainly for festivals and the religious calendar. Dates: Paanch May, Bees October. To say what day it is: Aaj kaun sa din hai? — Aaj Mangalvaar hai. What is the date? Aaj ki tareekh kya hai? — Aaj do November hai. Today is aaj, tomorrow is kal, yesterday is also kal — context tells you which — this week is is hafte, next week is agle hafte, last week is pichhle hafte.

Practical money and shopping vocabulary, since numbers live there: Yeh kitne ka hai? (How much is this?), Yeh das rupay ka hai (This is ten rupees), Bahut mehenga hai (It is very expensive), Yeh sasta hai (It is cheap), Kya aap ke paas chutta hai? (Do you have change?), Bill dijiye, kripya (The bill, please). In markets you will hear prices as bare numbers — paintaalees for 45 — so number fluency pays off immediately. Bargaining is normal in markets: Kam kijiye (Reduce the price, please) and Thoda kam karo (Make it a little less) are useful, said with a smile. The rupee is the currency, and prices are often written with Devanagari numerals as well as Western numerals, so recognizing the Devanagari digits from Lesson 1 helps.

Dialogue — at a market:

Dukaandar: Namaste! Aap ko kya chahiye? — Hello! What do you want? Graahak: Mujhe do kilo seb chahiye, kripya. — I would like two kilos of apples, please. Dukaandar: Baarah rupay hue. Aur kuch? — That is twelve rupees. Anything else? Graahak: Santre kitne ke hain? — How much are the oranges? Dukaandar: Paanch rupay kilo. — Five rupees a kilo. Graahak: Mujhe teen kilo dijiye. Aap kitne baje band karte hain? — Give me three kilos. What time do you close? Dukaandar: Ham aath baje band karte hain. — We close at eight. Graahak: Dhanyavaad. Kya aap ke paas bees rupay ke chutta hai? — Thanks. Do you have change for twenty rupees? Dukaandar: Haan, zaroor. — Yes, of course.

Practice: Count aloud from 0 to 100 without stopping — including the irregular teens and the twenties and thirties — then backwards from 50 to 0. Write today's date, your birthday, and three appointment times in Hindi. Then drill time: say the current time in Hindi every 15 minutes for two hours (Kitne baje hain? — ... baje hain), paying special attention to sava, saadhe, and paune forms. Finally, memorize the market dialogue's price lines and role-play a shopping trip where you buy three different items, asking Yeh kitne ka hai? each time.

Lesson 5: Asking Questions — Directions, Shopping, and Small-Talk Dialogues

This lesson turns you into someone who can get answers: how to form every kind of question, ask for directions, handle a full shopping conversation, and keep small talk going. Up to now you have mostly made statements; questions are what make you independent in a Hindi-speaking place. The good news: Hindi questions are simple. You already know the main pattern from the dialogues so far, and this lesson makes it automatic.

Hindi has two main ways to ask a question. 1) Rising intonation: keep normal word order and raise your voice at the end — Aap Hindi bolte hain? (Do you speak Hindi?). This is the most common in speech. 2) Question word in place: put the question word where the answer would go — Aap kahan rehte hain? (Where do you live?), Yeh kitne ka hai? (How much is this?), Aap kab jaa rahe hain? (When are you going?). The verb stays at the end in both patterns, which is the golden rule of Hindi word order. The core question words: kya (what — Yeh kya hai? = What is this?), kaun (who), kaun sa (which), kahan (where), kab (when), kyun (why), kaise (how), kitna/kitne (how much/many). Note that kya at the start of a sentence turns it into a yes/no question — Kya aap Hindi bolte hain? — while kya in the middle asks what: Aap kya karte hain? (What do you do?). Kyunki (because) answers kyun — Kyun? Kyunki main thaka hua hoon (Why? Because I am tired).

Directions vocabulary is survival-level. Kahan hai...? (Where is...?), Main ... kaise jaaoon? (How do I get to...?), Kya yeh paas hai? (Is it near?), Kya yeh door hai? (Is it far?), daayein (to the right), baayein (to the left), seedha (straight ahead), kone par (on the corner), ... ke beech mein (between... and...). When someone answers, listen for: seedha jaaiye (go straight), daayein mudiye (turn right), baayein mudiye (turn left), paar kijiye (cross). Polite opener: Maaf kijiye, station kahan hai? (Excuse me, where is the station?). And the lifesaver follow-up: Kya aap mujhe map par dikha sakte hain? (Can you show me on the map?). Hindi uses postpositions where English uses prepositions — mein (in), par (on), se (from/with), ko (to), tak (until) come after the noun: bazaar mein (in the market), ghar se (from home), Delhi tak (until Delhi).

Shopping questions beyond prices: Kya aap ke paas... hai? (Do you have...?), Kya ... hai? (Is there...? / Are there...?), Kya main pehen kar dekh sakta hoon? (Can I try it on? — masculine) or Kya main pehen kar dekh sakti hoon? (feminine), Kya aap ke paas bada size / chhota size hai? (Do you have a bigger/smaller size?), Kya main card se bhugtan kar sakta hoon? (Can I pay by card?), Aap kitne baje kholte hain? (What time do you open?). Small-talk questions that keep conversations alive: Aap kahan se hain? (Where are you from?), Aap kya karte hain? (What do you do?), Aap yahan kab se hain? (How long have you been here?), Kya aap ko sheher pasand hai? (Do you like the city?), Aap kya salaah denge? (What do you recommend?). Two mistakes to avoid: saying Aap ka naam kya hai? with tum endings by accident (keep aap with hain, tum with ho), and answering a question you only half understood — use Mujhe theek se samajh nahi aaya, kya aap dobara keh sakte hain? instead of guessing.

Dialogue 1 — asking directions:

Yatri: Maaf kijiye, museum kahan hai? — Excuse me, where is the museum? Raahgir: Yeh paas hai. Seedha jaaiye aur baayein mudiye. — It is near. Go straight and turn left. Yatri: Kya yeh paidal door hai? — Is it far on foot? Raahgir: Nahi, paanch minute hai. Yeh bank aur pharmacy ke beech mein hai. — No, it is five minutes. It is between the bank and the pharmacy. Yatri: Bahut dhanyavaad. Is sadak

ka naam kya hai? — Thank you very much. What is this street called? Raahgir: Yeh MG Road hai. — This is MG Road.

Dialogue 2 — small talk at a café:

Lata: Aap kahan se hain? — Where are you from? Mark: Main Belgium se hoon. Aur aap? — I am from Belgium. And you? Lata: Main yahin se hoon, Delhi se. Aap yahan kab se hain? — I am from here, from Delhi. How long have you been here? Mark: Do hafte se. Mujhe sheher bahut pasand hai. — For two weeks. I like the city a lot. Lata: Aap kya dekhne ki salaah denge? — What do you recommend seeing? Mark: Main kal park jaana chahta hoon. Aap kyun nahi aate? — I want to go to the park tomorrow. Why do you not come? Lata: Achha vichaar hai! Kitne baje? — Good idea! At what time? Mark: Das baje. Ham yahin milte hain. — At ten. We meet here.

Practice: Write ten questions — two each for directions, shopping, time, personal info, and opinions — and say each aloud with rising intonation. Then memorize the direction-giving sequence (seedha, daayein, baayein, kone par) and give directions from your front door to the nearest shop, in Hindi, out loud. Finally, role-play Dialogue 2 with a partner or alone, then change three details (city, time, place) and run it again — this is how small talk becomes automatic.

Lesson 6: Talking About the Past — The Past Tense

This lesson gives you the past tense so you can say what you did, where you went, and what happened. Without a past tense you are stuck in the eternal present; with it, you can tell stories, explain your day, and answer the most common small-talk question in any language: Aap ne kal kya kiya? (What did you do yesterday?). Hindi past tense has one special feature — the word ne — but once you learn its pattern, the tense is regular and easy to use.

The past tense has two patterns. For intransitive verbs (verbs with no direct object, like go, come, sleep, stay), the formula is: stem + -a (masculine) / -i (feminine) / -e (masculine plural) + tha/thi/the. Main gaya (m, I went), Main gayi (f, I went), Main soya (m, I slept), Main soyi (f, I slept), Vah aaya (m, he came), Vah aayi (f, she came), Ham gaye (we went). For transitive verbs (verbs with a direct object, like eat, drink, see, buy, do), Hindi uses the ergative marker ne after the subject, and the verb agrees with the OBJECT, not the subject: Main ne khaana khaaya (m, I ate food — khaana is masculine so khaaya), Main ne kitaab padhi (f, I read a book — kitaab is feminine so padhi), Usne chai pee (He drank tea — chai is feminine so pee), Main ne film dekhi (f, I watched a movie — film is feminine so dekhi), Ham ne khaana khaaya (We ate food). This ne pattern is the single most important grammar point in Hindi — drill it until it feels natural.

Common past forms to memorize now: kiya (did — karna), khaaya/khaayi (ate — khaana), peeya/peeyi (drank — peena), dekha/dekhi (saw — dekhna), gaya/gayi (went — jaana), aaya/aayi (came — aana), liya/li (took — lena), diya/di (gave — dena), bola/boli (said — bolna), mila/mili (met/found — milna), soya/soyi (slept — sona), khareeda/khareedi (bought — khareedna). Time words anchor the past: kal (yesterday/tomorrow), kal raat (last night), pichhle hafte (last week), pichhle saal (last year), aaj subah (this morning). Note that tha/thi/the is often dropped in casual speech when the time is clear: Main kal bazaar gaya is understood as I went to the market yesterday, though Main kal bazaar gaya tha is the full form.

Negation goes before the verb: Main ne khaana nahi khaaya (I did not eat food), Vah nahi aaya (He did not come). Questions work as in Lesson 5: Aap ne khaana khaaya? / Kya aap ne khaana khaaya? with rising intonation is the everyday form. Two beginner warnings. First, forgetting ne: saying Main khaana khaaya instead of Main ne khaana khaaya is the most common beginner error in Hindi — ne is required with transitive verbs in the past tense. Second, agreeing the verb with the subject instead of the object: Main ne kitaab padha is wrong because kitaab is feminine, so it must be Main ne kitaab padhi. Check two things every time: is there a ne after the subject, and does the verb ending match the object?

Dialogue — talking about the weekend:

Anita: Aap ne is hafte ke ant mein kya kiya? — What did you do this weekend? Rohan: Main apne parivaar ke saath samudra tat par gaya. — I went to the beach with my family. Anita: Kaisa tha? Mausam achha tha? — How was it? Was the weather nice? Rohan: Haan, mausam bahut achha tha. Ham ne tairna kiya aur machhli khaayi. — Yes, the weather was very nice. We swam and ate fish. Anita: Badhiya! Main ghar par rahi. Main ne film dekhi aur kitaab padhi. — Great! I stayed home. I watched a movie and read a book. Rohan: Kya aap ko film pasand aayi? — Did you like the movie? Anita: Haan, mujhe bahut pasand aayi. Aur Ravivaar ko, aap ne kya kiya? — Yes, I loved it. And Sunday, what did you do? Rohan: Main der tak soya aur apne mata-pita se mila. — I slept in and met my parents.

Practice: Write a paragraph of 8–10 sentences about what you did yesterday, using at least five different past-tense verbs (mix ne verbs and intransitive verbs, masculine and feminine objects). Read it aloud twice. Then conjugate in writing: jaana, khaana, dekhna, karna, peena — full past tense, masculine and feminine, with correct ne and correct object agreement. Finally, answer these aloud in complete Hindi sentences: Aap ne kal kya khaaya? Aap kitne baje uthe? Aap ne pichhle hafte ke ant mein kya kiya? Kya aap ne is hafte film dekhi?

Lesson 7: Making Plans — Future Expressions + Restaurant and Travel Dialogues

This lesson teaches you to talk about the future — making plans, accepting invitations, and handling two of the most useful real-world conversations: ordering in a restaurant and navigating travel. Future talk is what turns you from a tourist into someone who can actually live in Hindi: booking, inviting, promising, and planning.

Hindi has two easy ways to express the future. The everyday one is the future tense itself: stem + -unga (masculine singular, I), -ungi (feminine singular, I), -ega (masculine singular, he), -egi (feminine singular, she), -enge (plural/formal). Main jaaunga (m, I will go), Main jaaungi (f, I will go), Main khaaunga (m, I will eat), Main khaaungi (f, I will eat), Vah aayega (m, he will come), Vah aayegi (f, she will come), Ham jaayenge (we will go), Aap aayenge (you will come — formal). The second way is the present tense with a future time word, which is very common in casual speech: Kal main Delhi jaata hoon (Tomorrow I go to Delhi) is understood as a future plan. For this level, default to the future endings in conversation and recognize the present-tense-with-future-time pattern when you hear it. Promises use the future tense: Main aap ko kal phone karoonga (m, I will call you tomorrow), Main aap ko kal phone karoongi (f).

Restaurant vocabulary and phrases. Menu (the menu), shuruaat (starter), mukhya khaana (main course), meetha (dessert/sweets), peene ki cheez (drink). Ordering: Mujhe... chahiye (I would like...), Mere liye... (For me...), Aap kya salaah denge? (What do you recommend?). During the meal: Yeh bahut swaadisht hai (It is delicious), Kya aap mujhe... laa sakte hain? (Can you bring me...?), Bill dijiye, kripya (The bill, please). Common mistake: ordering with a bare Mujhe chahiye is fine, but adding kripya (please) and using aap forms with the waiter sounds much better. Tipping: a small tip is appreciated in restaurants, though service charges are sometimes included — check the bill. Indian food to know: roti (flatbread), naan (oven bread), chawal (rice), daal (lentils), sabzi (vegetables), samosa (fried pastry), thali (platter meal), chai (tea), lassi (yogurt drink), mithai (sweets). Vegetarian food is everywhere — Main shakahari hoon (I am vegetarian) is a useful sentence.

Travel phrases. Hawai adda par (at the airport): Gate kahan hai? (Where is the gate?), Meri flight ... baje hai (My flight is at...), Meri booking hai (I have a reservation). Hotel mein: Mujhe ek double kamra chahiye (I would like a double room), Nashta kitne baje hai? (What time is breakfast?), Kya aap ke paas wifi hai? (Do you have wifi?). Problems: Mera passport kho gaya hai (I lost my passport), Mujhe madad chahiye (I need help), Pharmacy kahan hai? (Where is a pharmacy?). At the station: Train kis platform se jaayegi? (From which platform does the train leave?), Delhi ke liye ek ticket dijiye, kripya (A ticket to Delhi, please). Note how much of this reuses Lessons 2–5 — the course is compounding.

Dialogue 1 — at a restaurant:

Waiter: Namaste. Kya aap ki booking hai? — Hello. Do you have a reservation? Claire: Haan, Claire ke naam par. — Yes, under the name Claire. Waiter: Is taraf, kripya. Kya aap menu dekhna chahti hain? — This way, please. Would you like to see the menu? Claire: Haan, dhanyavaad. Aaj ka khaas khaana kya hai? — Yes, thanks. What is the special dish today? Waiter: Yeh murghi aur chawal hai. Yeh bahut swaadisht hai. — It is chicken and rice. It is delicious. Claire: Mujhe yeh chahiye, aur ek salad. Aur aap, Jeroen? — I would like that, and a salad. And you, Jeroen? Jeroen: Mere liye machhli. Kya aap mujhe paani laa sakte hain, kripya? — For me, the fish. Can you bring me water, please? Waiter: Abhi laata hoon. — Right away. (baad mein) Claire: Bill dijiye, kripya. — The bill, please. Waiter: Paintees rupay

hue. — That is thirty-five rupees.

Dialogue 2 — making weekend plans:

Nikhil: Aap Shanivaar ko kya karenge? — What will you do on Saturday? Emma: Main apni behen se milne jaaungi. Aur aap? — I am going to visit my sister. And you? Nikhil: Main Shanivaar subah bazaar jaaunga. Kya aap aana chahti hain? — I am going to go to the market on Saturday morning. Do you want to come? Emma: Haan, zaroor. Ham kitne baje milenge? — Yes, gladly. What time shall we meet? Nikhil: Nau baje. Uske baad ham saath khaana khaayenge. — At nine. Afterwards we will eat lunch together. Emma: Badhiya. Main aap ko Shukravaar ko phone karoongi. — Perfect. I will call you Friday to confirm.

Practice: Write three plans for next week using the future tense, and three promises using the future tense with a time word. Then write a full restaurant dialogue (8+ lines) where you order a starter, main, drink, and dessert, ask for the bill, and react to the food. Say it all aloud. Finally, plan a real weekend in Hindi: where you will go, who you will see, what you will eat — five sentences minimum, out loud.

Lesson 8: Conversation Practice Drills + Your 30-Day Practice Plan

This final lesson does two things: it gives you structured conversation drills that combine everything from Lessons 1–7, and it hands you a concrete 30-day plan so your Hindi keeps growing after the course ends. A course can teach you the material, but only a routine turns material into fluency — so treat this lesson as the most important one to actually do. Hindi rewards speakers who try: even a short Hindi sentence earns goodwill, and every conversation you attempt makes the next one easier.

Drill 1 — The Two-Minute Monologue. Set a timer for two minutes and talk about your day — present for routines (Main saat baje uthta hoon...), past for what already happened (Main ne chai pee...), future for what is next (Main kaam par jaaunga...). Do not stop to look up words; describe around them (likhne wali cheez = the thing for writing, if you forget pen). Fluency is built by keeping going, not by being perfect. Do this drill daily — it is the single highest-value exercise in this course.

Drill 2 — Question Tennis. Write 20 question words and topics on slips of paper (Kahan...?, Kab...?, Kyun...?). Draw one and answer in two full sentences, then ask a follow-up question. Example: draw Kahan? → Aap kahan paida hue? Main Ohio mein paida hua. Draw Kyun? → Aap Hindi kyun seekh rahe hain? Main Hindi seekh raha hoon kyunki main yatra karna chahta hoon. If you practice with a partner, rally: they answer your question, then ask you one back. Ten minutes of question tennis covers more ground than an hour of passive review.

Drill 3 — Situation Cards. Write real situations on cards: Your hotel has no hot water — complain politely, Ask a stranger for directions to the pharmacy, Order dinner for two including a dietary restriction, Small talk with a taxi driver for five minutes. Draw a card and perform it aloud, both roles. These force you to combine vocabulary, grammar, and politeness under mild pressure — exactly what real conversations feel like. Rotate through all your cards weekly.

Two mistakes that stall learners at this stage. First, the perfection trap: refusing to speak until every conjugation is certain. Hindi speakers are generous with beginners who try; they cannot help beginners who stay silent. Speak with 70% accuracy today rather than 100% accuracy never. Second, passive consumption: re-reading lessons and calling it study. Reading feels productive but builds recognition, not production. Every minute of speaking aloud counts triple compared to silent review — your mouth needs the reps. A third Hindi-specific trap: mixing up aap, tum, and tu endings. When you switch levels mid-conversation, it confuses the listener — pick aap with strangers and stay with aap until the other person invites you to tum.

Your 30-day practice plan. Week 1 — Foundations lock-in: 20 minutes daily. Days 1–2: re-read Lessons 1–2 dialogues aloud. Days 3–4: conjugate hona and present-tense verbs from Lesson 3 out loud, masculine and feminine. Days 5–7: number and time drills from Lesson 4 until 0–100 is automatic, including the sava, saadhe, and paune time forms. Week 2 — Conversation ignition: 25 minutes daily. Daily two-minute monologue plus question tennis; re-do the Lesson 5 and 6 dialogues with changed details. Week 3 — Real situations: 30 minutes daily. One situation card per day performed aloud; write a daily journal of five Hindi sentences about your day (present + past + future mix). Week 4 — Live fire: 30+ minutes daily. Have at least three real conversations — with a tutor, a language-exchange partner, a Hindi-speaking neighbor, or even ordering food in Hindi. Record one, listen back, and note your three most frequent errors to drill next month.

Track it simply: a notebook page per day with the date, minutes practiced, and one line about what felt easier than last week. If you miss a day, never miss twice — the streak matters more than any single session. After 30 days, repeat the cycle with harder situation cards and longer monologues; you now own a system, not just a course.

Practice: Do Drill 1 right now — two full minutes about your day in Hindi, out loud, no stopping. Then write out your personal Week 1 schedule with exact times (e.g., 7:30 AM, 20 minutes, Lessons 1–2 dialogues). Finally, write five situation cards of your own based on your life — travel you plan, restaurants you visit, people you meet — and perform the first one today. Shubhkaamnaayein — you are a Hindi speaker now. Keep speaking.
