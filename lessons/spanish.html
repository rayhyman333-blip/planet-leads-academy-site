Speak Spanish from day one: pronunciation, core grammar, and real everyday dialogues.

Lesson 1: Pronunciation — The Five Vowels, Tricky Consonants, and Word Stress

This lesson teaches you to pronounce Spanish clearly from the start. Spanish pronunciation is one of the most consistent in the world: letters almost always sound the same way, so once you learn the rules you can read almost any word correctly. That consistency is your biggest advantage as a beginner. Your goal in this lesson is not a perfect accent — it is clarity. If your vowels are right and your stress lands on the right syllable, Spanish speakers will understand you even while you are still learning words.

Spanish has exactly five vowel sounds, and they never change. Say them now: A (ah, as in father), E (eh, as in bed), I (ee, as in see), O (oh, as in go), U (oo, as in moon). That is it — no short-vs-long games, no silent-vowel surprises. Practice these three words slowly: casa (CAH-sah, house), mesa (MEH-sah, table), luna (LOO-nah, moon). Each vowel gets the same amount of time; do not stretch one vowel longer than the others the way English habits push you to. A very common beginner mistake is reading Spanish vowels with English values — for example, saying 'si' (yes) like the English word 'sigh' instead of 'see'. When in doubt, keep every vowel short and pure.

Next, the consonants that trip up English speakers. The rolled R (rr) is the famous one: pero (but) has a single soft R, perro (dog) has the rolled rr — mixing them up changes the word, so practice matters. To learn the roll, put your tongue behind your upper teeth and blow air firmly while relaxing the tongue tip; it takes days or weeks, and that is normal. The letter J sounds like a strong English H (as in 'hot'): jueves (Thursday) is HWAY-bes. LL and Y usually sound like the English Y: lluvia (rain) is YOO-vyah, and yo (I) is YOH. The letter Ñ is the 'ny' sound in 'canyon': mañana (tomorrow) is mah-NYAH-nah. B and V sound almost identical in Spanish — a soft sound between the two, like 'vino' (wine) with the lips barely touching. Finally, C before E or I sounds like S in Latin America (gracias = GRAH-syahs), and Z is also an S sound there — one less thing to worry about in this course, which uses Latin American pronunciation throughout.

Word stress follows simple rules, and they are worth memorizing today. Rule 1: words ending in a vowel, N, or S stress the second-to-last syllable — casa (CAH-sah), hablan (AH-blahn), mesas (MEH-sahs). Rule 2: words ending in any other consonant stress the last syllable — hablar (ah-BLAHR), ciudad (see-oo-DAHD). Rule 3: a written accent mark (tilde) overrides everything — the stressed syllable is the marked one: médico (MEH-dee-koh, doctor), café (kah-FEH, coffee). Notice how médico and medico would be different words if the accent moved. A classic beginner mistake is stressing English-style, like saying 'com-PU-ta-dora' for computadora (computer) when it is com-poo-tah-DOH-rah. Trust the rules, not your English ear.

Here is a short dialogue using only sounds from this lesson. Read it aloud slowly, stressing the right syllables:

María: ¡Hola! ¿Cómo te llamas? (OH-lah koh-moh teh YAH-mahs) — Hello! What is your name? Carlos: Me llamo Carlos. ¿Y tú? (meh YAH-moh KAHR-lohs) — My name is Carlos. And you? María: Me llamo María. Mucho gusto. (MOO-choh GOOS-toh) — Nice to meet you. Carlos: Mucho gusto. ¿Hablas español? (AH-blahs ess-pahn-YOHL) — Do you speak Spanish? María: Sí, un poco. (see oon POH-koh) — Yes, a little.

Notice every vowel is short and pure, and the stress falls exactly where the rules predict. Say the whole dialogue five times today; it is your first Spanish conversation.

Practice: Write out these ten words and mark the stressed syllable in each, then say them aloud three times: ventana, teléfono, español, gracias, mañana, biblioteca, hotel, familia, zapato, universidad. Next, write your own name and three English words you use often, then 'Spanish-ize' their pronunciation using today's vowel rules and read them aloud. Finally, record yourself (any phone recorder works) reading the dialogue above, listen back, and circle any vowel that sounded English — re-say those lines until the vowels sound short and pure.

Lesson 2: Greetings, Introductions, and Survival Phrases

This lesson gives you your first real social toolkit: greeting people politely, introducing yourself, and surviving conversations when you do not understand something. These phrases are the highest-value sentences in the language because they unlock every interaction — ordering food, asking for help, meeting people. Learn them cold before you worry about grammar, because even a flawless sentence is useless if you cannot open and close a conversation gracefully.

Start with greetings. Hola (OH-lah) means hello and works everywhere. Buenos días (BWAY-nohs DEE-ahs) is good morning, buenas tardes (BWAY-nahs TAHR-dehs) is good afternoon, and buenas noches (BWAY-nahs NOH-chehs) is good evening or good night. Notice that 'good' is plural here — that is just how Spanish says it, so do not 'fix' it to buen día, which sounds odd to native ears. For introductions: Me llamo... (meh YAH-moh) means 'my name is' — literally 'I call myself'. ¿Cómo te llamas? (KOH-moh teh YAH-mahs) asks someone's name informally; ¿Cómo se llama usted? is the formal version for strangers, elders, or officials. Encantado (if you are male) or encantada (if you are female) means delighted, and mucho gusto (MOO-choh GOOS-toh) — nice to meet you — works for everyone.

Now the small-talk core. ¿Cómo estás? (KOH-moh ehs-TAHS) is 'how are you' for friends; ¿Cómo está usted? is the formal version. Common answers: Muy bien, gracias (very well, thanks), Más o menos (so-so), and the wonderfully honest Estoy cansado/cansada (I am tired — masculine/feminine). ¿Y tú? (ee TOO) means 'and you?' — short and essential. De nada (deh NAH-dah) is 'you're welcome', por favor (pohr fah-VOHR) is 'please', and gracias (GRAH-syahs) is 'thank you'. A frequent beginner mistake is answering ¿Cómo estás? with a full life story; a short answer plus ¿Y tú? back keeps the conversation balanced and gives you twice the practice.

Survival phrases are your safety net — memorize these before anything else. No entiendo (noh ehn-TYEHN-doh) — I don't understand. ¿Puedes repetir, por favor? (PWEH-dehs reh-peh-TEER) — Can you repeat, please? Habla más despacio, por favor (AH-blah mahs dehs-PAH-syoh) — Speak more slowly, please. ¿Habla inglés? (AH-blah een-GLEHS) — Do you speak English? Perdón (pehr-DOHN) — excuse me / sorry. Disculpe (dees-KOOL-peh) — excuse me (to get attention, more polite). Lo siento (loh SYEHN-toh) — I'm sorry. ¿Qué significa...? (keh seeg-nee-FEE-kah) — What does ... mean? These eight phrases will rescue you in any Spanish-speaking country, and native speakers respect learners who ask for clarification instead of nodding blankly.

Watch for this common mistake: mixing up tú and usted forms at the wrong moment. Using tú with a stranger is not a grammar failure — it is a social one, and it can come across as rude with older people or in formal settings like banks and clinics. When in doubt, start with usted and switch to tú only when the other person does. A second mistake: saying 'estoy embarazado' thinking it means 'I'm embarrassed'. Embarazada means pregnant — a legendary false friend. Say tengo vergüenza (TEHN-goh behr-GWEHN-sah) for 'I'm embarrassed'.

Full dialogue — a first meeting. Read aloud with the translations:

Ana: ¡Buenos días! ¿Cómo se llama usted? — Good morning! What is your name? Luis: Buenos días. Me llamo Luis. ¿Y usted? — Good morning. My name is Luis. And you? Ana: Me llamo Ana. Mucho gusto. — My name is Ana. Nice to meet you. Luis: Mucho gusto, Ana. ¿De dónde es usted? — Nice to

meet you, Ana. Where are you from? Ana: Soy de Texas. ¿Y usted? — I'm from Texas. And you? Luis: Soy de México. ¿Habla español? — I'm from Mexico. Do you speak Spanish? Ana: Un poco. Habla más despacio, por favor. — A little. Speak more slowly, please. Luis: Claro. ¿Cómo está hoy? — Of course. How are you today? Ana: Muy bien, gracias. ¿Y usted? — Very well, thanks. And you? Luis: Muy bien. ¡Hasta luego! — Very well. See you later!

Practice: Write a self-introduction of five lines (greeting, name, where you are from, one feeling word, goodbye) and memorize it — this is your personal script for the next month. Then practice the survival phrases: cover the Spanish column, read the English, and say the Spanish from memory until you can do all eight without peeking. Finally, role-play the dialogue above twice, switching roles, saying every line aloud with the correct vowel sounds from Lesson 1.

Lesson 3: Ser vs. Estar and the Present Tense

This lesson tackles the two most important verbs in Spanish — ser and estar, both meaning 'to be' — plus the present tense, which lets you describe what you do every day. English uses one 'to be' for everything; Spanish splits the job in two, and mixing them up is the single most common beginner error. Master this distinction now and every sentence you build later gets easier.

SER is for things that are permanent, essential, or defining: identity, origin, characteristics, time, and profession. Yo soy de Texas (I am from Texas). Ella es alta (She is tall). Son las tres (It is three o'clock). Él es médico (He is a doctor). ESTAR is for states, conditions, locations, and things in progress: Estoy cansado (I am tired). La tienda está cerrada (The store is closed). Estamos en casa (We are at home). A simple memory rule: ser = what something IS, estar = how something is or WHERE it is. Location always uses estar, even for permanent places: Madrid está en España (Madrid is in Spain).

Conjugations (memorize these shapes — they never change): SER: yo soy, tú eres, él/ella/usted es, nosotros somos, ellos/ustedes son. ESTAR: yo estoy, tú estás, él/ella/usted está, nosotros estamos, ellos/ustedes están. Read them aloud now. Notice the accents on estás, está, están — the stress rule from Lesson 1 in action.

Now the present tense of regular verbs. Spanish verbs end in -ar, -er, or -ir, and each family has its own endings. For -AR verbs like hablar (to speak): hablo, hablas, habla, hablamos, hablan. For -ER verbs like comer (to eat): como, comes, come, comemos, comen. For -IR verbs like vivir (to live): vivo, vives, vive, vivimos, viven. The pattern: drop the -ar/-er/-ir and add the endings. Practice with trabajar (to work): trabajo en una oficina (I work in an office), ¿trabajas los sábados? (Do you work Saturdays?). Notice that Spanish usually drops the subject pronoun — 'hablo' already means 'I speak', so 'yo hablo' is only used for emphasis.

A few very common verbs are irregular — learn them as vocabulary: TENER (to have): tengo, tienes, tiene, tenemos, tienen. Tengo dos hermanos (I have two brothers). IR (to go): voy, vas, va, vamos, van. Voy al mercado (I go to the market). HACER (to do/make): hago, haces, hace, hacemos, hacen. Hago ejercicio (I exercise). QUERER (to want): quiero, quieres, quiere, queremos, quieren. Quiero café (I want coffee). These four irregulars appear in nearly every conversation, so they repay memorization immediately.

Two beginner mistakes to avoid. First, the ser/estar swap: saying 'estoy aburrido' when you mean 'soy aburrido' — the first means 'I am bored', the second means 'I am a boring person'. Adjectives change meaning with the verb: ser listo = to be clever, estar listo = to be ready. Second, translating 'do' word-for-word: English 'Do you speak Spanish?' is just ¿Hablas español? — Spanish does not use a helper verb for questions, so never say '¿Haces hablas español?'.

Dialogue — two coworkers at lunch:

Pedro: ¿Dónde trabajas? — Where do you work? Sofía: Trabajo en un banco. Soy cajera. ¿Y tú? — I work at a bank. I am a cashier. And you? Pedro: Soy profesor. Trabajo en una escuela. — I am a teacher. I work at a school. Sofía: ¿Estás cansado hoy? — Are you tired today? Sofía: No, estoy bien. ¿Quieres comer conmigo? — No, I'm fine. Do you want to eat with me? Pedro: Sí, quiero. ¿Vamos al restaurante? — Yes, I want to. Shall we go to the restaurant? Sofía: Vamos. Tengo hambre. — Let's go. I'm hungry.

(Note: tengo hambre — 'I have hunger' — is how Spanish says 'I'm hungry'. Another estar/ser trap avoided by using tener.)

Practice: Conjugate these verbs fully in writing: hablar, comer, vivir, tener, ir. Then write six sentences about yourself: two with ser (identity/origin/profession), two with estar (feeling/location), two with present-tense action verbs. Say each sentence aloud. Finally, translate to Spanish: 'She is a doctor. She works in a hospital. She is tired today. She wants coffee.' Check: Es médica. Trabaja en un hospital. Está cansada hoy. Quiere café.

Lesson 4: Numbers, Time, Days — The Everyday Vocabulary Core

This lesson builds your everyday number vocabulary: counting, telling time, and using days, months, and dates. Numbers are pure utility — prices, phone numbers, addresses, schedules — and telling the time unlocks appointments, opening hours, and travel. There is no grammar trick here, just memorization plus a few patterns, so this lesson is built for repetition and out-loud drilling.

Numbers 0–20 (memorize these exactly): 0 cero, 1 uno, 2 dos, 3 tres, 4 cuatro, 5 cinco, 6 seis, 7 siete, 8 ocho, 9 nueve, 10 diez, 11 once, 12 doce, 13 trece, 14 catorce, 15 quince, 16 dieciséis, 17 diecisiete, 18 dieciocho, 19 diecinueve, 20 veinte. Notice 16–19 are compound words (diez + seis = dieciséis). Tens: 30 treinta, 40 cuarenta, 50 cincuenta, 60 sesenta, 70 setenta, 80 ochenta, 90 noventa, 100 cien. For 21–99, join the ten and the unit with 'y': 21 veintiuno (special single word), 22 veintidós, 23 veintitrés — but from 31 up it is treinta y uno, cuarenta y dos, cincuenta y tres, and so on. So 47 = cuarenta y siete, 83 = ochenta y tres. Hundreds: 200 doscientos, 500 quinientos, 1,000 mil. Uno becomes un before masculine nouns (un café) and una before feminine (una mesa).

Telling time: Spanish uses ser for time — ¿Qué hora es? (What time is it?). For 1 o'clock: Es la una. For all other hours: Son las dos, Son las tres... Son las diez. Minutes: Son las cuatro y diez (4:10), Son las seis y media (6:30 — 'y media' = and half), Son las ocho menos cuarto (7:45 — literally 'eight minus a quarter', very common). Noon is el mediodía, midnight is la medianoche. For appointments: la cita es a las tres (the appointment is at three). A classic mistake is saying 'Es las dos' — remember, only 1:00 uses 'es', everything else uses 'son' because the hours are plural.

Days of the week (all masculine, never capitalized in Spanish): lunes, martes, miércoles, jueves, viernes, sábado, domingo. Use 'el' for 'on': el lunes = on Monday, los lunes = on Mondays (habit). Months: enero, febrero, marzo, abril, mayo, junio, julio, agosto, septiembre, octubre, noviembre, diciembre. Dates: el cinco de mayo, el veinte de octubre. To say what day it is: ¿Qué día es hoy? — Hoy es martes. What is the date? ¿Cuál es la fecha? — Es el dos de noviembre. Note the order: day first, then month, then year — el 2 de noviembre, never 'noviembre 2'.

Practical vocabulary for money and shopping, since numbers live there: ¿Cuánto cuesta? (How much does it cost?), Cuesta diez dólares (It costs ten dollars), Es muy caro (It's very expensive), Es barato (It's cheap), ¿Tiene cambio? (Do you have change?), la cuenta, por favor (the check, please). Money amounts: 1.50 = uno cincuenta, 25 = veinticinco. In shops you will hear prices as bare numbers — 'cuarenta y cinco' for 45 — so number fluency pays off immediately.

Dialogue — at a market:

Vendedor: ¡Buenos días! ¿Qué desea? — Good morning! What would you like? Cliente: Quiero dos kilos de manzanas, por favor. — I want two kilos of apples, please. Vendedor: Son doce dólares. ¿Algo más? — That's twelve dollars. Anything else? Cliente: ¿Cuánto cuestan las naranjas? — How much are the oranges? Vendedor: Cinco dólares el kilo. — Five dollars a kilo. Cliente: Deme tres kilos. ¿A qué hora cierra? — Give me three kilos. What time do you close? Vendedor: Cerramos a las ocho. — We close at eight. Cliente: Gracias. ¿Tiene cambio de veinte? — Thanks. Do you have change for twenty? Vendedor: Sí, claro. — Yes, of course.

Practice: Count aloud from 0 to 100 without stopping, then backwards from 50 to 0. Write today's date, your birthday, and three appointment times in Spanish. Then drill time: set a timer and say the current

time in Spanish every 15 minutes for two hours (¿Qué hora es? — Son las...). Finally, memorize the market dialogue's price lines and role-play a shopping trip where you buy three different items, asking ¿Cuánto cuesta? each time.

Lesson 5: Asking Questions — Directions, Shopping, and Small-Talk Dialogues

This lesson turns you into someone who can get answers: how to form every kind of question, ask for directions, handle a full shopping conversation, and keep small talk going. Up to now you have mostly made statements; questions are what make you independent in a Spanish-speaking place. The good news: Spanish questions are simpler than English ones — no 'do/does/did' helpers, no word-order gymnastics.

The core question words (all carry a written accent — memorize them with it): ¿Qué? (what), ¿Quién? (who), ¿Cuál?/¿Cuáles? (which one(s)), ¿Dónde? (where), ¿Cuándo? (when), ¿Por qué? (why — two words), ¿Cómo? (how), ¿Cuánto/a? (how much), ¿Cuántos/as? (how many). Porque (one word, no accent) means 'because' — a classic beginner mix-up is writing '¿porque?' with no accent. To form a question, keep normal word order and just raise your intonation at the end, with inverted ¿ at the start: ¿Hablas español? ¿Dónde está el banco? You can also invert subject and verb for emphasis — ¿Dónde está ella? — but statement order works fine.

Directions vocabulary is survival-level. ¿Dónde está...? (Where is...?), ¿Cómo puedo llegar a...? (How can I get to...?), Está cerca (It's near), Está lejos (It's far), a la derecha (to the right), a la izquierda (to the left), siga derecho (keep going straight), en la esquina (on the corner), entre... y... (between... and...). When someone answers, listen for: gire (turn), cruce (cross), siga (continue). Polite opener: Disculpe, ¿dónde está la estación? (Excuse me, where is the station?). And the lifesaver follow-up: ¿Puede mostrarme en el mapa? (Can you show me on the map?).

Shopping questions beyond prices: ¿Tiene...? (Do you have...?), ¿Hay...? (Is there...? / Are there...?), ¿Puedo probármelo? (Can I try it on?), ¿Tiene una talla más grande/pequeña? (Do you have a bigger/smaller size?), ¿Aceptan tarjeta? (Do you take card?), ¿A qué hora abren? (What time do you open?). Small-talk questions that keep conversations alive: ¿De dónde eres? (Where are you from?), ¿A qué te dedicas? (What do you do for work?), ¿Cuánto tiempo llevas aquí? (How long have you been here?), ¿Te gusta la ciudad? (Do you like the city?), ¿Qué recomiendas? (What do you recommend?). Two mistakes to avoid: saying '¿Qué es tu nombre?' instead of ¿Cómo te llamas? (nobody says the first), and answering a question you only half understood — use No entiendo bien, ¿puede repetir? instead of guessing.

Dialogue 1 — asking directions:

Turista: Disculpe, ¿dónde está el museo? — Excuse me, where is the museum? Vecino: Está cerca. Siga derecho y gire a la izquierda. — It's near. Go straight and turn left. Turista: ¿Está lejos a pie? — Is it far on foot? Vecino: No, son cinco minutos. Está entre el banco y la farmacia. — No, it's five minutes. It's between the bank and the pharmacy. Turista: Muchas gracias. ¿Cómo se llama esta calle? — Thank you very much. What is this street called? Vecino: Es la calle Juárez. — It's Juárez Street.

Dialogue 2 — small talk at a café:

Elena: ¿De dónde eres? — Where are you from? Tomás: Soy de Colombia. ¿Y tú? — I'm from Colombia. And you? Elena: Soy de aquí, de Madrid. ¿Cuánto tiempo llevas aquí? — I'm from here, from Madrid. How long have you been here? Tomás: Llevo dos semanas. Me gusta mucho la ciudad. — I've been here two weeks. I like the city a lot. Elena: ¿Qué recomiendas visitar? — What do you recommend visiting? Tomás: Quiero ver el parque mañana. ¿Por qué no vienes? — I want to see the

park tomorrow. Why don't you come? Elena: ¡Buena idea! ¿A qué hora? — Good idea! At what time? Tomás: A las diez. Nos vemos aquí. — At ten. See you here.

Practice: Write ten questions — two each for directions, shopping, time, personal info, and opinions — and say each aloud with rising intonation. Then memorize the direction-giving sequence (derecho, a la derecha, a la izquierda, en la esquina) and give directions from your front door to the nearest shop, in Spanish, out loud. Finally, role-play Dialogue 2 with a partner or alone, then change three details (city, time, place) and run it again — this is how small talk becomes automatic.

Lesson 6: Talking About the Past — The Preterite Tense

This lesson gives you the past tense — the preterite — so you can say what you did, where you went, and what happened. Without a past tense you are stuck in the eternal present; with it, you can tell stories, explain your day, and answer the most common small-talk question in any language: ¿Qué hiciste ayer? (What did you do yesterday?). The preterite is for completed, finished actions with a clear end: yesterday, last week, this morning.

Regular preterite endings. For -AR verbs like hablar: hablé, hablaste, habló, hablamos, hablaron. For -ER/-IR verbs like comer and vivir: comí, comiste, comió, comimos, comieron. Read those aloud and feel the rhythm — the endings carry the meaning, so the subject pronoun is optional: Hablé con María (I spoke with María). Comimos pizza (We ate pizza). The nosotros form of -ar verbs (hablamos) is identical to the present tense — context tells you which: Hablamos ayer (We spoke yesterday) vs. Hablamos cada día (We speak every day). Time words are your disambiguation tool: ayer (yesterday), anoche (last night), la semana pasada (last week), el año pasado (last year), esta mañana (this morning).

The verbs you use most in the past are irregular, so learn these as set phrases. IR/SER share one conjugation (context decides): fui, fuiste, fue, fuimos, fueron. Fui al médico (I went to the doctor). Fue un buen día (It was a good day). TENER: tuve, tuviste, tuvo, tuvimos, tuvieron — Tuve un problema (I had a problem). HACER: hice, hiciste, hizo, hicimos, hicieron — Hice la tarea (I did the homework). ESTAR: estuve, estuviste, estuvo, estuvimos, estuvieron — Estuve enfermo (I was sick). DECIR (to say/tell): dije, dijiste, dijo, dijimos, dijeron — Me dijo la verdad (He told me the truth). QUERER: quise, quisiste, quiso... — Quise llamarte (I tried to call you — note: in the preterite, querer often means 'tried').

Two important beginner warnings. First, Spanish has TWO past tenses — preterite (completed) and imperfect (ongoing/habitual) — and this course teaches only the preterite, which is enough for Level 2 conversations. Do not worry about the imperfect yet; using the preterite for everything at first is understandable and will not block communication. Second, watch the accent marks: hablo (I speak) vs. habló (he/she spoke) differ only by the accent, and mixing them changes who did the action. Write the accents every time until your hand does it automatically. A third trap: translating 'I was' as era/fui confusion — just use fui/estuve for completed past states for now: Fui feliz ese día (I was happy that day).

Dialogue — talking about the weekend:

Lucía: ¿Qué hiciste el fin de semana? — What did you do on the weekend? Diego: Fui a la playa con mi familia. — I went to the beach with my family. Lucía: ¿Qué tal? ¿Hizo buen tiempo? — How was it? Was the weather good? Diego: Sí, hizo mucho sol. Nadamos y comimos pescado. — Yes, it was sunny all day. We swam and ate fish. Lucía: ¡Qué bien! Yo me quedé en casa. Vi una película y leí un libro. — How nice! I stayed home. I watched a movie and read a book. Diego: ¿Te gustó la película? — Did you like the movie? Lucía: Sí, me encantó. ¿Qué hiciste el domingo? — Yes, I loved it. What did you do Sunday? Diego: Dormí hasta tarde y visité a mis padres. — I slept in and visited my parents.

Practice: Write a paragraph of 8–10 sentences about what you did yesterday, using at least five different preterite verbs (mix regular and irregular). Read it aloud twice. Then conjugate in writing: hablar, comer, ir, tener, hacer — full preterite, all persons. Finally, answer these aloud in complete

Spanish sentences: ¿Qué comiste ayer? ¿A qué hora te despertaste? ¿Qué hiciste el fin de semana pasado? ¿Viste una película esta semana?

Lesson 7: Making Plans — Future Expressions + Restaurant and Travel Dialogues

This lesson teaches you to talk about the future — making plans, accepting invitations, and handling two of the most useful real-world conversations: ordering in a restaurant and navigating travel. Future talk is what turns you from a tourist into someone who can actually live in Spanish: booking, inviting, promising, and planning.

Spanish has two easy ways to express the future. The everyday one is IR + A + INFINITIVE: Voy a estudiar (I'm going to study), Vamos a viajar (We are going to travel), ¿Vas a venir? (Are you coming?). Just conjugate ir (voy, vas, va, vamos, van) and add any infinitive — this one structure covers most future needs. The second is the simple future tense, formed by adding endings to the infinitive: hablaré, hablarás, hablará, hablaremos, hablarán (I/you/he/we/they will speak); comeré, viviré follow the same pattern. Use it for promises, predictions, and formal plans: Te llamaré mañana (I will call you tomorrow), Hará sol (It will be sunny). For this level, default to ir + a + infinitive in conversation and recognize the simple future when you hear it.

Restaurant vocabulary and phrases. La carta / el menú (the menu), el plato del día (dish of the day), la entrada (starter), el plato principal (main course), el postre (dessert), la bebida (drink). Ordering: Quisiera... (I would like... — polite), Para mí... (For me...), ¿Qué me recomienda? (What do you recommend?). During the meal: Está riquísimo (It's delicious), ¿Me trae...? (Can you bring me...?), La cuenta, por favor (The check, please). Common mistake: saying 'estoy caliente' to mean 'I'm hot' (temperature) — caliente with estar means aroused. Say tengo calor (I'm hot) and tengo frío (I'm cold). Another: ordering with 'quiero' barked at the waiter is grammatically fine but socially blunt — quisiera or me gustaría sounds much better.

Travel phrases. En el aeropuerto (at the airport): ¿Dónde está la puerta...? (Where is gate ...?), Mi vuelo sale a las... (My flight leaves at...), Tengo una reserva (I have a reservation). En el hotel: Quisiera una habitación doble (I'd like a double room), ¿A qué hora es el desayuno? (What time is breakfast?), ¿Tienen wifi? (Do you have wifi?). Problems: Perdí mi pasaporte (I lost my passport), Necesito ayuda (I need help), ¿Dónde hay una farmacia? (Where is there a pharmacy?). Note how much of this reuses Lessons 2–5 — the course is compounding.

Dialogue 1 — at a restaurant:

Camarero: Buenas noches. ¿Tienen reserva? — Good evening. Do you have a reservation? Marta: Sí, a nombre de Marta. — Yes, under Marta. Camarero: Por aquí, por favor. ¿Desean ver la carta? — This way, please. Would you like to see the menu? Marta: Sí, gracias. ¿Cuál es el plato del día? — Yes, thanks. What is the dish of the day? Camarero: Es pollo con arroz. Está riquísimo. — It's chicken with rice. It's delicious. Marta: Quisiera eso, y una ensalada. ¿Y tú, Juan? — I'd like that, and a salad. And you, Juan? Juan: Para mí, el pescado. ¿Me trae agua, por favor? — For me, the fish. Can you bring me water, please? Camarero: Enseguida. — Right away. (later) Marta: La cuenta, por favor. — The check, please. Camarero: Son treinta y cinco dólares. — It's thirty-five dollars.

Dialogue 2 — making weekend plans:

Pablo: ¿Qué vas a hacer el sábado? — What are you going to do Saturday? Carmen: Voy a visitar a mi hermana. ¿Y tú? — I'm going to visit my sister. And you? Pablo: Voy a ir al mercado por la mañana. ¿Quieres venir? — I'm going to go to the market in the morning. Do you want to come? Carmen: Sí, me

gustaría. ¿A qué hora nos vemos? — Yes, I'd like to. What time shall we meet? Pablo: A las nueve. Después vamos a almorzar juntos. — At nine. Afterwards we'll have lunch together. Carmen: Perfecto. Te llamaré el viernes para confirmar. — Perfect. I'll call you Friday to confirm.

Practice: Write three plans for next week using ir + a + infinitive, and three promises using the simple future. Then write a full restaurant dialogue (8+ lines) where you order a starter, main, drink, and dessert, ask for the check, and react to the food. Say it all aloud. Finally, plan a real weekend in Spanish: where you will go, who you will see, what you will eat — five sentences minimum, out loud.

Lesson 8: Conversation Practice Drills + Your 30-Day Fluency Plan

This final lesson does two things: it gives you structured conversation drills that combine everything from Lessons 1–7, and it hands you a concrete 30-day plan so your Spanish keeps growing after the course ends. A course can teach you the material, but only a routine turns material into fluency — so treat this lesson as the most important one to actually do.

Drill 1 — The Two-Minute Monologue. Set a timer for two minutes and talk about your day — present for routines (Me levanto a las siete...), preterite for what already happened (Desayuné café...), future for what is next (Voy a trabajar...). Do not stop to look up words; describe around them (la cosa para escribir = the thing for writing, if you forget 'pen'). Fluency is built by keeping going, not by being perfect. Do this drill daily — it is the single highest-value exercise in this course.

Drill 2 — Question Tennis. Write 20 question words and topics on slips of paper (¿Dónde...?, ¿Cuándo...?, ¿Por qué...?). Draw one and answer in two full sentences, then ask a follow-up question. Example: draw '¿Dónde?' → ¿Dónde naciste? Nací en Ohio. Draw '¿Por qué?' → ¿Por qué estudias español? Estudio español porque quiero viajar. If you practice with a partner, rally: they answer your question, then ask you one back. Ten minutes of question tennis covers more ground than an hour of passive review.

Drill 3 — Situation Cards. Write real situations on cards: 'Your hotel has no hot water — complain politely', 'Ask a stranger for directions to the pharmacy', 'Order dinner for two including a dietary restriction', 'Small talk with a taxi driver for five minutes'. Draw a card and perform it aloud, both roles. These force you to combine vocabulary, grammar, and politeness under mild pressure — exactly what real conversations feel like. Rotate through all your cards weekly.

Two mistakes that stall learners at this stage. First, the perfection trap: refusing to speak until every conjugation is certain. Native speakers are generous with beginners who try; they cannot help beginners who stay silent. Speak with 70% accuracy today rather than 100% accuracy never. Second, passive consumption: re-reading lessons and calling it study. Reading feels productive but builds recognition, not production. Every minute of speaking aloud counts triple compared to silent review — your mouth needs the reps.

Your 30-day fluency plan. Week 1 — Foundations lock-in: 20 minutes daily. Days 1–2: re-read Lessons 1–2 dialogues aloud. Days 3–4: conjugate ser/estar/present verbs from Lesson 3 out loud. Days 5–7: number and time drills from Lesson 4 until 0–100 is automatic. Week 2 — Conversation ignition: 25 minutes daily. Daily two-minute monologue plus question tennis; re-do the Lesson 5 and 6 dialogues with changed details. Week 3 — Real situations: 30 minutes daily. One situation card per day performed aloud; write a daily journal of five Spanish sentences about your day (present + preterite + future mix). Week 4 — Live fire: 30+ minutes daily. Have at least three real conversations — with a tutor, a language-exchange partner, a Spanish-speaking neighbor, or even ordering food in Spanish. Record one, listen back, and note your three most frequent errors to drill next month.

Track it simply: a notebook page per day with the date, minutes practiced, and one line about what felt easier than last week. If you miss a day, never miss twice — the streak matters more than any single session. After 30 days, repeat the cycle with harder situation cards and longer monologues; you now own a system, not just a course.

Practice: Do Drill 1 right now — two full minutes about your day in Spanish, out loud, no stopping. Then write out your personal Week 1 schedule with exact times (e.g., '7:30 AM, 20 minutes, Lessons 1–2 dialogues'). Finally, write five situation cards of your own based on your life — travel you plan, restaurants you visit, people you meet — and perform the first one today. ¡Buena suerte — you are a Spanish speaker now. Keep speaking.
