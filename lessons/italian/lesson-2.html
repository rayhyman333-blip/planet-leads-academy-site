This lesson gives you your first real social toolkit: greeting people politely, introducing yourself, and surviving conversations when you do not understand something. These phrases are the highest-value sentences in the language because they unlock every interaction — ordering food, asking for help, meeting people. Learn them cold before you worry about grammar, because even a flawless sentence is useless if you cannot open and close a conversation gracefully.

Start with greetings. Buongiorno (bwohn-JOHR-noh) means good day and works from morning until late afternoon. Buonasera (bwoh-nah-SEH-rah) is good evening. Ciao (chah-OH) is hi/bye among friends — warm and universal in informal settings, but use buongiorno with shopkeepers and officials on first meeting. Arrivederci (ahr-ree-veh-DEHR-chee) is goodbye, a presto (ah PREHS-toh) is see you soon, and buona giornata (BWHOH-nah johr-NAH-tah) means have a good day. For introductions: Mi chiamo... (mee KYAH-moh) means 'my name is'. Come si chiama? (KOH-meh see KYAH-mah) asks someone's name formally; Come ti chiami? is the informal version. Piacere (pyah-CHEH-reh) means pleased to meet you.

Now the small-talk core. Come sta? (KOH-meh stah) is 'how are you' formally; Come stai? (KOH-meh stigh) or Come va? (KOH-meh vah) are casual. Common answers: Molto bene, grazie (MOHL-toh BEH-neh GRAHT-syeh — very well, thanks), Così così (so-so), Non c'è male (not bad), Sono stanco (m) / stanca (f) (I am tired). E Lei? (eh LEH-ee) means 'and you?' formally; E tu? informally — short and essential. Per favore (pehr fah-VOH-reh) is please, grazie is thank you, grazie mille is thank you very much, prego (PREH-goh) is you're welcome. A frequent beginner mistake is answering Come stai? with a long life story; a short answer plus E tu? keeps the conversation balanced and gives you twice the practice.

Survival phrases are your safety net — memorize these before anything else. Non capisco (nohn kah-PEES-koh) — I don't understand. Può ripetere, per favore? (pwah ree-PEH-teh-reh) — Can you repeat, please? Parli più lentamente, per favore (PAHR-lee pyoo lehn-tah-MEHN-teh) — Speak more slowly, please. Parla inglese? (PAHR-lah een-GLEH-zeh) — Do you speak English? Scusi (SKOO-zee) — excuse me / sorry (informal); Mi scusi (mee SKOO-zee) — formal. Mi dispiace (mee dees-PYAH-cheh) — I'm sorry. Cosa significa...? (KOH-sah see-NYEE-fee-kah) — What does ... mean? These phrases will rescue you in any Italian-speaking place, and people respect learners who ask for clarification instead of nodding blankly.

Watch for this social mistake: Italian runs on the tu/Lei distinction. Use Lei (formal 'you') with strangers, shopkeepers, elders, and in formal settings; switch to tu only when the other person does or with clear peers and friends. Using tu with a stranger is not a grammar failure — it is a social one. When in doubt, start with Lei. A second classic trap: assuming 'camera' means camera — camera means ROOM in Italian (a false friend); the photo device is macchina fotografica. And 'libreria' is a bookstore, not a library (biblioteca).

Full dialogue — a first meeting. Read aloud with the translations:

Elena: Buongiorno! Come si chiama? — Good day! What is your name?
Paolo: Buongiorno. Mi chiamo Paolo. E Lei? — Good day. My name is Paolo. And you?
Elena: Mi chiamo Elena. Piacere. — My name is Elena. Pleased to meet you.
Paolo: Piacere, Elena. Di dov'è? — Pleased to meet you, Elena. Where are you from?
Elena: Sono di New York. E Lei? — I am from New York. And you?
Paolo: Sono di Roma. Parla italiano? — I am from Rome. Do you speak Italian?
Elena: Un po'. Parli più lentamente, per favore. — A little. Speak more slowly, please.
Paolo: Certo. Come sta oggi? — Of course. How are you today?
Elena: Molto bene, grazie. E Lei? — Very well, thanks. And you?
Paolo: Molto bene. Arrivederci! — Very well. Goodbye!

Practice: Write a self-introduction of five lines (greeting, name, where you are from, one feeling word, goodbye) and memorize it — this is your personal script for the next month. Then practice the survival phrases: cover the Italian column, read the English, and say the Italian from memory until you can do all seven without peeking. Finally, role-play the dialogue above twice, switching roles, saying every line aloud with the double consonants from Lesson 1.
