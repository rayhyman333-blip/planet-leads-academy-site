This lesson teaches you to pronounce Italian clearly from the start. Italian is one of the most phonetic languages in the world: letters almost always sound the same way, so once you learn the rules you can read almost any word correctly. Your goal in this lesson is not a perfect accent — it is clarity. If your vowels are pure and your double consonants are crisp, Italian speakers will understand you even while you are still learning words.

Italian has seven vowel sounds, and they never glide. A sounds like 'ah' in father: casa (KAH-sah, house). E has two sounds: closed E like 'ay' in day — sera (SEH-rah, evening); open E like 'eh' in bed — caffè (kahf-FEH, coffee). I sounds like 'ee' in see: vino (VEE-noh, wine). O has two sounds: closed O like 'oh' in go — sole (SOH-leh, sun); open O like 'aw' in law — posta (POHS-tah, mail). U sounds like 'oo' in moon: luna (LOO-nah, moon). Each vowel gets the same amount of time; do not stretch one vowel longer than the others. A very common beginner mistake is reading Italian vowels with English values — for example, saying 'grazie' (thank you) like 'GRAY-zee' instead of 'GRAHT-syeh'. When in doubt, keep every vowel short and pure.

Double consonants are the signature of Italian rhythm — and the most common beginner error. A double consonant is held LONGER, not said louder: pala (PAH-lah, shovel) versus palla (PAHL-lah, ball), caro (KAH-roh, dear/expensive) versus carro (KAHR-roh, cart). Mixing them changes the word, so practice matters. Drill these pairs daily: sete (thirst) / sette (seven), pena (pain/sorrow) / penna (pen), nono (ninth) / nonno (grandfather). Feel the tiny pause before releasing the doubled consonant — that pause IS the double letter.

Now the consonant combinations that trip up English speakers. C before E or I sounds like 'ch' in church: ciao (chah-OH, hello/bye), cena (CHEH-nah, dinner). C before A, O, U is a hard K: casa, cosa (KOH-sah, thing). CH is always hard K: perché (pehr-KEH, why). G before E or I sounds like 'j' in joy: gelato (jeh-LAH-toh, ice cream); G before A, O, U is hard: gatto (GAHT-toh, cat). GH is hard G: spaghetti (spah-GET-tee). GLI sounds like 'lyee': famiglia (fah-MEEL-yah, family). GN sounds like 'ny' in canyon: lasagne (lah-ZAHN-yeh). SC before E or I is 'sh': uscire (oo-SHEE-reh, to go out); before A, O, U it is 'sk': scala (SKAH-lah, stairs). Z is usually 'ts': grazie (GRAHT-syeh), pizza (PEET-tsah). The letter H is always silent: hotel (oh-TEHL), ho (oh, I have).

Word stress usually falls on the second-to-last syllable: casa (KAH-sah), parlare (pahr-LAH-reh, to speak), telefono (teh-LEH-foh-noh). A written accent mark (à, è, ì, ò, ù) signals stress on the final syllable: città (cheet-TAH, city), caffè, università. Notice how the accent also distinguishes words: e (and) versus è (is). A classic beginner mistake is stressing English-style — say pahr-LAH-reh, never 'PAHR-lah-reh'. Trust the rhythm: even syllables, gentle lift near the end.

Here is a short dialogue using only sounds from this lesson. Read it aloud slowly:

Giulia: Ciao! Come ti chiami? (chah-OH KOH-meh tee KYAH-mee) — Hi! What is your name?
Marco: Mi chiamo Marco. E tu? (mee KYAH-moh MAHR-koh eh too) — My name is Marco. And you?
Giulia: Mi chiamo Giulia. Piacere. (mee KYAH-moh JOO-lyah pyah-CHEH-reh) — My name is Giulia. Pleased to meet you.
Marco: Piacere. Parli italiano? (pyah-CHEH-reh PAHR-lee ee-tah-LYAH-noh) — Pleased to meet you. Do you speak Italian?
Giulia: Sì, un po'. (see oon poh) — Yes, a little.

Say the whole dialogue five times today; it is your first Italian conversation.

Practice: Write out these ten words, mark the stressed syllable, and say each aloud three times: grazie, famiglia, gelato, spaghetti, città, caffè, telefono, lasagne, uscire, pizza. Next, drill the double-consonant pairs (pala/palla, caro/carro, sete/sette, pena/penna) until the length difference is obvious. Finally, read the dialogue above aloud five times, then cover the pronunciation hints and read it again — you should be able to produce the sounds from the spelling alone.
