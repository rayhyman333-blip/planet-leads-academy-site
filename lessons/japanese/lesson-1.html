This lesson teaches you to pronounce Japanese clearly and introduces the three writing systems you will see in every lesson: hiragana, katakana, and kanji. Japanese pronunciation is beautifully consistent — each character is pronounced the same way every time — so once you learn the sounds, you can read almost any word correctly. Your goal is not a perfect accent today; it is clarity. If your vowels are pure and your syllables get equal time, Japanese speakers will understand you.

Japanese uses three scripts together. Hiragana (ひらがな, hiragana) writes native Japanese words and grammar endings: わたし (watashi, I/me), たべます (tabemasu, eat). Katakana (カタカナ, katakana) writes foreign loanwords and names: コーヒー (kōhī, coffee), アメリカ (amerika, America). Kanji (かんじ, kanji) are characters that carry meaning: 日 (sun/day), 本 (book/origin), 人 (person) — together 日本 (nihon) means Japan. Beginners learn hiragana first, then katakana, then kanji word by word. In this course every Japanese phrase appears in Japanese script with romaji (Roman letters) beside it, so you can start speaking immediately while your reading catches up.

The five vowels are the foundation — learn them perfectly and everything else follows. あ (a) as in "father." い (i) as in "machine." う (u) as in "flute" but with unrounded lips — say "oo" without pursing your lips. え (e) as in "bed." お (o) as in "go" but short, without the English glide. Each vowel is short and pure, always the same sound. Practice these five words slowly: あめ (ame, rain), いぬ (inu, dog), うみ (umi, sea), えき (eki, station), おかね (okane, money). Give every syllable equal time — Japanese is mora-timed, so たべます (ta-be-ma-su) has four equal beats, never "taBEmaSU" with English-style stress.

Most consonants match English, but learn these four special sounds now. し is "shi" (not "si"). ち is "chi" (not "ti"). つ is "tsu" — say "ts" plus "oo," as in つき (tsuki, moon). ふ is "fu" — blow air gently with your lips nearly closed, halfway between English "who" and "foo." The ら-row (ra ri ru re ro) is a light flap: tap your tongue once against the ridge behind your upper teeth, softer than an English r and lighter than an l. Try らくだ (rakuda, camel).

Three spelling rules change meaning, so master them today. First, long vowels: a vowel held for two beats instead of one. おばさん (obasan) means aunt; おばあさん (obaasan) means grandmother. ビル (biru) is a building; ビール (bīru) is beer. In hiragana, a long "o" is usually written おう (とうきょう, tōkyō) and a long "e" as えい; in katakana a long dash ー marks it (コーヒー, kōhī). Second, the small っ doubles the following consonant with a tiny pause: きて (kite) means "come," but きって (kitte) means a stamp or ticket; がっこう (gakkō) means school. Third, ん (n) is its own syllable: ほん (hon, book) is two beats, ho-n, not one.

Three particles are pronounced differently from how they are written, and this confuses every beginner exactly once. は is pronounced "wa" when it marks the topic: わたしは (watashi wa, as for me). へ is pronounced "e" when it marks direction: とうきょうへ (tōkyō e, to Tokyo). を is pronounced "o" when it marks the object: すしを (sushi o). You now know the three exceptions, so they will never trick you.

Here is the basic hiragana chart — the 46 core characters. Each row shares a consonant: あいうえお (a i u e o), かきくけこ (ka ki ku ke ko), さしすせそ (sa shi su se so), たちつてと (ta chi tsu te to), なにぬねの (na ni nu ne no), はひふへほ (ha hi fu he ho), まみむめも (ma mi mu me mo), やゆよ (ya yu yo), らりるれろ (ra ri ru re ro), わをん (wa wo n). Katakana mirrors this structure exactly: アイウエオ, カキクケコ, and so on — learn hiragana solidly and katakana takes days, not weeks. Kanji each have readings you learn word by word: 日 is ひ (hi) in ひ (sun) and にち (nichi) in 日本 (nihon, Japan). For now, just recognize 日, 本, and 人 as a set.

Practice: Write the あ-row (あいうえお) five times by hand, saying each sound aloud as you write. Read these ten words aloud, giving every syllable equal time: あめ, いぬ, うみ, えき, おかね, さくら (sakura, cherry blossom), ともだち (tomodachi, friend), がっこう, コーヒー, きって. Then drill the long-vowel pairs: say おばさん/おばあさん and ビル/ビール until the two-beat difference is obvious. Finally, read this sentence aloud slowly: わたしは がくせいです (watashi wa gakusei desu — I am a student). It uses only sounds from this lesson, and it is your first real Japanese sentence.
