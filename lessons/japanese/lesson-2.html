This lesson gives you your first real social toolkit: greeting people politely, introducing yourself, and surviving conversations when you do not understand something. These phrases are the highest-value sentences in the language because they unlock every interaction — ordering food, asking for help, meeting people. Learn them cold before you worry about grammar, because even a flawless sentence is useless if you cannot open and close a conversation gracefully.

Start with greetings, which change with the time of day. おはようございます (ohayō gozaimasu) is good morning — polite and safe everywhere; friends shorten it to おはよう (ohayō). こんにちは (konnichiwa) is hello, used from late morning through the afternoon. こんばんは (konbanwa) is good evening. おやすみなさい (oyasumi nasai) is good night, said before sleep. For introductions: はじめまして (hajimemashite) means "nice to meet you" and is used only the first time you meet someone. わたしはアンナです (watashi wa Anna desu) means "I am Anna" — swap in your own name. [place] からきました ([place] kara kimashita) means "I came from [place]" — for example, アメリカからきました (amerika kara kimashita, I came from America). Close every introduction with よろしくおねがいします (yoroshiku onegai shimasu), which roughly means "please treat me well" — there is no English equivalent, and skipping it makes introductions feel unfinished to Japanese ears.

Politeness words carry enormous weight in Japan. ありがとうございます (arigatō gozaimasu) is thank you; ありがとう (arigatō) is the casual version for friends. どういたしまして (dō itashimashite) is you're welcome. すみません (sumimasen) is the hardest-working word in the language — it means excuse me, sorry, and "may I have your attention," depending on the situation. Use it to get a shop clerk's attention, to apologize for bumping someone, and to preface any request. ごめんなさい (gomen nasai) is sorry for a real mistake. For goodbyes: さようなら (sayōnara) sounds formal and final, like you may not meet again — for everyday partings say じゃあね (jā ne, see you) with friends or しつれいします (shitsurei shimasu, excuse me) when leaving a room or ending a meeting politely.

Survival phrases are your safety net — memorize these before anything else. わかりません (wakarimasen) — I don't understand. もういちどおねがいします (mō ichido onegai shimasu) — one more time, please. ゆっくりはなしてください (yukkuri hanashite kudasai) — please speak slowly. えいごがはなせますか (eigo ga hanasemasu ka) — can you speak English? トイレはどこですか (toire wa doko desu ka) — where is the bathroom? たすけてください (tasukete kudasai) — please help me. These six phrases will rescue you anywhere in Japan, and people respect learners who ask for clarification instead of nodding blankly.

Two common mistakes to avoid. First, overusing さようなら for casual goodbyes — it can sound oddly dramatic, as if you are leaving forever. Default to じゃあね with people you will see again. Second, answering the phone or greeting with もしもし (moshi moshi) in person — もしもし is phone-only; saying it face-to-face confuses people. In person, start with すみません or こんにちは.

Full dialogue — a first meeting. Read aloud with the romaji and translations:

Yuki: はじめまして。わたしはゆきです。アメリカからきました。(Hajimemashite. Watashi wa Yuki desu. Amerika kara kimashita.) — Nice to meet you. I am Yuki. I came from America.
Ken: はじめまして。わたしはけんです。よろしくおねがいします。(Hajimemashite. Watashi wa Ken desu. Yoroshiku onegai shimasu.) — Nice to meet you. I am Ken. Please treat me well.
Yuki: よろしくおねがいします。にほんごがわかりません。(Yoroshiku onegai shimasu. Nihongo ga wakarimasen.) — Likewise. I don't understand Japanese.
Ken: だいじょうぶです。ゆっくりはなします。(Daijōbu desu. Yukkuri hanashimasu.) — It's fine. I'll speak slowly.
Yuki: ありがとうございます。(Arigatō gozaimasu.) — Thank you very much.
Ken: どういたしまして。じゃあね。(Dō itashimashite. Jā ne.) — You're welcome. See you.

Practice: Write a self-introduction of five lines (greeting, name, where you are from, one thing about yourself, よろしくおねがいします) and memorize it — this is your personal script for the next month. Then drill the survival phrases: cover the Japanese, read the English, and say the Japanese from memory until you can do all six without peeking. Finally, role-play the dialogue above twice, switching roles, saying every line aloud with equal syllable timing from Lesson 1.
