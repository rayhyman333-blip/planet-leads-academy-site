This lesson teaches you to pronounce Portuguese clearly from the start. Portuguese spelling is fairly consistent once you know the patterns, and Brazilian pronunciation — used throughout this course — has open, clear vowels that reward careful practice. Your goal in this lesson is not a perfect accent — it is clarity. If your vowels are clean and your nasal sounds land, Brazilian speakers will understand you even while you are still learning words.

Start with the pure vowels. A sounds like 'ah' in father: casa (KAH-zah, house). E has two sounds: closed E like 'ay' in day — você (voh-SEH, you); open E like 'eh' in bed — café (kah-FEH, coffee). I sounds like 'ee' in see: obrigado (oh-bree-GAH-doo, thank you — said by men; women say obrigada). O has two sounds: closed O like 'oh' in go — ovo (OH-voo, egg); open O like 'aw' in law — porta (POHR-tah, door). U sounds like 'oo' in moon: lua (LOO-ah, moon). Unstressed final E and O reduce: noite (NOY-chee, night), obrigado ends in 'doo'. Keep stressed vowels full and pure — do not let them glide the way English habits push you to.

Now the nasal vowels — the sounds that make Portuguese sound like Portuguese. A vowel followed by M or N (or marked with a tilde ~) is nasalized: air flows through your nose. ÃO is the famous one: não (nah-oom, no/not), pão (pah-oom, bread), coração (koh-rah-SAH-oom, heart). Ã: manhã (mahn-YAH, morning). ÕE: põe (poy, puts). IN/EN: tempo (TEHM-poo, time), bem (beh-eem, well). ON/UN: bom (boh-oom, good), mundo (MOON-doo, world). Do not pronounce the M or N fully — they only signal the nose, like French. Beginners often say 'nao' flat like the English word 'now' — drill não daily until the nasal glide feels natural.

Key consonant rules. R at the start of a word or doubled RR is a throaty H sound in most of Brazil: rua (HOO-ah, street), carro (KAH-hoo, car). A single R between vowels is a light flap like the Spanish single R: caro (KAH-roo, expensive), para (PAH-rah, for). S between vowels sounds like Z: casa (KAH-zah), mesa (MEH-zah). At the end of a word, S often sounds like 'sh' in Rio or 's' in São Paulo — either is understood; this course uses the São Paulo 's'. CH sounds like 'sh': chave (SHAH-vee, key). LH sounds like 'ly': filho (FEE-lyoo, son). NH sounds like 'ny' in canyon: manhã, dinheiro (jee-NYAY-roo, money). QU before E or I is just K: que (kee, that/what), aqui (ah-KEE, here). The letter H is always silent: hotel (oh-TEHL).

Word stress usually falls on the second-to-last syllable: casa (KAH-zah), falar (fah-LAHR, to speak), mesa (MEH-zah). A written accent (á, é, í, ó, ú, â, ê, ô, ã, õ) marks the stressed syllable and often the vowel quality: é is open (café), ê is closed (você). Notice avó (ah-VOH, grandmother) versus avô (ah-VOH, grandfather) — same stress, different vowel openness, different person. A classic beginner mistake is stressing English-style — say fah-LAHR, never 'FAH-lahr'. Trust the rhythm: even syllables, gentle lift near the end.

Here is a short dialogue using only sounds from this lesson. Read it aloud slowly:

Mariana: Oi! Como você se chama? (oy KOH-moo voh-SEH see SHAH-mah) — Hi! What is your name?
Rafael: Me chamo Rafael. E você? (mee SHAH-moo hah-fah-EHL ee voh-SEH) — My name is Rafael. And you?
Mariana: Me chamo Mariana. Prazer. (mee SHAH-moo mah-ree-AH-nah prah-ZEHR) — My name is Mariana. Pleased to meet you.
Rafael: Prazer. Você fala português? (prah-ZEHR voh-SEH FAH-lah poor-too-GEHS) — Pleased to meet you. Do you speak Portuguese?
Mariana: Sim, um pouco. (seem oom POH-koo) — Yes, a little.

Say the whole dialogue five times today; it is your first Portuguese conversation.

Practice: Write out these ten words, mark the stressed syllable and any nasal vowel, and say each aloud three times: não, pão, manhã, coração, obrigado, chave, filho, dinheiro, rua, tempo. Next, drill the pairs caro/carro and para/barra until the single-R flap versus throaty-R difference is obvious. Finally, read the dialogue above aloud five times, then cover the pronunciation hints and read it again — you should be able to produce the sounds from the spelling alone.
