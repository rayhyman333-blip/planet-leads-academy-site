This lesson makes you fluent in the analyst's daily tool: the spreadsheet. By the end you will sort and filter a dataset of hundreds of rows, write formulas that calculate new columns, and use the core functions — SUM, AVERAGE, COUNT, MIN, MAX, IF, and VLOOKUP or XLOOKUP — correctly. Spreadsheets handle a surprising share of professional analysis, and everything you learn here transfers to the dashboard and statistics lessons later.

Start with a clean layout. Put your data in a proper table: one header row with short, clear names (date, loaves, revenue), one row per record below it, no blank rows in the middle, and no merged cells. Keep raw data on one sheet and do your analysis on another, so the originals are never at risk. This discipline feels fussy until the day a careless sort scrambles your data — and sorting is the first skill. Select your data and sort by a column: sorting the bakery data by loaves sold from largest to smallest instantly shows the best and worst days. The golden rule of sorting is to select ALL columns before sorting, or use your program's "sort range with headers" feature, so each row stays intact. Sorting one column alone while the others stay put silently destroys your data — this is the most damaging beginner error in spreadsheets, and the habit of selecting the whole table first prevents it.

Filtering lets you look at a slice without deleting anything. Turn on filters for your header row, then use the dropdown on the weather column to show only rainy days: the spreadsheet hides the other rows and, crucially, functions like SUBTOTAL adjust to show only the visible rows. Filtering answers questions like "what is our average revenue on sunny days?" in seconds: filter to sunny, then average the revenue column. A filter never changes the underlying data — it only changes the view — so it is always safe to experiment. Combine filters across columns to narrow further: rainy days AND loaves below 65. When you are done, clear the filter to bring everything back.

Formulas turn raw columns into answers. Every formula starts with = and can reference cells: =B2*2.5 computes double the value in B2. The functions you will use constantly are SUM (total), AVERAGE (mean), COUNT (how many numbers), COUNTA (how many non-empty cells), MIN and MAX. So =AVERAGE(C2:C31) gives average daily revenue for a month, and =MAX(B2:B31) finds the best sales day. IF makes decisions: =IF(B2>80,"Strong day","Normal day") labels each day, and you can nest conditions for more bands. VLOOKUP (or XLOOKUP in newer programs) pulls matching data from another table — for example, looking up each product's category from a price list by its product code. When you copy a formula down a column, references shift relatively (B2 becomes B3), which is usually what you want; put a $ before the letter or number to lock a reference, like $B$2, when it must not move — for example, a tax rate stored in one cell that every row's formula uses.

Two more mistakes to engrave in memory. First, numbers stored as text: values that look like numbers but were typed or pasted as text will not sort or average correctly, and your totals will silently undercount. The symptom is SUM returning 0 or AVERAGE ignoring cells; the fix is to convert the column to numbers (most programs offer "convert to number" or multiplying by 1 in a helper column). Second, dividing by zero or referencing empty cells in AVERAGE: empty cells are ignored by AVERAGE, which is usually fine, but a truly missing value is different from a zero — a day the bakery was closed should be blank, not 0, or your average will sink unfairly. Lesson 3 goes deeper on exactly this distinction.

Practice: Rebuild the five-day bakery dataset from Lesson 1 in a spreadsheet. Add a sixth and seventh day of your own invention. Then: (1) sort by revenue, largest first, and confirm whole rows moved together; (2) filter to rainy days and compute their average loaves with AVERAGE; (3) add a column with =IF(B2>75,"Big day","Regular day") and copy it down; (4) at the bottom, compute total revenue with SUM, average loaves with AVERAGE, and the best day with MAX. Save this file — you will extend it in Lessons 3, 4, and 5.
