This lesson teaches the least glamorous and most important analyst skill: cleaning messy data. By the end you will run a repeatable cleaning routine — checking for missing values, fixing inconsistent formats, removing duplicates, and validating your work — on any dataset. Real-world data is never as tidy as textbook examples: entries are misspelled, dates arrive in three formats, half the rows are missing something. Analysts commonly spend most of a project's time cleaning, because every analysis built on dirty data is wrong in ways that look convincing.

Start with a cleaning mindset: never edit the raw data. Make a copy or work on a duplicate sheet, and keep a short log of what you changed — "removed 3 duplicate rows", "filled 2 missing revenues with the daily average". This log is your proof that the final numbers are honest, and it lets you or anyone else redo the work. Then inspect before you fix: sort each column, scan for odd values, and use filters to spot blanks. A quick scan of a customer list might reveal "New York", "new york", "NY", and "Nwe York" all meaning the same city, plus two identical rows and a phone column mixing (555) 123-4567 with 555.123.4567. Each of these is a different defect with a different fix.

Missing values are the most common defect, and the fix depends on why the value is missing. A blank revenue on a day the shop was closed is not a zero — it is "not applicable," and the honest treatment is to leave it blank so averages skip it, as Lesson 2 noted. A blank in a survey response where someone skipped the question is genuinely unknown. Your options are: leave it blank (best when few are missing and the analysis tolerates it), fill it with a sensible substitute like the column's average or median (acceptable when you disclose it in your log), or drop the row (only when the row is unusable otherwise). What you must never do is silently treat blanks as zeros without thinking — zeros drag averages down and can flip a conclusion. As a rule, if more than a small fraction of a column is missing, the problem is in how the data was collected, and you should say so in your findings rather than patch over it.

Inconsistent formats are the next defect. Standardize text with find-and-replace and consistent capitalization: pick "New York" and convert every variant to it. Standardize dates to one format (2026-10-26 style, year-month-day, sorts correctly as text too). Standardize numbers: strip currency symbols and commas into plain numbers in one column, keeping the original if you like. Remove exact duplicates — most spreadsheet programs have a "remove duplicates" command — but check first that the rows are truly identical and not two legitimate identical-looking records (two separate $20 sales on the same day are real; a pasted-twice import is not). Finally, validate: recompute your row counts before and after (you removed exactly the duplicates, nothing else), spot-check ten random rows against the raw copy, and re-run your key formulas. If the totals changed in a way you cannot explain from your log, something went wrong — go back.

Two mistakes define this lesson. First, cleaning without a log: a month later you will not remember whether you deleted those rows because they were duplicates or because they were inconvenient, and neither will anyone checking your work. Second, "fixing" data by deleting rows that contradict the story you want. Removing a terrible sales day because it spoils the trend is not cleaning — it is fabrication. The rule is simple: you may only remove or change values for documented data-quality reasons (duplicate, impossible value like a negative age, obvious typo), never because you dislike the result. When in doubt, keep the row and note the oddity.

Practice: Take your bakery spreadsheet from Lesson 2 and deliberately mess up a copy: misspell one weather entry, enter one date in a different format, duplicate one row, delete one revenue value, and add a stray space to a product name if you have one. Then clean the copy using this lesson's routine — inspect, log each fix, standardize, dedupe, validate — and write a five-line cleaning log. Confirm your totals match the original file afterward. Keep the log format; the checklist in your materials packet is built from exactly this routine.
