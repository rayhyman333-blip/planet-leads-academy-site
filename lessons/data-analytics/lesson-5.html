This lesson teaches you to turn numbers into pictures that tell the truth quickly. By the end you will choose the right chart for each question, build clean honest charts in a spreadsheet, and spot the tricks that make charts misleading — including in other people's work. A good chart lets a busy person grasp in five seconds what a table of numbers takes five minutes to reveal; a bad chart does the opposite, and sometimes lies.

Every chart answers one of a few question types, and the question picks the chart. Comparing categories? Use a bar chart: loaves sold per weekday, revenue per product. Showing change over time? Use a line chart: daily revenue across the month, where the eye naturally follows the trend. Showing parts of a whole? Use a pie chart only for a few slices (two to five) — payment method shares, for example — or a stacked bar when there are more. Showing the relationship between two numbers? Use a scatter plot: each day is a dot with temperature on one axis and loaves on the other, revealing whether hotter days sell more. Showing a distribution? Use a histogram: how many days fell into each sales band. When in doubt, a bar chart is the safest default — it is the hardest chart to misread. Match the chart to the question before touching any formatting; a beautiful wrong chart is still wrong.

Honest charts follow a short set of principles. Start bar charts at zero: a bar's length encodes its value, so cutting the axis at 70 makes a 75-to-80 difference look enormous when it is actually small. Label everything: chart title stating the finding, axis labels with units ("Revenue, dollars"), and data labels on key points. Keep it simple: one message per chart, no 3D effects, no decorative images, no rainbow of fifteen colors when two will do. Use color to highlight, not decorate — gray for the background data, one strong color for the point you want noticed. And keep the data visible: a chart should never hide the numbers behind so much styling that the reader cannot check your work. These rules are not about taste; each one prevents a specific misunderstanding.

Learn to spot misleading charts, because you will meet them in reports, news, and sales pitches. The truncated axis is the classic: bars or lines that start at a non-zero value to exaggerate a tiny change — always check where the axis begins. The cherry-picked timeframe: a line chart starting right after a dip to show "amazing growth" — ask what the longer history looks like. The area trick: using pictures (coins, people icons) sized by height when the eye reads area, doubling the visual exaggeration — if the value doubles, the icon's area quadruples. The missing context: "sales up 50%" with no mention that last month was the worst on record. And the dual-axis trap: two lines on different scales engineered to look correlated. Your defense is one habit: before reacting to any chart, read the axes, the labels, and the source note. Ten seconds of skepticism defeats most chart tricks.

The workflow for building a chart is: question first, then the right data slice, then the right chart type, then honest formatting, then a one-sentence takeaway written beneath it. For example: question — "are weekends stronger than weekdays?" Slice — average revenue grouped by day of week. Chart — bar chart of the seven averages, axis from zero, titled "Average daily revenue by weekday, October". Takeaway — "In this data, Saturday averaged the highest revenue and Monday the lowest." Notice the takeaway describes the data without claiming why. Build every chart this way and your visuals will be trustworthy by construction.

Two mistakes to avoid. First, charting raw unsorted tables: a line chart of dates in random order produces spaghetti that hides the trend — sort by date first, always. Second, pie charts with too many slices: a twelve-slice pie is unreadable; if you have many categories, use a bar chart sorted largest to smallest, or group the small ones into "Other". If your reader has to work to decode the chart, the chart has failed.

Practice: In your bakery spreadsheet, build three charts from your fourteen-day dataset: (1) a line chart of daily revenue over time, sorted by date, with labeled axes; (2) a bar chart comparing average loaves on sunny versus rainy versus cloudy days, axis starting at zero; (3) a pie chart of days by weather type (keep it to the weather categories you have). Under each chart write a one-sentence honest takeaway. Then deliberately make a misleading version of chart 2 by starting the axis just below your smallest value, and compare the two side by side — notice how different the story feels, and write one sentence about why the honest version is the one you would show the bakery owner.
