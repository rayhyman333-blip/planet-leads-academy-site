This lesson teaches you to build a dashboard: one page that shows the key numbers for a business at a glance and updates as new data arrives. By the end you will choose the right metrics, lay out a clean one-page dashboard in a spreadsheet, wire it to update automatically, and avoid the clutter that ruins most dashboards. A dashboard is the analyst's main product — the thing a busy owner actually looks at every morning — so building one well is a career-level skill.

A dashboard answers three questions in under a minute: how are we doing, is it getting better or worse, and where should I look closer? Everything on the page must serve one of those. Start by picking four to six key metrics with the decision-maker, not alone: for the bakery, daily revenue, loaves sold, average order value, and waste (unsold loaves) cover health, volume, value, and efficiency. Each metric needs three properties: a clear definition (is "daily revenue" before or after refunds?), a target or comparison (versus last week, versus a goal), and an owner who acts when it moves. A metric nobody acts on is decoration — cut it. Resist the temptation to show everything; a dashboard with thirty numbers is a report nobody reads. The discipline is: if removing a number would change no decision, remove it.

Lay out the page in three zones. The top strip holds the key metrics as big numbers with their comparison: "Revenue today: $212 (up $18 vs last 7-day average)". Big, current, instantly readable. The middle holds two or three trend charts: revenue over the last 30 days as a line, loaves by weekday as bars — the "getting better or worse" zone from Lesson 5's principles, axes honest and labeled. The bottom holds one breakdown that invites closer looks: sales by product or by hour, so a dip in the top number can be investigated one glance down. One page, no scrolling if you can manage it, consistent formatting, and a small "data as of" timestamp so nobody acts on stale numbers. White space is not wasted space — it is what lets the eye find the important thing.

The "updates itself" part is simpler than it sounds. Keep one sheet as the raw data log where new rows are appended daily (date, loaves, revenue, and so on), and build the dashboard on a second sheet whose cells reference the log with formulas. Today's revenue is a lookup of the latest date's row; the 7-day average is AVERAGE of the last seven rows; the 30-day trend chart's data range covers the log's recent rows. When you append today's numbers to the log, every metric and chart refreshes — no rebuilding, no copy-paste. Use structured references or a defined table so formulas extend automatically as rows are added, and put data validation on the log's key columns (dates as dates, weather from a dropdown list) so tomorrow's entry cannot silently break the dashboard with a typo. This append-only log plus formula-driven display is the entire architecture of most small-business dashboards.

Two mistakes ruin dashboards. First, the vanity metric: totals that only go up and never inform a decision, like "total loaves sold since opening" — impressive, useless. Every metric must pass the "so what?" test: if it dropped 20% tomorrow, would someone do something specific? If not, replace it. Second, the stale dashboard: a beautiful page whose data is three weeks old because updating it was manual and annoying. Automate the update path (the log-plus-formulas design), put the "data as of" date where it cannot be missed, and if you cannot keep it fresh, take it down — a stale dashboard actively misleads, which is worse than none.

Practice: Build a one-page bakery dashboard in a new spreadsheet file. Sheet 1 is the log: your fourteen days of data in clean table form. Sheet 2 is the dashboard: a top strip with four big-number metrics (latest revenue, 7-day average revenue, total loaves this week, average loaves per day) each with a comparison to the prior period; a middle zone with a 14-day revenue line chart and a weekday bar chart; a bottom zone with a small table of average loaves by weather. Add a "data as of" date. Then append a fifteenth day to the log and confirm every metric and chart updates without you touching Sheet 2. Show it to someone and time how long it takes them to answer "how is the bakery doing" — under a minute means you built it right.
