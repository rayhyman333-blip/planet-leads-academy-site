This final lesson walks a complete analysis from question to recommendation, using every skill in the course, and then maps where your learning goes next. By the end you will see how the pieces connect in a realistic project, know how to structure your own analyses, and have a clear next-steps roadmap. This is the lesson that turns eight separate skills into one professional workflow.

The case: the bakery owner asks, "Should I start baking on Sundays? I'm currently closed." That is a good question — specific, tied to a decision, answerable with data. Step one is planning: what data would answer it? Current Saturday sales (the closest proxy for Sunday demand), foot-traffic observations, and the costs of opening (staff wages, ingredients, utilities). The owner has six months of daily sales records — a spreadsheet with date, loaves, revenue, and weather. Step two is cleaning: you scan the data and find the problems Lesson 3 trained you for — three dates typed in a different format, one week where revenue was entered as text with dollar signs, two duplicate rows from a double-import, and four days with blank revenue that turn out to be holidays when the shop was closed (blanks, not zeros — you log every fix). Step three is analysis. Saturdays average 118 loaves versus a 75-loaf weekday average, and the median tells the same story, so the demand signal is real and not driven by outliers. Weather splits show the pattern holds on rainy Saturdays too. A scatter plot of temperature versus loaves shows no strong relationship, so you set that aside rather than forcing a finding.

Step four is the part beginners skip: checking the finding before presenting it. You ask the hard questions. Is six months enough? It covers summer and fall but not the holiday season or deep winter — you note that limit openly. Could something else explain strong Saturdays? They follow payday Fridays for many customers and have full staffing — you cannot prove cause, so you report the pattern without claiming why, exactly as Lesson 4 taught. What would it cost? Staff wages for a Sunday shift plus ingredients run about $180, and at the Saturday average revenue of roughly $295 with typical ingredient costs, the margin looks positive — but you present this as an estimate with stated assumptions, not a promise. Step five is the presentation: a one-page summary with three visuals — Saturday versus weekday bar chart, a six-month Saturday trend line, and the cost-versus-revenue comparison — each with a one-sentence takeaway, plus your recommendation: trial Sundays for eight weeks, track the same metrics on the dashboard from Lesson 7, and decide with real Sunday data. Notice the recommendation includes how to verify it — good analysis always says what would prove it wrong.

That workflow — question, plan, clean, analyze, check, present, verify — is the template for every analysis you will ever do. Write it on a card and keep it visible. The checking step is what separates analysts from spreadsheet operators: anyone can compute an average, but the professional asks "what could make this wrong?" and answers honestly in the report. Your materials packet's checklist is built around this workflow.

Where to go next depends on what pulled you. If databases excited you, go deeper into SQL: joins across multiple tables, subqueries, and window functions are the next tier, and any free SQL tutorial plus practice datasets will carry you. If statistics grabbed you, the next concepts are correlation, basic probability, and hypothesis testing — introductory statistics textbooks written for non-mathematicians are the right level. If dashboards and presentation were the thrill, learn a dedicated dashboard tool and study information design: how layout, color, and typography guide attention. If you want it all to scale, learning a programming language for data work lets you automate cleaning and analysis on datasets far beyond spreadsheet size. Whichever path you take, the method stays the same: small real datasets, question first, clean carefully, check honestly, present clearly.

Practice: Run your own end-to-end mini case. Pick a question about your own life that data could answer — for example, "do I spend more on weekdays or weekends?" or "which days am I most productive?" Collect two weeks of simple data in a log (date plus two or three columns), clean it with your checklist, compute one comparison with statistics, build one honest chart, and write a half-page summary with a recommendation — including one paragraph on what could make your finding wrong and what data would check it. Save the spreadsheet and the summary together; this is the first full case study in your portfolio, and it demonstrates the complete workflow to anyone who sees it.
