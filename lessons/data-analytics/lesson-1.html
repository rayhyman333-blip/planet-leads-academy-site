This lesson defines data analytics in plain terms and teaches you the four types of data you will work with throughout the course. By the end you will be able to explain what an analyst actually does day to day, tell the four data types apart on sight, and match each type to the questions it can and cannot answer. Everything in this course — cleaning, statistics, charts, SQL — sits on top of these foundations, so take the time to get comfortable here.

Data analytics is the practice of turning raw records into answers that help someone make a decision. Raw records are facts captured in the course of activity: each sale rung up, each order shipped, each customer sign-up. An analyst takes those records and does four things. First, they clarify the question: "Which product should we restock?" is answerable, while "Are we doing well?" is too vague. Second, they gather the relevant data and check that it is trustworthy. Third, they analyze it — count, group, compare, summarize — to find patterns. Fourth, they present the finding clearly, often with a chart, plus a recommendation. Notice what is not on the list: guessing, cherry-picking one favorable number, or dressing up a weak finding with a fancy chart. The analyst's credibility comes from honest handling of the data, and Lessons 3 through 5 teach the techniques that keep you honest.

Data comes in four types, and the type decides what you are allowed to do with it. Numerical data (also called quantitative) is numbers where arithmetic makes sense: sales amounts, temperatures, ages, quantities. You can add and average these. Categorical data (qualitative) is labels that group things: product names, colors, payment methods, customer segments. You can count how many fall in each category, but averaging "red" and "blue" is meaningless. Text data is free-form writing: reviews, comments, descriptions. You can search it and count words, but it needs processing before it behaves like the other types. Date and time data marks when things happened: order dates, timestamps. You can sort by it, group by month or week, and measure gaps between events. A single dataset usually mixes all four — an order record has a date, a product name (category), a quantity and price (numbers), and a customer note (text).

Here is a small sample dataset you will see referenced through the course — a neighborhood bakery's daily records. Each row is one day:

Date | Loaves sold | Revenue | Busiest hour | Weather | Staff note
2026-10-26 | 84 | 210.00 | 8 AM | sunny | "Ran out of rye by 10"
2026-10-27 | 61 | 152.50 | 9 AM | rainy | "Slow morning"
2026-10-28 | 77 | 192.50 | 8 AM | cloudy | ""
2026-10-29 | 95 | 237.50 | 8 AM | sunny | "Extra batch at noon"
2026-10-30 | 58 | 145.00 | 10 AM | rainy | "Two staff out sick"

Loaves sold and Revenue are numerical. Busiest hour and Weather are categorical. The date is date data. The staff note is text. Already you can see real questions this answers: which weather brings more sales, what a typical day looks like, whether staffing notes explain the slow days. An analyst would start by asking the bakery owner which decision the answer feeds — for example, how many loaves to bake tomorrow — and only then compute.

Two beginner mistakes start in this lesson. First, treating categories like numbers: assigning 1 = sunny, 2 = rainy and then "averaging" the weather to 1.4 is nonsense, even though a spreadsheet will happily compute it. Always ask: does arithmetic mean anything here? If not, count instead of averaging. Second, jumping to analysis before defining the question. It is tempting to open a dataset and start calculating everything, but analysis without a question produces trivia. Write the question in one sentence first — "Do rainy days sell fewer loaves?" — and let it choose which numbers you compute. The habit of question-first analysis is the single most valuable thing this lesson teaches.

Practice: Invent a small dataset of your own with ten rows about something you can observe this week — your daily cups of water, minutes spent on chores, or items in your shopping cart. Include at least one numerical column, one categorical column, and a date column. Then write three one-sentence questions your dataset could answer and three it could not (for example, water data cannot tell you about sleep). Finally, label each column in the bakery dataset above with its data type, and explain in one sentence why "busiest hour" is categorical rather than numerical even though it contains a number.
