This lesson teaches descriptive statistics — the handful of numbers that summarize a dataset — in plain English with no mathematical jargon unexplained. By the end you will compute and interpret the mean, median, and mode (three kinds of "average"), measure spread with range and standard deviation, and know which summary fits which situation. These summaries are the vocabulary of analysis: nearly every report, dashboard, and business conversation about data uses them.

Start with the three averages, because each answers a slightly different question. The mean is what most people mean by "average": add everything up and divide by the count. The bakery's loaves over five days — 84, 61, 77, 95, 58 — sum to 375, divided by 5 gives a mean of 75. The median is the middle value when the numbers are lined up in order: 58, 61, 77, 84, 95, so the median is 77. The mode is the most frequent value — with these five distinct numbers there is no mode, but in a month of weather data the mode might be "sunny". The crucial difference: the mean is pulled by extremes, the median is not. Add one catering order of 400 loaves and the mean jumps to about 129 while the median barely moves. That is why analysts report the median for things with occasional huge values — incomes, house prices, order sizes — and the mean when values are well-behaved. When someone quotes an "average" without saying which one, ask; the choice can completely change the story.

Spread tells you how much the numbers vary, and a summary without spread is half a story. "Average 75 loaves a day" could mean every day sells 73 to 77, or it could mean alternating 40 and 110 — same average, wildly different bakery. The range is the simplest measure: max minus min, here 95 − 58 = 37. The standard deviation is the more useful one: roughly, the typical distance of a day's sales from the mean. A small standard deviation means consistent days; a large one means volatile days. You do not need to compute it by hand — every spreadsheet has a STDEV function — but you need the intuition: two shops can share an average while one is predictable and the other is a roller coaster, and the standard deviation is the number that reveals which is which. Percentiles add one more useful lens: the 90th percentile is the value that 90% of days fall below, handy for questions like "how big a day should we be ready for?"

Choosing the right summary is a small decision tree. Describing a typical value? Use the median if outliers exist, the mean otherwise, the mode for categories. Describing consistency? Use range for a quick sense, standard deviation for the real picture. Comparing two groups — sunny versus rainy days, this month versus last? Compute the same summary for each group side by side and look at the gap. And always sanity-check summaries against the raw data: if the median says 77 but a glance at the column shows mostly 50s and 60s, something is off — perhaps a filter is still on, or text-numbers are being ignored. Summaries compress reality; the raw column is the ground truth you check against.

Two mistakes to avoid. First, quoting the mean for skewed data without mentioning it: saying "average order value is $85" when one giant order did most of the work misleads whoever plans inventory from that number. Report the median alongside, or at least note the outlier. Second, confusing correlation with cause, which starts here because summaries invite it: rainy days averaging fewer loaves does not prove rain causes low sales — perhaps rainy days in your data were also the days staff were out sick (remember October 30). A difference between groups is a clue that deserves investigation, not a conclusion. Write "rainy days averaged 15 fewer loaves in this data" — a true statement — rather than "rain causes slow days" — a claim the data alone cannot support.

Practice: Extend your bakery spreadsheet to fourteen days (invent seven more rows, keeping the same columns). Compute the mean, median, and mode of loaves sold, plus the range and standard deviation (STDEV). Then split the days into sunny versus not-sunny and compute the mean loaves for each group. Write three honest sentences summarizing what you found — using "in this data" language, not causal claims. Finally, add one extreme day (a 300-loaf catering order) and watch what happens to the mean versus the median; write one sentence explaining which summary you would report to the bakery owner and why.
