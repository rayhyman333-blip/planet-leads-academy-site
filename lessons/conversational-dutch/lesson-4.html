This lesson builds your everyday number vocabulary: counting, telling time, and using days, months, and dates. Numbers are pure utility — prices, phone numbers, addresses, schedules — and telling the time unlocks appointments, opening hours, and travel. Dutch numbers are built on a simple pattern, with one famous quirk: numbers from 21 onward are said backwards, ones before tens, just like in German. This lesson is built for repetition and out-loud drilling, because numbers only become useful when they are automatic.

Numbers 0–20 (memorize these exactly): 0 nul, 1 een, 2 twee, 3 drie, 4 vier, 5 vijf, 6 zes, 7 zeven, 8 acht, 9 negen, 10 tien, 11 elf, 12 twaalf, 13 dertien, 14 veertien, 15 vijftien, 16 zestien, 17 zeventien, 18 achttien, 19 negentien, 20 twintig. Tens: 30 dertig, 40 veertig, 50 vijftig, 60 zestig, 70 zeventig, 80 tachtig, 90 negentig. 100 = honderd, 200 = tweehonderd, 1,000 = duizend. Now the backwards quirk: 21 is eenentwintig (one-and-twenty), 25 is vijfentwintig, 37 is zevenendertig, 48 is achtenveertig, 99 is negenennegentig. It feels strange for a week, then becomes automatic. When you hear eenentwintig, train your ear to catch the een first and the twintig second. Phone numbers are read digit by digit or in pairs: nul zes, een twee, drie vier.

Telling time: Hoe laat is het? (What time is it?). Het is een uur (1:00), Het is twee uur, Het is drie uur. Minutes: Het is tien over vier (4:10), Het is vijf voor half drie (2:25 — literally five before half three), Het is kwart over zes (6:15), Het is kwart voor acht (7:45). Here is the famous Dutch trap: half drie does NOT mean 3:30 — it means 2:30, half way to three. Half vier is 3:30, half vijf is 4:30. Every beginner gets caught by this at least once, so drill it hard: half + the coming hour = thirty minutes before that hour. For appointments: De afspraak is om drie uur (The appointment is at three o'clock). Twelve uur is noon, middernacht is midnight. Dutch often uses the 24-hour clock in writing — 15.30 for 3:30 PM — so learn to read it: achttien uur = 6 PM.

Days of the week (never capitalized in the middle of a sentence, but often written with a capital at the start): maandag, dinsdag, woensdag, donderdag, vrijdag, zaterdag, zondag. Use op for on: op maandag = on Monday, op maandag as a habit = on Mondays. Months: januari, februari, maart, april, mei, juni, juli, augustus, september, oktober, november, december. Dates: vijf mei, twintig oktober. To say what day it is: Welke dag is het vandaag? — Het is dinsdag. What is the date? Wat is de datum? — Het is twee november. Note the order: day first, then month — 2 november, never november 2. Today is vandaag, tomorrow is morgen, yesterday is gisteren, this week is deze week, next week is volgende week, last week is vorige week.

Practical money and shopping vocabulary, since numbers live there: Hoeveel kost dit? (How much does this cost?), Dit kost tien euro (This costs ten euros), Dat is te duur (That is too expensive), Dat is goedkoop (That is cheap), Heeft u wisselgeld? (Do you have change?), Mag ik de rekening? (Can I have the check?). In shops you will hear prices as bare numbers — vijfenveertig for 45 — so number fluency pays off immediately. Dutch shops often round cash payments to the nearest five cents, and many places prefer cards, so learn Kan ik met een kaart betalen? (Can I pay by card?). The euro is the currency, and prices are written with a comma for decimals: 2,50 euro.

Dialogue — at a market:

Verkoper: Goedemorgen! Wat wilt u? — Good morning! What would you like?
Klant: Ik wil graag twee kilo appels, alstublieft. — I would like two kilos of apples, please.
Verkoper: Dat is twaalf euro. Nog iets? — That is twelve euros. Anything else?
Klant: Hoeveel kosten de sinaasappels? — How much are the oranges?
Verkoper: Vijf euro per kilo. — Five euros a kilo.
Klant: Geef me drie kilo. Hoe laat sluit u? — Give me three kilos. What time do you close?
Verkoper: Wij sluiten om acht uur. — We close at eight.
Klant: Dank u. Heeft u wisselgeld van twintig? — Thanks. Do you have change for twenty?
Verkoper: Ja, natuurlijk. — Yes, of course.

Practice: Count aloud from 0 to 100 without stopping — including the backwards 21s, 30s, and 40s — then backwards from 50 to 0. Write today's date, your birthday, and three appointment times in Dutch. Then drill time: say the current time in Dutch every 15 minutes for two hours (Hoe laat is het? — Het is...), paying special attention to the half-hour forms. Finally, memorize the market dialogue's price lines and role-play a shopping trip where you buy three different items, asking Hoeveel kost dit? each time.
