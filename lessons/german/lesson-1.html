This lesson teaches you to pronounce German clearly from the start. German spelling is remarkably consistent: once you learn the sound rules, you can read almost any word correctly. Your goal in this lesson is not a perfect accent — it is clarity. If your vowels are clean and your consonant clusters are crisp, German speakers will understand you even while you are still learning words.

Start with the pure vowels, which are shorter and steadier than English vowels. A sounds like 'ah' in father: danke (DAHN-kuh, thank you). E sounds like 'eh' in bed: Bett (beht, bed). I sounds like 'ee' in see: Liebe (LEE-buh, love). O sounds like 'oh' in go, but shorter: Brot (broht, bread). U sounds like 'oo' in moon: gut (goot, good). Keep every vowel short and pure — do not let them glide into a second sound the way English habits push you to. A very common beginner mistake is reading German vowels with English values — for example, saying 'mein' (mine) like the English word 'main' instead of 'mine'. When in doubt, keep vowels short.

Now the umlauts — ä, ö, ü — the sounds that make German sound like German. Ä sounds like 'eh': Mädchen (MEHD-khen, girl). Ö: round your lips as if to say 'oh' but say 'eh' instead — schön (shurn, beautiful). Ü: round your lips as if to say 'oo' but say 'ee' instead — Tür (tewr, door), grün (grewn, green). These are the famous hard sounds; practice them daily by alternating pairs: schon (already) versus schön (beautiful), Mutter (mother) versus Mütter (mothers). The letter combination EI sounds like the English 'eye': mein (mine), klein (small). IE is the reverse — a long 'ee': Liebe, Bier (beer). EU and ÄU sound like 'oy': heute (HOY-tuh, today), Häuser (HOY-zer, houses).

German consonants are crisp and firm. The CH has two sounds: after a, o, u it is a throaty scrape from the back of the throat — Bach (bahkh), Buch (bookh); after e, i, and umlauts it is a soft hiss like a cat — ich (ikh, I), Milch (milk). The R is rolled or gargled in the throat — rot (roht, red); a light throat gargle is perfectly acceptable and standard. W sounds like English V: Wasser (VAH-ser, water). V sounds like English F: Vater (FAH-ter, father). Z sounds like 'ts': Zeit (tsite, time). S before a vowel sounds like English Z: Sonne (ZON-nuh, sun); but SS and ß (Eszett) are a sharp S: Straße (SHTRAH-suh, street), Wasser. SCH is 'sh': schön, Schule (SHOO-luh, school). SP and ST at the start of a word add the sh: sprechen (SHPREH-khen, to speak), Stadt (shtat, city). J sounds like English Y: ja (yah, yes). Finally, German devoices consonants at the end of words: Tag (day) ends with a K sound, not G; Hund (dog) ends with a T sound, not D.

Word stress in German usually falls on the FIRST syllable of native words: DANke, GUTen Tag, WASser, MORgen. This is the opposite of French and one of the first things that makes your German sound right. Longer compound words stress the first part: HAUSaufgaben (homework), BAHNhof (train station). Trust first-syllable stress for everyday words and your rhythm will sound natural.

Here is a short dialogue using only sounds from this lesson. Read it aloud slowly:

Anna: Guten Tag! Wie heißen Sie? (GOO-tun tahk vee HIGH-sun zee) — Good day! What is your name?
Lukas: Ich heiße Lukas. Und Sie? (ikh HIGH-suh LOO-kahs oont zee) — My name is Lukas. And you?
Anna: Ich heiße Anna. Freut mich. (ikh HIGH-suh AH-nah froyt mikh) — My name is Anna. Pleased to meet you.
Lukas: Freut mich. Sprechen Sie Deutsch? (froyt mikh SHPREH-khen zee doytsh) — Pleased to meet you. Do you speak German?
Anna: Ja, ein bisschen. (yah ine BIS-khen) — Yes, a little.

Say the whole dialogue five times today; it is your first German conversation.

Practice: Write out these ten words and say each aloud three times, exaggerating the umlauts and CH sounds: schön, Tür, Mädchen, ich, Buch, sprechen, Wasser, Zeit, Straße, heute. Next, practice the pairs schon/schön, Mutter/Mütter, mein/meine until the differences are obvious. Finally, read the dialogue above aloud five times, then cover the pronunciation hints and read it again — you should be able to produce the sounds from the spelling alone.
