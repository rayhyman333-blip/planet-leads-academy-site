This lesson teaches positioning and messaging: how to explain what makes your offer different in a way that matters to the customer, and how to turn that into words you can use everywhere. Positioning is the place your offer occupies in the customer's mind compared with the alternatives. Messaging is how you express that position in plain language. Together they answer the two questions every customer asks, usually without saying them aloud: why should I choose you, and why should I believe you? If you cannot answer those two questions clearly, your marketing will be a louder version of everyone else's.

Start with the alternatives, because positioning only exists relative to something else. List the real alternatives your customer considers — not just direct competitors, but the other ways they solve the problem. A tutoring service competes with other tutors, but also with doing nothing, with free study guides, and with a parent helping at the kitchen table. A meal-delivery service competes with other delivery services, but also with cooking at home and with picking up food on the way home. For each alternative, note what it does well and where it falls short for your primary customer. Your position lives in the gap: the thing your customer wants that the alternatives do not deliver well. A tutoring service might be the only one that explains concepts in plain language and checks understanding after every session. A bakery might be the only one in the neighbourhood that bakes in small batches every morning. The gap must matter to the customer — being different in a way nobody cares about is not positioning.

Turn the gap into a positioning statement you can use internally. A simple template: for a named customer group, who face a named problem, our offer is the option that delivers a named outcome, because of a named reason to believe. Fill it in: "For busy parents who need weeknight dinners, our meal-planning email is the option that delivers five 30-minute recipes plus a shopping list every Sunday, because every recipe is tested in a real home kitchen and takes under thirty minutes." That sentence is too long for an advert, but it is exactly what you need internally: it forces you to name the customer, the problem, the outcome, and the proof. If you cannot fill in the reason to believe, your position is a wish, not a position. Proof can be a process, a guarantee you can keep, a detail about how you work, or a specific feature that produces the outcome — anything concrete that makes the claim believable.

Then turn the statement into messages for real use. Your core message is one or two sentences that name the customer's situation, the outcome, and one clear next step: "Tired of the same five weeknight dinners? Get five new 30-minute recipes plus a shopping list every Sunday — join free." Adapt it to the format: shorter for social posts, longer for a web page, personal for email. Keep the customer's words from Lesson 2 — the exact phrases people use to describe their problem are the strongest words in your message. Test every message with a simple check: could a stranger read it and repeat back who it is for, what they get, and what to do next? If any of those three is unclear, the message is not ready.

Two mistakes to avoid. The first is claiming everything: "the cheapest, the fastest, the highest quality, and the friendliest." A business that claims every advantage is believed on none of them. Pick one main difference and one supporting difference, and let the rest be ordinary. The second mistake is copying a competitor's message. If your words could belong to any business in your field, they belong to none of them — and customers will choose on price alone, because you gave them nothing else to choose on. Your message should sound like you, in your customer's language, naming the specific outcome you deliver.

Practice: Complete the positioning work for your running example. (1) List three real alternatives your customer considers, including doing nothing, and note what each does well and where it falls short. (2) Write your positioning statement using the template above, filling in the customer, the problem, the outcome, and the reason to believe. (3) Write three versions of your core message: one sentence for a social post, three sentences for an email, and one paragraph for a web page. (4) Read each version to someone else if you can, and ask them to repeat back who it is for, what they get, and what to do next. Rewrite anything they cannot repeat.
