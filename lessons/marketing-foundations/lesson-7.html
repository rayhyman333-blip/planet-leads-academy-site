This lesson teaches measurement: tracking what your marketing does so you can do more of what works and stop spending time — or money — on what does not. By the end you will set up basic tracking, read the few numbers that matter, run simple comparisons between approaches, and build a monthly review habit. Marketing without measurement is guesswork: you keep doing what feels productive instead of what is productive, and the two are rarely the same. Measurement does not need to be complicated or expensive. For a beginner, a notebook and a spreadsheet are enough.

Start tracking before you need the data, because you cannot measure retroactively. The essentials are simple: a web analytics tool on your site showing visitors, where they came from, and what they did; your email provider's built-in reports (opens, clicks, unsubscribes); each social platform's native insights (reach, saves, follows per post); and a simple spreadsheet where you log weekly numbers by hand. That hand-logged sheet is underrated — fifteen minutes every Monday copying six numbers into a table builds the habit of looking, and the trend column you add next to each number is where insights live. Also label your links: add simple labels to the links you share in different places (email versus social versus content) so your analytics can tell you which channel brought each visitor. Set this up in week one of any marketing effort; adding tracking later means flying blind through the most instructive period.

Five numbers matter; the rest is context. Visitors by source tells you which channels actually send people, so effort follows results instead of hunches. Conversion rate — the share of visitors who take your key action, such as joining your list — tells you whether your pages do their job; track it per source, because email visitors converting well while social visitors barely convert changes where you invest. Email engagement (opens and clicks trending, unsubscribes per send) tells you whether your list still wants to hear from you. Social engagement per post (saves, shares, replies) tells you which content your audience values enough to act on. And enquiries or sales attributed to marketing — even roughly, through labelled links and asking new customers how they found you — closes the loop between activity and business. Everything else (follower counts alone, raw page views, likes) is a vanity number: nice to see, dangerous to steer by.

Compare approaches with simple tests instead of debates. Change one thing at a time: two subject lines to two halves of your list, two headlines on your signup page for two weeks each, two post formats with equal effort. Then read the result with basic honesty: the winner is the one with more of the outcome you want, by a margin big enough that it probably was not chance — a near tie over a tiny sample proves nothing, while a consistent gap over hundreds of people means something. You do not need advanced statistics for beginner tests; you need the discipline of one variable at a time and samples big enough to trust. Write down what you tested and what you learned in one line each — a running log of tests becomes a real advantage within a year, because you stop re-testing what you already know.

The monthly review turns numbers into decisions in one hour. Pull up your weekly log and ask four questions: what grew (do more of it next month), what shrank (fix it or cut it), what surprised you (investigate — surprises are where learning hides), and what will you test next month (one test, named in advance). Then update your plan: shift one block of weekly time from the weakest channel to the strongest, and schedule the test. This review is the difference between marketers who improve and marketers who repeat year one five times. Bring your Lesson 1 triangle to the review too — when a channel underperforms, the cause is usually the customer, the offer, or the message rather than the channel itself.

Two mistakes to avoid. The first is drowning in dashboards: tracking forty numbers guarantees you act on none. Six numbers in a spreadsheet you actually open beats a beautiful dashboard you never check. The second is confusing coincidence with cause in your own data: email signups rose the week you changed the subject line — but it was also a holiday week. Note alternative explanations before declaring victory, and re-test the change in a normal week. Honest measurement sometimes says "we do not know yet," and that answer protects you from confident wrong decisions.

Practice: (1) Set up your tracking on paper: list the tools or manual logs you will use for website, email, and social, and write the six numbers you will log every Monday. (2) Build the weekly log spreadsheet with columns for each number plus a trend note. (3) Fill two weeks with plausible sample numbers and practise the monthly review: write what grew, what shrank, what surprised you, and your one test for next month. (4) Design one real test you could run next week — name the single variable, the two versions, how you will split the audience, and what result would convince you. Save the log template; Lesson 8's plan runs on it.
