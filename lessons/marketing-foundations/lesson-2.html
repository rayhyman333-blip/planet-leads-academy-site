This lesson teaches you to describe your customer in concrete detail, because every marketing decision — where to show up, what to say, what to offer — depends on knowing who you are talking to. By the end you will write a customer profile that a stranger could read and use to judge whether a message, a piece of content, or a channel fits. A good customer profile is not a stereotype or a guess about an entire age group. It is a specific, observable picture of a real kind of person: what their days look like, what problem they are trying to solve, what they have already tried, and what a good outcome would look like for them.

Start with the situation, not the demographics. Age and location can matter, but they rarely explain why someone buys. What explains it is the situation: a new parent who needs dinner on the table in twenty minutes, a small business owner who loses track of invoices and dreads tax season, a student who failed a subject and needs to pass it this term. Write the situation in two or three sentences, as if describing one actual person. Give that person a name if it helps — a fictional name for a typical customer makes the profile easier to use, because you can ask "would Maya care about this?" instead of debating abstractions. Then add three things: the problem they feel most keenly, the outcome they actually want, and what they have already tried that did not work. That last item is gold: if they tried a cheap fix and it failed, they are ready to hear about a better one, and you know exactly which objection to address.

Gather your picture from observation and conversation, not from imagination alone. If you already have customers, listen to what they actually say: the words they use to describe their problem are the words your marketing should use. Read the questions people ask you, the complaints they repeat, and the reasons they give for choosing you over an alternative. If you have no customers yet, talk to people who match your imagined customer and ask open questions: what is frustrating about this right now, what have you tried, what would a good solution look like, and how would you describe it to a friend? Take notes in their words. Five such conversations will teach you more about your customer than weeks of guessing. Write down exact phrases people use — "I just want it to stop leaking" is a better headline than any slogan you invent in a meeting room.

Next, sort your customers into groups, because one business usually serves more than one kind of person, and each kind needs a different message. A bakery serves the weekday commuter who wants a quick breakfast, the parent ordering a birthday cake, and the office manager ordering pastries for a meeting. Same bakery, three different situations, three different messages, three different places to reach them. Pick the one or two groups that matter most — the ones you can serve best and who are easiest to reach — and make those your primary audience. The others can wait. Trying to speak to all three at once produces a message that fits none of them. Your primary group is the one your Lesson 8 plan will be built around.

Two mistakes to avoid. The first is the imaginary customer: describing who you wish bought from you instead of who actually does. If your real buyers are busy parents and your message chases teenagers, nothing else in the course will work, because the foundation is wrong. Check your profile against real people you have observed or spoken with, and correct it when the evidence disagrees with your guess. The second mistake is stopping at demographics: "women aged 25 to 45" is not a customer profile, because it says nothing about what those people need, fear, or want. Demographics can help you choose where to reach people, but the situation and the problem are what make the message land.

Practice: Build your customer profile using the worksheet in the materials. Write a one-paragraph profile of your primary customer group: situation, problem, desired outcome, and what they have tried before. Then write a second paragraph for a secondary group, and one sentence explaining which group is primary and why. Finally, list five questions you would ask a real person from your primary group to check whether your profile is accurate, and write down the exact phrases you expect to hear — then, if you can, ask one real person two of those questions and note what they actually say.
