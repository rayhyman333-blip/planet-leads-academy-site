This lesson teaches you the basic rock beat: the groove that sits underneath a huge share of popular music. The rock beat is built from three parts working together - the hi-hat keeping steady time, the snare landing on beats two and four, and the bass drum anchoring beats one and three. Learn it slowly and cleanly, and you will have a beat you can play with real songs by the end of this lesson.

Build the beat one layer at a time. Start with the hi-hat alone: play steady quarter notes with your right hand, one hit per beat, counting "1, 2, 3, 4" out loud. Keep the stick height low and even, and listen for a consistent tick on every beat. Do this for one full minute without adding anything else. Next, add the snare: keep the hi-hat going and play the snare on beats two and four, with your left hand. Those two snare hits are called the backbeat, and they are the heartbeat of the groove - they should be the loudest, clearest sound in the beat. Practice hi-hat plus snare for two minutes, counting aloud, and notice how the backbeat makes the pattern feel like music instead of a ticking clock.

Now add the bass drum. Keep the hi-hat and snare going, and play the bass drum on beats one and three with your right foot. The coordination is the challenge: your right foot plays on one and three while your left hand plays on two and four, so the limbs alternate in a steady cycle. If it falls apart, slow down. Play the full beat at a tempo so slow it feels almost silly, and only speed up when the slow version is solid. A useful trick is to say the pattern out loud while you play it: "bass, snare, bass, snare" in time with the beat, so your voice keeps the limbs organized. When the three layers lock together, you will feel the groove click into place - the pattern starts to drive itself.

The bass drum foot needs its own technique. Start with heel-down playing: rest your whole foot on the pedal, keep your heel on the floor, and press the pedal with the ball of your foot, letting the beater strike and fall back. Heel-down gives you control and a softer sound, which is right for learning. As you get comfortable, you can try heel-up: lift your heel off the floor and press with the whole foot, which gives more power. Either way, the beater should rebound off the drumhead after each stroke rather than being buried into it - a buried beater chokes the sound and slows the next stroke. Practice ten bass drum strokes alone, listening for a full, round tone on each one.

Time for drills. First, the layer builder: play one minute of hi-hat alone, one minute of hi-hat plus snare, then two minutes of the full beat, all at the same slow tempo. Second, the counting test: play the full beat while counting "1, 2, 3, 4" out loud for two minutes without stopping, keeping every limb on its assigned beat. Third, the tempo ladder: set a metronome to a slow pulse, play the beat for one minute, then raise the tempo by a small step and play another minute - stop raising the tempo the moment the beat starts to wobble, and stay at the last clean tempo.

Two common mistakes. The first is rushing the tempo: beginners speed up the moment the beat feels good, and the groove falls apart within a few measures. The metronome is your honesty check - if you drift ahead of it, you are rushing. The second is a weak backbeat: the snare on beats two and four should crack, not whisper. Give the snare a confident stroke with a slightly higher stick lift than the hi-hat, so the backbeat cuts through the groove.

Practice: Play the basic rock beat - hi-hat quarter notes, snare on two and four, bass drum on one and three - for five minutes at a slow, steady tempo with a metronome, counting aloud. Keep a written tally of clean measures versus measures with a mistake, and aim for twenty clean measures in a row before you stop. Finish by playing the beat for one full minute with your eyes closed, trusting your limbs to hold the pattern.
