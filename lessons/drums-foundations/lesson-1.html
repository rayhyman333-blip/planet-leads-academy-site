This lesson teaches you how to hold drumsticks, sit at the kit, and set up your drums so that playing feels natural from the very first beat. Everything later in this course - steady beats, clean fills, and playing along to songs - builds directly on the physical habits you set up today. Good grip and posture prevent the two problems that stop most beginners: tension and soreness that make practice unpleasant, and stiff habits that cap how fast and cleanly you can play later. Give this lesson a full practice session on its own. It may feel like you are not really playing music yet, but this foundation work pays off every single time you sit down at the drums.

Start with the sticks. This course uses matched grip, where both hands hold the sticks the same way. Pick up a stick in your right hand and find the balance point, roughly one third of the way up from the butt end. Rest the stick in the web between your thumb and index finger, with the thumb pressing gently on top of the stick and the index finger curled underneath it. This thumb-and-index partnership is called the fulcrum, and it is the pivot the stick turns on. The remaining three fingers wrap loosely around the stick - they guide it and catch it on the rebound, but they do not squeeze it. Do the same with the left hand, mirroring the shape exactly. Hold the sticks out in front of you and shake your hands loose: the sticks should feel like an extension of your hands, not like objects you are strangling.

Now the stroke. A drum stroke is not a hit from the shoulder; it is a small, relaxed motion that starts in the wrist. Let the stick rest on the drumhead, lift it about the height of your hand using your wrist, and let it drop back down. The stick should bounce back up on its own after it strikes - that rebound is free energy, and good drumming is mostly about letting the stick do the work. Practice slow single strokes, right hand then left hand, alternating evenly: right, left, right, left. Listen for an even sound on every stroke, and watch your hands: both should rise to the same height and fall at the same speed. If one hand sounds louder than the other, it is usually the hand that is gripping tighter, so loosen it and try again.

Next, sit at the kit. Sit on the drum throne with both feet flat on the floor or resting lightly on the pedals, and your knees bent at roughly a right angle. If your knees sit well above your hips you are too low, and if your legs dangle you are too high. Sit tall through your back without locking it stiff, and slide forward or back until you can reach every drum without leaning. The snare drum sits between your knees, tilted slightly toward you, at about waist height. The hi-hat sits to your left, close enough that your right stick crosses comfortably over to it. The bass drum is straight ahead of your right foot, and the floor tom sits to your right. Everything should be within easy reach - if you have to stretch or twist to reach a drum, move the drum, not your body.

Time for drills. First, the fulcrum check: hold both sticks in matched grip, drop them onto a pad or snare, and let them rebound ten times each hand, checking that the thumb and index finger stay in control while the other fingers stay loose. Second, the alternating singles: play right-left-right-left on the snare at a slow, steady pace for two full minutes, counting the strokes out loud in groups of four. Third, the height ladder: play four strokes with the sticks rising just a finger-width off the head, then four strokes rising a hand-width, then four strokes rising higher still, then back down - this teaches you to control volume with stick height, which is the basis of dynamics on the drums.

Two common mistakes to watch for. The first is the death grip: beginners squeeze the sticks so hard that the rebound dies, the sound gets choked, and the hands tire within minutes. If your forearms ache, you are gripping too hard. Shake your hands out completely loose, then rebuild the grip with only enough pressure to keep the stick from flying away. The second is playing from the shoulders: big arm swings look dramatic but they are slow, tiring, and uneven. Keep your elbows hanging relaxed at your sides and let the wrists do the work - your shoulders should barely move.

Practice: Sit at the kit or pad and rebuild your grip and posture from scratch. Play alternating single strokes, right-left-right-left, for three minutes at a slow, steady pace, counting in groups of four. Before every minute, run the checklist: fulcrum in place, fingers loose, wrists doing the work, back tall, feet flat. Finish with the height ladder drill twice through, ending on the softest strokes, so your last sound of the session is quiet and controlled.
