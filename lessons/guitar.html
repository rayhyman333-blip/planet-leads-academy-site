Lesson 1: Holding the Guitar, Tuning Up, and Your First Notes

This lesson teaches you how to hold the guitar, tune it, and play your first notes. Everything in this course - chords, strumming, songs - depends on the guitar being in tune and sitting stably in your hands. A surprising number of beginners fight their instrument for weeks because it was never tuned properly or held securely. Spend this whole lesson on setup: by the end you will hold the guitar comfortably, tune all six strings, and play clear open-string notes.

Holding the guitar (these instructions assume a right-handed player; left-handed players mirror everything). Sit in a straight-backed chair with both feet flat on the floor. Rest the waist of the guitar - the narrow curved middle of the body - on your right thigh, and pull the body gently against your torso. The neck should angle slightly upward, with the headstock roughly at shoulder height. Your right forearm rests on the upper edge of the body near the elbow, and your right hand falls naturally over the strings near the soundhole. Your left hand supports the neck with the thumb resting lightly on the back of the neck, roughly behind the fingers. The guitar should feel balanced, not like you are wrestling it - if the neck dives toward the floor, pull the body closer to you.

Tuning. The six strings, from thickest to thinnest, are E, A, D, G, B, and high E - remember them with "Eddie Ate Dynamite, Good Bye Eddie." Use an electronic clip-on tuner: it is inexpensive, fast, and accurate, and there is no virtue in tuning by ear as a beginner. Pluck one string at a time and turn its tuning peg slowly while watching the tuner display - turn one way and the pitch rises, the other way and it falls. Tune up to the note rather than down to it, and always pluck the string again after turning the peg to check. New strings stretch and slip out of tune quickly, so expect to retune several times in your first weeks - that is normal, not a broken guitar.

Your first notes: the open strings. Pluck each string one at a time with your right-hand thumb or a pick, starting from the thick low E and moving to the thin high E, saying each string's name out loud: E, A, D, G, B, E. Listen for a clean, ringing tone that lasts - a dull thud means you brushed a neighboring string or barely touched it. Next, your first fretted notes: press the low E string down at the first fret with your left index finger, right behind the metal fret wire (not on top of it, not halfway to the next fret), and pluck. That is F. Move the same finger to the second fret for F sharp, then the third for G. Press just hard enough for a clean note - white knuckles mean too much force.

Time for drills. First, the tuning round: tune all six strings with the tuner, then play them open from low to high naming each one, five times through. Second, the open-string ladder: pluck E-A-D-G-B-E then reverse E-B-G-D-A-E, ten times, aiming for an even volume on every string. Third, the first-fret workout: on the low E string, play open, first fret, second fret, third fret, and back down, with the index finger only, eight times slowly - this is your first taste of fretting, so keep the thumb relaxed on the back of the neck.

Two common mistakes. The first is pressing the string halfway between frets, which produces a buzzing, dead sound. Always fret just behind the wire - close to it, not on it. The second is squeezing the neck too hard: your thumb and fingers clamp down, your hand tires in minutes, and your wrist aches. If your hand hurts, you are pressing too hard. Ease off until the note just barely rings clean, and memorize that minimum pressure.

Practice: Tune all six strings, then play the open-string ladder (low to high and back) naming each string aloud, five times. Then play the first-fret workout on the low E string eight times slowly. Finish by retuning - check every string once more with the tuner, because playing pulls new strings slightly out of tune.

Lesson 2: Reading Chord Diagrams - Your First Chords (E Minor, A Major)

This lesson teaches you to read chord diagrams and play your first two chords: E minor and A major. Chords are the heart of guitar playing - strum a chord and you are already making music that sounds like songs. Reading a diagram takes five minutes to learn, and these two chords use simple shapes that train your fingers for everything ahead.

How to read a chord diagram. The diagram is a picture of the guitar neck standing upright: the vertical lines are the six strings (low E on the left, high E on the right) and the horizontal lines are the frets, with the thick top line representing the nut at the top of the neck. A dot on a string tells you which fret to press and usually carries a number telling you which finger to use: 1 is the index, 2 the middle, 3 the ring, 4 the little finger. An O above a string means play it open (no finger), and an X means do not play that string at all. Before touching the guitar, study each diagram until you can point to every finger placement and name the string and fret.

E minor first. The diagram shows: low E open (O), A string 2nd fret with finger 2, D string 2nd fret with finger 3, and G, B, high E open. Place finger 2 on the A string just behind the second fret wire, and finger 3 right beside it on the D string at the same fret. Strum all six strings slowly from low to high. Now A major: the diagram shows an X on the low E (skip it), then A open, D string 2nd fret finger 1, G string 2nd fret finger 2, B string 2nd fret finger 3, high E open. Crowd those three fingers together on the second fret - they will feel cramped at first, and that is normal. Strum from the A string down, five strings only.

The clarity check is the most important habit in this lesson. After forming each chord, pluck every string individually, slowly, and listen: each string should ring clearly. If one buzzes or sounds muted, look at your fingers - usually a fingertip is leaning against a neighboring string, or a finger is sitting too far from the fret wire. Adjust by a millimeter, not a mile: roll the fingertip more upright so it presses with the very tip, and nudge closer to the fret. A chord is only learned when every string rings.

Time for drills. First, the clarity check drill: form E minor, pluck each string individually, fix any dead string, then strum the full chord - repeat ten times. Do the same with A major, ten times. Second, the slow switch: change from E minor to A major and back, giving yourself a full four counts to move, twenty switches total. Do not worry about speed - clean shapes matter, and speed comes later. Third, the eyes-closed test: form each chord, look away, strum, and judge by ear whether it sounds clean.

Two common mistakes. The first is the leaning finger: your fingertip touches the string next to it and mutes it, so the chord sounds thin. Curl your fingers more so they come down vertically onto the strings, like little hammers. The second is placing fingers in the middle between frets, which causes buzzing. Slide each finger up until it sits just behind the fret wire.

Practice: Do the clarity check drill for both chords (ten clean repetitions each), then twenty slow switches between E minor and A major with four counts per change. End by strumming each chord four times in a row - down-strums only - listening for a full, ringing sound on every strum. Finish with the string-aim test: strum E minor but deliberately miss the low E string, then strum it fully, alternating five times. This trains your strumming hand to aim accurately, a skill every later lesson depends on.

Lesson 3: Strumming - Patterns, Rhythm, and Timing

This lesson teaches you strumming: how to move your right hand across the strings in steady rhythmic patterns. Chords give you the notes; strumming gives you the groove. A great strum pattern can make two simple chords sound like a real song, while stiff, arrhythmic strumming makes even fancy chords sound amateur. By the end of this lesson you will play three core strumming patterns in time.

The mechanics. Hold the pick between your thumb and the side of your index finger, with only a small tip of the pick sticking out - gripping too much pick makes the strum clumsy and loud. Keep your wrist loose: the strumming motion comes from the wrist (with a little forearm), not from the elbow swinging like a pendulum and not from stiff fingers. Strum downstrokes with a relaxed downward brush across the strings, and upstrokes with a light upward flick - upstrokes should be quieter and touch fewer strings, mostly the top three or four. Rest your forearm lightly on the guitar body and let the wrist do the work; if your shoulder gets involved, you are using too much arm.

The patterns, counted in 4/4 time. Pattern one, the foundation: four down-strums, one per beat - counted "1, 2, 3, 4." Play it on E minor until it is metronome-steady. Pattern two: down, down-up, down, down-up - counted "1, 2-and, 3, 4-and." The "and" is the upstroke falling between beats. Pattern three: continuous down-up strumming on every eighth note - down-up-down-up-down-up-down-up, counted "1-and-2-and-3-and-4-and." Learn the patterns in order, and do not move to the next until the current one stays steady for two full minutes.

Timing is everything. Practice with a metronome at a slow tempo - slow enough that you never rush. Play pattern one on E minor for two full minutes without stopping, then switch to A major for two minutes. Then pattern two, same deal. The goal is not speed but unbroken steadiness: if you stumble, do not stop and restart - keep the right hand moving and rejoin on the next beat, because real playing never gets a restart button. Count out loud at first ("1-and-2-and..."), then let the counting go internal once it is solid.

Once a pattern is steady, add accents - slightly emphasizing certain beats. In pattern one, accent beat one: play the first down-strum of each measure a touch louder and the other three lighter, counted "ONE, 2, 3, 4." This single change makes the rhythm feel alive instead of mechanical. Try the same with pattern two, accenting beats one and three. Then practice dynamic control: play pattern three very softly for four measures, then loudly for four, then softly again - keeping the tempo identical throughout. Beginners often speed up when playing loud and drag when playing soft; holding the tempo steady across volume changes is an advanced skill disguised as a simple exercise.

Two common mistakes. The first is a stiff wrist: beginners lock the wrist and strum from the elbow, producing a harsh, uneven sound and a tired arm. Shake your hand loose, slow down, and strum as if brushing dust off the strings. The second is strumming too hard: digging the pick deep between the strings catches and jerks the rhythm. The pick should skim the surface - you want a light, even brush, with the upstrokes noticeably lighter than the downstrokes.

Time for drills. First, the two-minute steadiness drill: pattern one on E minor, metronome slow, two minutes unbroken. Second, the pattern ladder: play pattern one for four measures, pattern two for four, pattern three for four, then back down, all on one chord, three times through. Third, the chord-change strum: play pattern two, changing from E minor to A major every four measures, for five minutes - this merges your Lesson 2 chord changes with your new strumming.

Practice: Set the metronome slow. Play pattern two on E minor for two minutes, then A major for two minutes, counting aloud. Then play the pattern ladder (patterns 1-2-3-2-1, four measures each) twice through on E minor. Finish with five minutes of pattern two while switching chords every four measures.

Lesson 4: The Essential Open Chords (G, C, D, E Minor, A Minor)

This lesson teaches you the five essential open chords: G, C, D, E minor, and A minor. With these five shapes you can play thousands of songs in the keys of G, C, D, and A minor - this is the working vocabulary of the rhythm guitarist. You already know E minor; now you add the other four and start changing between all of them.

The shapes, one at a time, with the clarity check after each. G major: low E 3rd fret with finger 2, A string 2nd fret with finger 1, high E 3rd fret with finger 3, and D, G, B open - strum all six strings. C major: low E is not played (X), A string 3rd fret finger 3, D string 2nd fret finger 2, B string 1st fret finger 1, and G and high E open - strum five strings, skipping the low E. D major: low E and A are not played (X), D open, G string 2nd fret finger 1, B string 3rd fret finger 3, high E 2nd fret finger 2 - strum the four thinnest strings. A minor: low E is not played (X), A open, D string 2nd fret finger 2, G string 2nd fret finger 3, B string 1st fret finger 1, high E open - strum five strings.

Learn them in this order: Em (already known), Am, C, G, D. For each new chord: study the diagram, place the fingers one at a time, run the clarity check by plucking each string individually, adjust anything that buzzes or mutes, and then strum. Spend at least five minutes per chord before moving on. Say the chord name and its finger numbers out loud as you place them - naming wires the shape into memory faster than silent repetition.

Now the changes. The one-minute change drill is the fastest known way to build switching speed: pick two chords, set a timer for one minute, and count how many clean changes you can make, strumming once after each change to verify. Only clean changes count. Do Em to Am, then Am to C, then C to G, then G to D, then D to Em. Write down each score. Repeat the whole circuit daily and watch the numbers climb - when every pair is above thirty clean changes per minute, your hands know the shapes.

Speed up your changes with the hover technique. When you lift your fingers to change chords, do not pull them far off the strings - instead, let them hover just above the strings in roughly the shape of the next chord, as if the shape were a shadow waiting to land. Practice this in slow motion: form G, then lift all fingers only a centimeter, reshape them mid-air into the C shape, and set them down together. Do this shape-in-the-air move ten times between each chord pair before you start the one-minute drill. Changes built this way stay fast even under pressure, because the fingers never travel farther than they have to.

Two common mistakes. The first is the flying-fingers habit: when changing chords, beginners lift all fingers high off the neck and rebuild the shape from scratch. Keep your fingers close to the strings during changes - the less they travel, the faster the change. Look for pivot fingers: positions that barely move between two chords should glide, not lift. The second is the death grip: squeezing harder during changes because the new shape feels insecure. Tension slows you down - if your forearm burns, loosen up and slow the drill.

Time for drills. First, the circuit: one-minute changes for each of the five pairs above, recording your scores. Second, the progression loop: play G-C-D-Em, one strum per chord, slowly, ten times through - this is a real four-chord song progression. Third, the blind change: form each chord, close your eyes, lift your fingers one inch, and reform the shape by feel, five times per chord.

Practice: Run the full one-minute-change circuit (all five pairs) and record your scores. Then play the G-C-D-Em loop with pattern two strumming from Lesson 3, four beats per chord, for five minutes. Finish with the blind-change drill on your two weakest chords.

Lesson 5: Switching Chords Smoothly + Your First Full Song

This lesson turns your chords and strumming into your first full song. You will learn the techniques that make chord changes sound smooth - anticipation, pivot fingers, and rhythmic continuity - and then play an original two-part song written for this course, "First Light," which uses only the chords you know. Playing a complete song start to finish is a milestone: it is the moment practice becomes music.

Smooth switching is about what happens before the change, not during it. Anticipation: on the final beat before a chord change, your fretting hand should already be moving - the change happens in the last fraction of the beat so the new chord lands exactly on beat one. Practice this deliberately: play G for three beats, and on beat four lift and move while the right hand keeps strumming. Pivot fingers: identify fingers that barely move between two chords and let them guide the hand - for example, moving from Em to Am, your middle and ring fingers shift together as a unit. Rhythmic continuity: the right hand never stops. Even if the left hand is late, the strum keeps going - a slightly late chord in rhythm sounds fine, while a stopped strum sounds broken.

Now the song. "First Light" has two parts. The verse is Em - C - G - D, four beats each, played twice through. The chorus is C - G - D - Em, four beats each, played twice. The strumming pattern is pattern two from Lesson 3 (down, down-up, down, down-up). The structure is: verse, verse, chorus, verse, chorus, chorus. Before playing it whole, drill each chord change in the song with the one-minute change method from Lesson 4 - especially C to G and G to D, which are the trickiest pairs here.

Build it in stages. Stage one: play the verse chord loop with one simple down-strum per chord, four times through, no pattern yet - just clean changes in time. Stage two: add pattern two strumming to the verse, four times through. Stage three: the same process for the chorus. Stage four: play the full structure start to finish, slowly, without stopping. Only when the whole song is clean at a slow tempo do you bring it up to a comfortable speed. Record yourself and listen back: you are checking for steady rhythm and clean changes, not perfection.

Two common mistakes. The first is stopping the strumming hand during changes: the moment the left hand struggles, the right hand freezes. Train the right hand to be stubborn - it keeps its pattern no matter what the left hand does. If a chord is not ready, strum the open strings for that beat and land the chord on the next one; in rhythm, nobody notices. The second is practicing the song only from the beginning: the ending then stays shaky forever. Start your practice from the chorus sometimes, or from the second verse, so every section gets equal work.

Time for drills. First, the anticipation drill: play any two-chord change with the move starting on beat four, twenty repetitions. Second, the non-stop strum: play the verse progression with pattern two for three minutes, and if you fumble a change, keep strumming and rejoin - no stopping allowed. Third, the section shuffle: play the song's sections in random order (chorus first, then verse) twice through, so no section depends on what came before it.

Practice: Play "First Light" all the way through, slowly, three times without stopping - verse, verse, chorus, verse, chorus, chorus. Use pattern two strumming throughout. Record the third run and listen back, writing down the one chord change that needs the most work, and drill that change for five minutes. Then play the song a fourth time, this time starting from the chorus, to prove every section stands on its own. End by playing just the verse progression with your eyes closed once - if the

changes stay clean, the shapes are truly in your hands.

Lesson 6: Single Notes - The Minor Pentatonic Scale

This lesson teaches you single-note playing through the minor pentatonic scale - the five-note scale behind countless melodies and solos in rock, blues, and folk music. Until now every note you played was part of a chord; now your fingers learn to play one note at a time with precision and even timing. This is also your introduction to playing up the neck, away from the open chords you know.

The shape. We will use the A minor pentatonic in fifth position, which means your index finger lives around the fifth fret. On each string you play two notes; the pattern across the six strings (low to high) is: 5th-8th frets, 5th-7th, 5th-7th, 5th-7th, 5th-8th, 5th-8th. The fingering rule for single-note playing: one finger per fret - index covers the 5th fret, middle the 6th, ring the 7th, little finger the 8th. So on the low E string you play 5 (index) then 8 (little finger); on the A string, 5 (index) then 7 (ring); and so on up across all six strings, then back down. Play it ascending and descending slowly, saying each fret number out loud at first.

Picking. Use alternate picking: downstroke, upstroke, downstroke, upstroke, one pick stroke per note, strictly alternating. It feels awkward at first and then becomes automatic - and it is the technique that makes fast, even single-note lines possible later. Steady your picking hand lightly: rest the heel of your palm near the bridge or let your pinky brush the pickguard, whichever feels stable, but keep the wrist loose and the motion small. Each note should sound for its full value with no accidental string noise - if neighboring strings ring, mute them by resting unused fretting fingers lightly against them.

Making music with it. A scale is a tool, not a song - so here is your first improvisation exercise. Set a slow metronome pulse, or simply count a steady beat, and play any notes from the scale in any order, four notes per beat-group, for two minutes. There are no wrong notes inside this scale over an A minor or C major backing - everything you play will sound intentional. Try repeating small groups of notes (motifs), leaving gaps of silence, and ending phrases on the 5th fret of the high E string (the note A, home base). This is how solos are born: not from speed, but from simple ideas played with feeling.

Two common mistakes. The first is uneven timing: the stretch from the 5th to the 8th fret takes longer than the step from the 5th to the 7th, so the rhythm lurches. Slow down until every note lands exactly on its beat, and give the wide stretches extra isolated repetitions. The second is collapsing finger joints: pressing with flat, buckled fingers instead of curved fingertips kills both speed and tone. Watch your fretting hand in a mirror - the fingers should arch over the strings like a small bridge.

Time for drills. First, the slow climb: play the full scale ascending and descending, alternate picking, at a slow metronome tempo, six times through. Second, the stretch isolation: on the low E string only, play 5-8-5-8 twenty times, then the high E string the same, training the wide index-to-little-finger stretch. Third, the two-minute improvisation: play freely within the scale over a steady pulse, focusing on simple repeating motifs rather than speed.

Practice: Play the scale ascending and descending six times at a slow tempo with strict alternate picking. Then improvise within the scale for three minutes over a steady beat, using at least three repeating motifs and ending each phrase on the A note (5th fret, high E string). Finish with the stretch isolation drill, twenty repetitions per string. As a final test, play the scale ascending only, then immediately improvise for one minute without stopping. Switching between the disciplined scale and free playing without pausing is exactly what real soloing feels like.

Lesson 7: Finger Exercises and an Intro to Barre Chords

This lesson builds finger strength and independence with targeted exercises, then introduces barre chords - the movable shapes that unlock every key on the guitar. Open chords only work in a few keys; a barre chord is a shape you can slide up and down the neck to play any chord anywhere. Barres are famously hard at first and famously worth it, so this lesson gives you a patient, step-by-step path.

The finger exercises. First, the spider: on the low E string play frets 1-2-3-4 with fingers 1-2-3-4, then move to the A string and repeat, crawling across all six strings and back - like a spider walking. Keep each finger pressed down until its string is finished; the goal is independence, not speed. Second, the stretch: play the 1st fret with the index and the 4th fret with the little finger on each string, back and forth ten times per string - this builds the span barres need. Third, the lift-off: fret any note, play it, then lift the finger exactly one millimeter and play the muted click, then press back down - ten times per finger. This trains the minimum pressure that produces a clean note, which is the secret to endurance.

The barre technique, step by step. Start with a half-barre at the fifth fret: lay your index finger flat across the top three strings (high E, B, G) at the fifth fret and press. The finger should be straight but not locked, rolled very slightly onto its bony outer edge rather than the fleshy underside, with the thumb planted on the back of the neck roughly behind the index finger for counter-pressure. Pluck each of the three strings: all must ring. If the middle string buzzes, it is sitting in a finger joint crease - roll the finger slightly or shift it a hair up or down until the crease moves off the string.

Now the full F barre at the first fret - the classic first barre chord. Barre all six strings at the first fret with the index finger, then add the E-major shape with fingers 2, 3, and 4 (middle on the G string 2nd fret, ring on the A string 3rd, little finger on the D string 3rd). This is hard: the first fret needs the most pressure on the whole neck. Build up to it over days, not minutes - practice the half-barre at the fifth fret (much easier) until it is clean, then slide the same shape to the third fret, then the first. Five minutes of barre practice per day beats one exhausting half-hour session; your hand needs recovery time to build strength.

Two common mistakes. The first is squeezing the whole neck with the thumb and fingers in a full-hand clamp: the barre needs firm, focused pressure from the index finger against the thumb, not a death grip. If your forearm burns within a minute, you are squeezing - reset with less force and find the minimum that rings clean. The second is placing the barre finger flat in the middle of the fret: like all fretting, the barre works best just behind the fret wire, and a finger parked midway between frets will buzz no matter how hard you press.

Time for drills. First, the spider across all six strings and back, three times slowly, keeping fingers down. Second, the half-barre ladder: a clean half-barre at the 5th fret (ten clean plucks), then 4th, then 3rd, then 2nd, moving down as each position rings clearly. Third, the five-minute barre: set a timer and practice the full F shape in short bursts - press, check each string, release and shake out, repeat - stopping the moment real pain (not effort) appears.

Practice: Do the spider three times and the stretch drill on every string. Then practice the half-barre ladder from the 5th fret down to the 2nd, getting ten clean plucks at each fret. Finish with five minutes of short-burst F barre practice, shaking your hand out between every attempt.

Lesson 8: Your Practice Routine + Five Songs to Learn Next

This lesson gives you a permanent practice routine and five songs to learn next. You now own the complete beginner toolkit: tuning, open chords, strumming patterns, smooth changes, single-note scales, and the beginnings of barre chords. What turns this toolkit into real musicianship is the same thing as in every skill: consistent, well-structured practice. This lesson builds that structure and points it at music you will love playing.

The routine has four blocks in a thirty-minute session. Block one, warm-up (five minutes): tune up, play the open-string ladder, then the spider exercise once through - waking the hands up. Block two, technique (ten minutes): rotate daily through chord-change drills, the pentatonic scale, and barre practice; work on whatever is weakest, with the metronome, slowly. Block three, songs (ten minutes): your current song, built in sections, never mindless full run-throughs - pick the worst section and drill it. Block four, fun (five minutes): play anything you enjoy, even imperfectly, and end every session wanting to come back tomorrow. The order is fixed: warm-up, technique, songs, fun.

The weekly plan. Play five or six days a week - thirty focused minutes beats a weekend marathon, because your hands and brain consolidate learning between sessions. Give each day a flavor: Monday, chord changes and the one-minute drill circuit; Tuesday, strumming patterns with the metronome; Wednesday, the pentatonic scale plus ten minutes of improvisation; Thursday, barre chord practice; Friday, song work - record yourself and listen back; weekend, one light session or rest. Keep the practice log from your materials: date, minutes, blocks completed, and one honest line about what improved. Review the log monthly - the progress you forget is the motivation you lose.

Five songs to learn next, each teaching something new. One, a 12-bar blues in A (using A7, D7, and E7 shapes): teaches seventh chords and the classic blues form that underpins rock and blues rhythm playing. Two, a three-chord folk strum in G (G-Em-C-D): teaches the world's most common progression with a new strumming pattern. Three, a minor-key arpeggio piece in Am: teaches picking individual strings inside chords, a gentle first step into fingerstyle. Four, a pentatonic melody piece: a simple composed melody using only the A minor pentatonic scale, teaching phrasing with single notes. Five, a barre-chord rocker in F (F to B flat to C): your first song built on movable barre shapes, proving the Lesson 7 work pays off. Learn them one at a time, one per two weeks, with the section method from Lesson 5, and record each one when it is clean.

Two common mistakes. The first is abandoning structure once the course ends: without the four blocks, practice dissolves into noodling on favorite chords. Keep the routine for at least three months - it is the difference between a player and someone who used to play. The second is collecting songs instead of finishing them: starting five songs and completing none teaches less than perfecting one. Finish each of the five songs - clean, in time, recorded - before moving to the next.

Time for drills. First, the routine rehearsal: do one full thirty-minute session today following the four blocks exactly, timing each block. Second, the song start: learn the A7, D7, and E7 shapes for the 12-bar blues today, using the clarity-check method from Lesson 2, ten clean repetitions each. Third, the baseline: run the one-minute-change circuit on your five weakest chord pairs and write down the scores to beat next month.

Practice: Complete one full thirty-minute session using the four-block routine. Fill in the practice log honestly. Then learn the A7, D7, and E7 shapes, running the clarity check on each ten times. Write down your baseline one-minute-change scores for your five weakest chord pairs. Finally, write your one-month goal at the top of the log in a single sentence, such as "Play First Light cleanly at full speed." Read it before every session - it turns thirty unfocused minutes into thirty minutes with a destination.
