Lesson 1: Sounds, Alphabet, and Greetings

Portuguese spelling is largely regular, but its sounds include nasal vowels and soft consonants that English speakers must practise deliberately. The alphabet has 26 letters, with *k, w,* and *y* used mainly in borrowed words. The five vowels — a, e, i, o, u — each have open and closed variants, and nasalisation changes meaning: *pão* (bread) is nasal, *pau* (stick) is not; *mãe* (mother) carries a nasal *ã*. Nasal vowels are marked by a tilde (*ã, õ*) or by a following *m* or *n* at the end of a syllable: *bom* (good), *bem* (well), *sim* (yes), *ontem* (yesterday), *cantar* is not nasal in the same way, but *cantam* (they sing) is. Practise pairs: *avó* (grandmother, open *o*, with accent) versus *avô* (grandfather, closed *o*), *sede* (thirst/headquarters) versus *sêde* in older spelling contexts, and *pão, mão* (hand), *coração* (heart).

Consonants: *c* before *a, o,* or *u* sounds like *k* (*casa, comer*), before *e* or *i* like *s* (*cena, cinema*) — in European Portuguese the *s* sound is sharper, in Brazilian Portuguese it is softer, but the distinction is the same. The letter *ç* (cedilla) always sounds like *s*: *faço* (I do/make), *coração*. The letter *g* before *a, o,* or *u* is hard (*gato*), before *e* or *i* it sounds like the *s* in "measure" (*gelo, girafa*). The letter *j* has that same sound: *janela* (window). The letter *h* at the start of a word is silent: *hora* (hour/time), *homem* (man). The letter *r* varies: at the start of a word or doubled (*rr*) it is a strong throaty or rolled sound depending on region (*rato, carro*), while a single *r* between vowels is a light tap (*caro*, expensive, versus *carro*, car — a minimal pair worth practising). The letter *s* between vowels sounds like *z* (*casa*), at the end of a word it sounds like *s* in Brazil but like *sh* in much of Portugal (*dois, mas*). The letter *x* has several sounds: *sh* in *xícara* (cup), *ks* in *táxi*, *z* in *exemplo*, *s* in *próximo* — learn it word by word. Stress usually falls on the second-to-last syllable, with written accents marking exceptions: *cidade, café, ônibus* (Brazilian spelling for bus; European Portuguese uses *autocarro*).

Greetings: *Bom dia* (good morning), *Boa tarde* (good afternoon), *Boa noite* (good evening at arrival, and good night at parting). *Olá* is a friendly general hello; *Oi* is informal, very common in Brazil. *Tchau* is an informal goodbye; *Adeus* is more formal or final. Formality uses *o senhor / a senhora* (you, formal, literally "the gentleman/lady") with third-person verb forms in Portugal, while Brazil commonly uses *você* with third-person verb forms in most everyday contexts. Asking how someone is: *Como vai?* (formal/neutral), *Como vai você?* (Brazil), *Como está?* (formal, Portugal), *Tudo bem?* (All well? — extremely common, informal and neutral). Reply: *Tudo bem, obrigado/a* (All well, thanks — *obrigado* masculine speaker, *obrigada* feminine speaker), *E você?* Introductions: *Meu nome é Ana* (My name is Ana), *Chamo-me Ana* (Portugal, literally "I call myself Ana"), *Prazer* (Pleased to meet you), *De onde você é? / Donde é?* (Where are you from?), *Sou do Brasil / Sou de Lisboa* (I am from Brazil / I am from Lisbon).

A common mistake is ignoring nasalisation, turning *pão* into *pau* and changing the meaning entirely. Another is pronouncing *r* the English way everywhere; practise the tap between vowels and the stronger sound at the start. Learners also confuse *bom dia* with a general daytime greeting; after midday, switch to *boa tarde*.

**Practice exercise:** Read these aloud slowly: *pão, mão, casa, carro, caro, janela, coração, obrigado/obrigada*. Then write a five-line introduction using *Bom dia*, your name, where you are from, and a *Tudo bem?* exchange, once with *você* forms and once noting the *senhor/senhora* alternative, and mark every nasal vowel.

Lesson 2: Ser, Estar, Ter, and Describing Yourself

Portuguese has two verbs for "to be," and choosing between them is a core skill. *Ser* is used for identity, origin, profession, inherent characteristics, time, and material: *Eu sou professora* (I am a teacher), *Ele é brasileiro* (He is Brazilian), *São duas horas* (It is two o'clock), *A casa é de pedra* (The house is made of stone). *Estar* is used for location, temporary states, feelings, and conditions: *Estou em casa* (I am at home), *Estou cansada* (I am tired, feminine), *O livro está na mesa* (The book is on the table), *Estamos felizes* (We are happy, in the sense of feeling happy right now). The distinction is about permanence versus state, not a rigid rule; *É bom* (It is good, inherent quality) versus *Está bom* (It is fine/okay, as an agreement or current state) shows how the choice changes meaning.

Present tense of *ser*: eu sou, você/ele/ela é, nós somos, vocês/eles/elas são. (The *tu* form, *tu és*, is used in Portugal and parts of Brazil; this course uses *você* forms as the neutral default and notes *tu* where it matters.) Present tense of *estar*: eu estou, você/ele/ela está, nós estamos, vocês/eles/elas estão. Present tense of *ter* (to have): eu tenho, você/ele/ela tem, nós temos, vocês/eles/elas têm. Note the accent distinguishing *tem* (singular) from *têm* (plural). Examples: *Tenho um irmão* (I have a brother), *Temos dois filhos* (We have two children), *Você tem um carro?* (Do you have a car?).

Adjectives agree in gender and number: *bonito/bonita/bonitos/bonitas* (beautiful), *alto/alta/altos/altas* (tall), *grande/grandes* (big, same for both genders in the singular, *-s* in the plural), *feliz/felizes* (happy). Nationality adjectives follow the same pattern: *inglês/inglesa/ingleses/inglesas* (English). Most descriptive adjectives follow the noun: *uma casa grande, um carro novo*. A few, like *bom, grande* (in the sense of great), and *novo* in some contexts, can come before: *um bom dia, um grande amigo* (a great friend, not necessarily a physically large friend).

Age uses *ter*, not *ser*: *Tenho trinta anos* (I am thirty years old, literally "I have thirty years"). States like hunger, thirst, cold, heat, fear, and sleepiness also use *ter* or *estar com*: *Tenho fome* (I am hungry), *Tenho sede* (I am thirsty), *Estou com frio* (I am cold), *Estou com medo* (I am afraid), *Estou com sono* (I am sleepy). Translating these with *ser* or *estar* alone is the most common beginner error.

Numbers 1–20: um, dois, três, quatro, cinco, seis, sete, oito, nove, dez, onze, doze, treze, catorze (Portugal) / quatorze (Brazil), quinze, dezasseis (Portugal) / dezesseis (Brazil), dezassete / dezessete, dezoito, dezanove / dezenove, vinte. *Um* becomes *uma* for feminine nouns (*uma casa*), and *dois* becomes *duas* (*duas casas*). Spelling differences between European and Brazilian Portuguese are noted where they affect a beginner word, but neither is "wrong."

**Practice exercise:** Write ten sentences about yourself and a friend: five with *ser* or *estar* (choosing deliberately and explaining why in a note), and five with *ter*, including age, a description, and something possessed. Then rewrite two *estar* sentences as *ser* sentences and explain how the meaning changes or why it becomes incorrect.

**Extra worked examples — describing people:** *Meu irmão é alto e gentil* (My brother is tall and kind), *Minhas irmãs são simpáticas e inteligentes* (My sisters are nice and intelligent — note *simpáticas* with *-as* and *inteligentes* with *-es*, because adjectives ending in *-e* take *-es* in the plural). *Nós temos uma casa pequena mas acolhedora* (We have a small but welcoming house). Opposites: *jovem/velho* (young/old), *rico/pobre* (rich/poor), *feliz/triste* (happy/sad), *forte/fraco* (strong/weak). When you describe a mixed group, the masculine plural wins: one brother and one sister are *altos*, not *altas*. Origin uses *de* or *ser de*: *Sou de São Paulo* (I am from São Paulo), *Ela é de Lisboa* (She is from Lisbon). Asking age: *Quantos anos você tem?* (How old are you?) and answering *Tenho vinte anos* — remember age always uses *ter*, so *Eu sou vinte anos* is one of the clearest markers of a beginner.

Lesson 3: Regular Verbs in the Present Tense

Portuguese verbs fall into three conjugations: *-ar* (falar, to speak), *-er* (comer, to eat), and *-ir* (partir, to leave). The present tense covers both "I speak" and "I am speaking" in most everyday use, though *estar + gerund* (*estou falando*) marks an action in progress, especially in Brazil. Learn one model per group and you unlock hundreds of verbs.

For *-ar* verbs, *falar* gives: eu falo, você/ele/ela fala, nós falamos, vocês/eles/elas falam. Others: *morar* (to live) — moro, mora, moramos, moram; *trabalhar* (to work) — trabalho, trabalha, trabalhamos, trabalham; *comprar* (to buy) — compro, compra, compramos, compram; *estudar* (to study) — estudo, estuda, estudamos, estudam. For *-er* verbs, *comer* gives: eu como, você/ele/ela come, nós comemos, vocês/eles/elas comem. Others: *vender* (to sell) — vendo, vende, vendemos, vendem; *aprender* (to learn) — aprendo, aprende, aprendemos, aprendem; *escrever* (to write) — escrevo, escreve, escrevemos, escrevem. For *-ir* verbs, *partir* gives: eu parto, você/ele/ela parte, nós partimos, vocês/eles/elas partem. Others: *dormir* (to sleep) — durmo (note the vowel change *o → u* in the first person singular), dorme, dormimos, dormem; *abrir* (to open) — abro, abre, abrimos, abrem; *assistir* (to watch/attend) — assisto, assiste, assistimos, assistem.

Negation is simple: put *não* before the verb. *Não falo português* (I do not speak Portuguese), *Não entendo* (I do not understand), *Não moro aqui* (I do not live here). Questions need no word-order change: raise intonation, or add a question word. *Você fala português?* (Do you speak Portuguese?), *Onde você mora?* (Where do you live?), *O que você faz?* (What do you do?). Question words: *quem* (who), *o que* (what), *onde* (where), *quando* (when), *por que* (why), *como* (how), *quanto/a* (how much). Note *por que* (two words, with accent on *que* in some positions) for questions, *porque* (one word) for answers: *Por que você estuda português? Porque gosto da língua* (Why do you study Portuguese? Because I like the language).

Reflexive verbs, used for daily routines, add a pronoun: *levantar-se* (to get up) — *Eu me levanto, Você se levanta, Nós nos levantamos, Vocês se levantam* (Brazilian order puts the pronoun before the verb; European Portuguese often puts it after in affirmative statements: *Levanto-me*). Others: *chamar-se* (to be called): *Chamo-me Ana* (Portugal) / *Me chamo Ana* (Brazil); *vestir-se* (to get dressed), *deitar-se* (to go to bed), *lembrar-se de* (to remember). Pronoun placement differs by region, and both orders are understood; beginners should pick one consistent pattern and recognise the other.

A common mistake is keeping the English pronoun in every sentence when the verb ending already shows the person, though Portuguese speakers do use pronouns more often than Spanish speakers, especially with *você*. Another is mixing conjugations: *falam* for *-ar*, *comem* for *-er*, *partem* for *-ir* — the third-person plural ending changes by group. A third is using *tu* endings with *você*: *você fala*, not *você falas*.

**Practice exercise:** Conjugate *morar, aprender,* and *partir* in full, then write a paragraph about your daily routine using at least six verbs, including one reflexive verb and one negative sentence. Swap the subject from *eu* to *nós* throughout and check each ending again.

**Extra worked examples — verbs in conversation:** A short dialogue: *— O que você faz de manhã? — Eu me levanto às sete, tomo café da manhã e vou para o trabalho. — E à noite? — Normalmente leio um livro ou assisto a um filme.* (What do you do in the morning? I get up at seven, have breakfast and go to work. And at night? I usually read a book or watch a film.) Note *tomar café da manhã* (literally 'to take breakfast') is the standard Brazilian phrase, while Portugal says *tomar o pequeno-almoço*. Irregular present forms to meet early: *ir* (vou, vai, vamos, vão), *fazer* (faço, faz, fazemos, fazem), *dizer* (digo, diz, dizemos, dizem), *vir* (venho, vem, vimos, vêm), *poder* (posso, pode, podemos, podem), *querer* (quero, quer, queremos, querem), *saber* (sei, sabe, sabemos, sabem). Use *poder* for ability and permission, *querer* for wants, and *saber* for knowing facts or how to do something, each followed by an infinitive where relevant: *Posso entrar? Quero aprender português. Sei nadar.*

Lesson 4: Articles, Nouns, and Gender

Every Portuguese noun has a gender — masculine or feminine — and the article must match it. Learn every new noun with its article: *o livro* (the book), *a caneta* (the pen), *o estudante, a amiga*. Definite articles (the): masculine singular *o*, feminine singular *a*, masculine plural *os*, feminine plural *as*. Indefinite articles (a/an): masculine singular *um*, feminine singular *uma*, masculine plural *uns*, feminine plural *umas* — the plurals mean "some": *uns livros* (some books), *umas casas* (some houses). Before a vowel, articles do not shorten as in Italian or French; *o amigo, a amiga* keep their full forms.

Noun endings give strong clues. Most nouns ending in *-o* are masculine (*o menino, o carro*), most ending in *-a* are feminine (*a menina, a casa*), most ending in *-ção, -são, -dade, -tude* are feminine (*a estação, a cidade, a liberdade*), most ending in *-ma* of Greek origin are masculine despite the *-a* (*o problema, o sistema, o tema* — important exceptions). Nouns ending in *-e* can be either (*o café* is masculine in the sense of coffee, *a noite* feminine; *o leite* masculine, *a gente* feminine). Plurals: add *-s* to nouns ending in a vowel (*casa/casas, livro/livros*), *-es* to nouns ending in *-r, -s,* or *-z* (*flor/flores, país/países, rapaz/rapazes*), and change *-m* to *-ns* (*homem/homens, bom/bons*). Nouns ending in *-ão* have three plural patterns, learned word by word: *-ãos* (*mão/mãos, irmão/irmãos*), *-ães* (*pão/pães, alemão/alemães*), *-ões* (*estação/estações, coração/corações*) — the last is the most common.

Possessives agree with the thing possessed, not the possessor, and usually keep the article: *o meu livro* (my book), *a minha caneta* (my pen), *os meus amigos, as minhas amigas*. Forms: *meu/minha/meus/minhas* (my), *teu/tua/teus/tuas* (your, informal *tu*), *seu/sua/seus/suas* (your, *você*, and also his/her/their — context disambiguates), *nosso/nossa/nossos/nossas* (our), *vosso/vossa* (your plural, mainly Portugal), *deles/delas* (their, clearer than *seu* when ambiguity matters). With family members, Brazilian Portuguese often drops the article (*minha mãe, meu pai*), while European Portuguese often keeps it (*a minha mãe, o meu pai*) — both are correct in their region.

Prepositions combine with articles into contractions that are obligatory, not optional: *de + o = do, de + a = da, de + os = dos, de + as = das; em + o = no, em + a = na, em + os = nos, em + as = nas; a + o = ao, a + a = à, a + os = aos, a + as = às; por + o = pelo, por + a = pela*. Examples: *Vou ao mercado* (I go to the market), *O livro está na mesa* (The book is on the table), *Venho do Brasil* (I come from Brazil), *Gosto do café* (I like coffee). City names usually take no article (*Vou a Lisboa, Moro em São Paulo*), but country names often do (*o Brasil, a França, os Estados Unidos*) — with exceptions like *Portugal* and *Angola*, which take no article in the standard construction (*Vou a Portugal*).

**Practice exercise:** Take twenty nouns, write each with its definite and indefinite article in singular and plural, and then write ten sentences using *de, em, a,* and *por* with contractions, including two country names and two city names. Circle any noun whose gender or plural pattern surprised you and memorise it with its article.

**Extra worked examples — articles in real sentences:** *O gato está no sofá* (The cat is on the sofa — *em + o = no*), *A chave está na bolsa* (The key is in the bag — *em + a = na*), *Venho do médico* (I come from the doctor — *de + o = do*), *Falo com os amigos* (I speak with friends — *com + os* stays *com os* in modern Portuguese). Mass nouns and abstract nouns often take the article where English drops it: *Gosto da música* (I like music), *O amor é importante* (Love is important), *O pão está fresco* (The bread is fresh). But after *de* in a general sense, the article may drop: *Preciso de pão* (I need bread) versus *O pão que comprei está bom* (The bread I bought is good). The rule of thumb is: specific, known thing — article; general, unspecified quantity — often no article after *de*. When in doubt in a shop, a quantity phrase from Lesson 6 (*um pouco de, um quilo de*) is the safe, natural choice.

Lesson 5: The Past Tense — Pretérito Perfeito

Portuguese tells completed past actions mainly with the *pretérito perfeito*, formed with a single word whose endings change by conjugation. For regular *-ar* verbs, *falar* gives: eu falei, você/ele/ela falou, nós falamos, vocês/eles/elas falaram. Note that *nós falamos* is identical in the present and the past for *-ar* verbs; context and time expressions disambiguate. For *-er* verbs, *comer* gives: eu comi, você/ele/ela comeu, nós comemos, vocês/eles/elas comeram. For *-ir* verbs, *partir* gives: eu parti, você/ele/ela partiu, nós partimos, vocês/eles/elas partiram. Other regular examples: *comprar → comprei, comprou, compramos, compraram; vender → vendi, vendeu, vendemos, venderam; abrir → abri, abriu, abrimos, abriram*.

Irregular verbs are extremely common and must be memorised: *ser/ir* share the same past forms — *fui, foi, fomos, foram* (context tells you whether it means "was" or "went": *Fui ao mercado* means "I went to the market," *Fui professor* means "I was a teacher"); *ter → tive, teve, tivemos, tiveram; fazer → fiz, fez, fizemos, fizeram; dizer → disse, disse, dissemos, disseram; ver → vi, viu, vimos, viram; dar → dei, deu, demos, deram; saber → soube, soube, soubemos, souberam; vir → vim, veio, viemos, vieram; pôr → pus, pôs, pusemos, puseram*. Examples: *Ontem fiz compras* (Yesterday I did the shopping), *Ano passado fui a Lisboa* (Last year I went to Lisbon), *Vimos um bom filme* (We saw a good film), *Ela disse a verdade* (She told the truth).

Time expressions anchor the past: *ontem* (yesterday), *ontem à noite* (last night), *anteontem* (the day before yesterday), *esta manhã* (this morning), *na semana passada* (last week), *no mês passado* (last month), *no ano passado* (last year), *há dois dias* (two days ago). Negation puts *não* before the verb: *Não falei, Não fui*. Questions keep the same order with rising intonation: *Você comeu? Você foi ao mercado?*

Portuguese also has the *pretérito imperfeito* for habitual or ongoing past actions — *eu falava* (I used to speak / I was speaking), *eu comia, eu partia* — which beginners should recognise even before producing it fluently. The contrast matters: *Quando era criança, falava português em casa* (When I was a child, I used to speak Portuguese at home) uses the imperfect for habit, while *Ontem falei português com um amigo* (Yesterday I spoke Portuguese with a friend) uses the perfect for a completed event. Mixing them is normal at first; the distinction becomes natural with reading and listening.

Common mistakes include using the present *falamos* where the past is meant without a time expression to clarify, confusing *ser* and *ir* past forms (they are identical, so check the sentence meaning), and forgetting the accent that distinguishes *tem* from *têm* in the present — a mistake that carries into past-tense narration when learners switch tenses. Another is translating English "I have gone" as a separate tense; the Portuguese perfect covers both "I went" and "I have gone" in most conversation, with the compound *tenho ido* reserved for repeated or recent relevance.

**Practice exercise:** Write a diary entry for yesterday using ten past-tense verbs — including two irregular verbs and one negative sentence — and then rewrite three of those sentences in the imperfect to describe what you used to do, explaining the change in meaning. Change the subject from *eu* to *nós* throughout and check every ending.

**Extra worked examples — narrating a weekend:** *No sábado de manhã, acordei cedo, tomei café da manhã e saí com os amigos. Visitamos um museu, almoçamos num restaurante pequeno e compramos presentes. À noite voltamos para casa cansados mas felizes.* (On Saturday morning I woke up early, had breakfast and went out with friends. We visited a museum, had lunch in a small restaurant and bought presents. At night we came home tired but happy.) Notice *num restaurante* is *em + um* contracted, and *acordei, tomei, saí, visitamos, almoçamos, compramos, voltamos* are all regular past forms except where noted. Sequence words help narration: *primeiro* (first), *depois* (then), *em seguida* (afterwards), *por fim* (finally), *de repente* (suddenly). Practise retelling the same weekend from a friend's point of view: *Ela acordou cedo, tomou café da manhã…* and watch every verb ending change from first person to third person. Then try the imperfect for background: *Estava sol quando saímos* (It was sunny when we went out) — background weather and setting take the imperfect, while the events themselves take the perfect.

Lesson 6: Food, Ordering, and Quantities

Food vocabulary is practical and culturally rich. Key words: *o pão* (bread), *o queijo* (cheese), *a manteiga* (butter), *a carne* (meat), *o peixe* (fish), *a fruta* (fruit), *os legumes* (vegetables; Brazil also uses *verduras*), *o arroz* (rice), *o feijão* (beans), *a batata* (potato), *a água* (water), *o vinho* (wine), *a cerveja* (beer), *o café* (coffee), *a sobremesa* (dessert), *a conta* (the bill). Meals: *o café da manhã* (breakfast, Brazil) / *o pequeno-almoço* (breakfast, Portugal), *o almoço* (lunch), *o jantar* (dinner), *um lanche* (a snack). At a café, *um café* in Brazil often means a small filtered coffee, while in Portugal *um café* means an espresso and *uma bica* is the Lisbon word for it; *um pingado* (Brazil) or *um garoto* (Portugal) is coffee with a little milk, and *um cappuccino* is understood in both.

Useful phrases: *Gostaria de…* (I would like…) is polite and standard; *Quero…* (I want…) is direct and can sound demanding to a waiter. *Gostaria de uma mesa para dois, por favor* (I would like a table for two, please). *O cardápio, por favor* (Brazil) / *A ementa, por favor* (Portugal) for the menu. *Você tem um cardápio em inglês?* (Do you have a menu in English?) *Vou querer…* (I'll have…, Brazil, very common) or *Vou pedir…* (I'll order…) are natural when deciding. *O que você recomenda?* (What do you recommend?), *É bom?* (Is it good?), *Sou alérgico/a a nozes* (I am allergic to nuts — change the ending for gender), *Sem glúten, por favor* (Gluten-free, please). To pay: *A conta, por favor. Posso pagar com cartão?* (The bill, please. Can I pay by card?)

Quantities use *de* plus the noun, or a measure word: *um pouco de pão* (a little bread), *um quilo de maçãs* (a kilo of apples), *um litro de água* (a litre of water), *uma garrafa de vinho* (a bottle of wine), *duzentos gramas de presunto* (two hundred grams of ham), *uma fatia de bolo* (a slice of cake). In shops: *Me dá…?* (Brazil, informal polite) / *Dá-me…?* (Portugal) — *Me dá trezentos gramas de tomates, por favor?* (Will you give me three hundred grams of tomatoes, please?). The partitive idea is carried by *um pouco de, um quilo de*, not by a separate article as in French or Italian.

Taste and preference adjectives: *bom* (good/tasty), *delicioso, saboroso* (tasty), *doce* (sweet), *salgado* (salty), *picante* (spicy), *amargo* (bitter), *fresco* (fresh), *quente* (hot), *frio* (cold). Liking uses *gostar de*, always with *de*: *Gosto de café* (I like coffee), *Gosto de maçãs* (I like apples), *Você gosta de peixe? Não gosto de azeitonas* (Do you like fish? I don't like olives). Forgetting *de* after *gostar* is the classic error: *Gosto café* is wrong; it must be *Gosto de café*. *Adorar* (to love) works the same way: *Adoro chocolate* — note *adorar* does not take *de*.

Common mistakes: ordering *um leite* expecting a milky coffee; in both Brazil and Portugal that is a glass of milk — ask for *um café com leite*. Another is saying *Quero* to a waiter when *Gostaria de* is expected. A third is using *gostar* without *de*, or confusing *almoço* (lunch) with *pequeno-almoço* (breakfast, Portugal) — in Brazil, *almoço* is lunch and breakfast is *café da manhã*.

**Practice exercise:** Write a full restaurant dialogue for two people: greeting, table request, drinks, mains, a dietary restriction, dessert, and paying. Include *gostaria de, vou querer/vou pedir, gosto de*, a quantity phrase, and a polite question to the waiter. Read it aloud, checking every article, contraction, and adjective ending.

**Extra worked examples — at the market and the café:** At a market stall: *— Bom dia! Me dá meio quilo de cerejas? — Claro. Mais alguma coisa? — Sim, também duzentos gramas de queijo e um pouco de manjericão. — São cinco reais e cinquenta. — Obrigado!* (Good morning! Will you give me half a kilo of cherries? Of course. Anything else? Yes, also two hundred grams of cheese and a little basil. That's five reais fifty. Thank you!) Note *meio quilo* (half a kilo) and *um pouco de* (a little of). In Portugal the same exchange would use *Dá-me* instead of *Me dá* and *euros* instead of *reais*. At a café: *— Um cappuccino e um croissant, por favor. — Para beber aqui ou para levar? — Para beber aqui, obrigado.* (A cappuccino and a croissant, please. To drink here or to take away? To drink here, thanks.) Other useful items: *o croissant, o pão de queijo* (Brazilian cheese bread, a must-try), *o suco de laranja* (Brazil) / *o sumo de laranja* (Portugal) for orange juice, *a água sem gás* (still water) versus *a água com gás* (sparkling water).

Lesson 7: Future, Plans, and the Weather

Portuguese expresses the future in two main ways, and the informal one dominates speech. The most common spoken future uses *ir* (to go) in the present plus the infinitive: *Vou estudar português* (I am going to study Portuguese / I will study Portuguese), *Vamos viajar amanhã* (We are going to travel tomorrow), *Ela vai chegar às oito* (She is going to arrive at eight). Present of *ir*: eu vou, você/ele/ela vai, nós vamos, vocês/eles/elas vão. This construction is used for plans, intentions, and near-future events, and beginners should master it first.

The formal future, formed with endings added to the infinitive, is used in writing, formal speech, and predictions: *falar* gives *falarei, falará, falaremos, falarão* (the full set: eu falarei, você/ele/ela falará, nós falaremos, vocês/eles/elas falarão). Similarly *comer → comerei, comerá, comeremos, comerão; partir → partirei, partirá, partiremos, partirão*. Irregular stems: *ser → serei, ter → terei, fazer → farei, dizer → direi, vir → virei, pôr → porei, trazer → trarei*. Examples: *Amanhã farei compras* (Tomorrow I will do the shopping, formal), *Será que vai chover?* (I wonder if it will rain? — *será que* is a common way to express doubt or supposition). The future is also used for probability: *Serão oito horas* (It must be about eight o'clock), *Ele terá trinta anos* (He must be about thirty).

Plans and intentions also use *pretender* (to intend), *pensar em* (to plan to / think of), and *estar prestes a* (to be about to): *Pretendo estudar português* (I intend to study Portuguese), *Penso em viajar sexta-feira* (I plan to travel on Friday), *Estou prestes a sair* (I am about to go out). Days of the week: segunda-feira, terça-feira, quarta-feira, quinta-feira, sexta-feira, sábado, domingo — note the first five are literally "second fair, third fair," and so on, from the church calendar, and they are feminine: *na segunda-feira* (on Monday), *às segundas-feiras* (on Mondays, habitually). Months: janeiro, fevereiro, março, abril, maio, junho, julho, agosto, setembro, outubro, novembro, dezembro.

Weather vocabulary: *Como está o tempo?* (What is the weather like?), *Está calor* (It is hot), *Está frio* (It is cold), *Está bom tempo / mau tempo* (The weather is nice/bad), *Está chovendo* (Brazil) / *Está a chover* (Portugal) for "It is raining," *Está nevando / Está a nevar* (It is snowing), *Está sol* (It is sunny), *Está ventando / Está vento* (It is windy), *Está nublado* (It is cloudy), *Está com neblina / Há nevoeiro* (It is foggy). Seasons: *a primavera, o verão, o outono, o inverno*. Temperatures: *Estão vinte graus* (It is twenty degrees) or *Faz vinte graus*.

Common mistakes include using the formal future for every tomorrow plan where *ir + infinitive* sounds more natural, forgetting that *ir* itself is irregular (*vou, vai, vamos, vão*), and confusing *tempo* (weather/time) with *hora* (hour/time of day): *Que horas são?* asks the time, *Como está o tempo?* asks the weather. Another is forming the formal future from the wrong stem for irregular verbs (*fazerei* is wrong; it is *farei*).

**Practice exercise:** Write your plans for next week, day by day, using five *ir + infinitive* futures, three formal futures (including one irregular stem), and a weather forecast for three days. Then rewrite one plan as a supposition with *será que* and explain the difference between a plan and a guess in Portuguese.

**Extra worked examples — making arrangements:** *— O que você vai fazer no sábado? — No sábado vou visitar minha irmã. No domingo vamos ficar em casa porque vai chover. — E na próxima semana? — Na segunda vou ter uma reunião, na terça vou viajar para Veneza e volto na quinta.* (What are you going to do on Saturday? On Saturday I'm going to visit my sister. On Sunday we'll stay home because it's going to rain. And next week? On Monday I'm going to have a meeting, on Tuesday I'm going to travel to Venice and I'll come back on Thursday.) Note how *ir + infinitive* carries almost every plan here, while the formal future would sound stiff in this chatty context. For invitations, use *gostaria de* or a question rather than a bare future: *Gostaria de convidar você para jantar* (I'd like to invite you to dinner), *Você quer ir ao cinema?* (Do you want to go to the cinema?), *Topa um café?* (Brazil, informal: Fancy a coffee?). Replies: *Com prazer!* (With pleasure!), *Desculpe, não posso* (I'm sorry, I can't), *Talvez outra hora* (Maybe another time). The future of probability appears in small talk: *Ele deve estar cansado* (He must be tired — note *dever* is often used for probability in speech, alongside the formal future).

Lesson 8: Travel, Directions, and Everyday Conversation

Travel Portuguese brings together everything so far: polite requests, numbers, directions, and past and future tenses. Transport words: *o trem* (train, Brazil) / *o comboio* (train, Portugal), *o ônibus* (bus, Brazil) / *o autocarro* (bus, Portugal), *o metrô* (underground, Brazil) / *o metro* (Portugal), *o táxi, o avião* (plane), *a estação* (station), *o aeroporto* (airport), *a plataforma* (platform), *a passagem* (ticket, Brazil) / *o bilhete* (ticket, Portugal), *a ida e volta* (return ticket), *a parada* (stop, Brazil) / *a paragem* (stop, Portugal). Key phrases: *Uma passagem para o Rio, por favor* (A ticket to Rio, please), *A que horas sai o trem?* (What time does the train leave?), *De qual plataforma?* (From which platform?), *É direto?* (Is it direct?), *Onde eu faço baldeação?* (Brazil) / *Onde faço transbordo?* (Portugal) for "Where do I change?", *Quanto tempo dura a viagem?* (How long is the journey?), *Com licença, onde fica a estação?* (Excuse me, where is the station?).

Directions use imperative forms and location words: *à direita* (to the right), *à esquerda* (to the left), *em frente* (straight ahead), *perto de* (near), *longe de* (far from), *em frente a* (opposite), *ao lado de* (next to), *entre… e…* (between), *na esquina* (on the corner). The polite imperative with *você* uses third-person forms: *Vá em frente, depois vire à direita* (Go straight, then turn right), *Pegue a segunda rua à esquerda* (Take the second street on the left). The informal imperative with *tu* differs by region and is less essential for beginners using *você* forms. With *você*, object pronouns come before the verb in Brazil (*Me diga!* — Tell me!) and after, attached, in Portugal (*Diga-me!*).

Everyday conversation fills in with small, high-frequency expressions: *Com licença* (excuse me / may I pass), *Desculpe / Desculpa* (sorry, formal/informal), *Por favor* (please), *De nada* (you're welcome), *Muito obrigado/a* (thank you very much), *Não tem de quê* (don't mention it), *Sinto muito* (I am very sorry), *Não importa* (it doesn't matter), *Está bem / Tudo bem* (okay / that's fine), *Combinado* (agreed), *Tomara!* (Hopefully! / If only!), *Então…* (So… / Well…), *De fato* (indeed / in fact), *De qualquer forma* (anyway). Shopping phrases: *Estou só olhando* (I am just looking), *Posso experimentar?* (Can I try it on?), *Você tem um tamanho maior/menor?* (Do you have a larger/smaller size?), *Quanto custa?* (How much does it cost?), *É muito caro* (It is too expensive), *Vou levar este* (I'll take this one).

Numbers beyond twenty: vinte e um, vinte e dois, vinte e três, vinte e quatro, vinte e cinco, vinte e seis, vinte e sete, vinte e oito, vinte e nove, trinta, quarenta, cinquenta, sessenta, setenta, oitenta, noventa, cem, mil, dois mil. Prices: *Custa dez reais e cinquenta* (Brazil, ten reais fifty) or *Custa dez euros e cinquenta* (Portugal, ten euros fifty). Note *real/reais* (Brazilian currency) versus *euro/euros* (Portugal), and that *real* changes in the plural while *euro* also takes *-s* in Portuguese (*dois euros*).

Common mistakes: asking *Onde fica…?* without *Com licença* first, which can sound abrupt; using *tu* commands with strangers instead of the polite *você* form; and confusing *à direita* with *em frente*. Another is translating "I am looking for" as *Estou procurando por*; Portuguese says *Estou procurando…* or *Procuro…* with no preposition in the standard construction: *Procuro um hotel*.

**Practice exercise:** Write a travel day in Portuguese: arriving at a station, buying a ticket, asking directions to a hotel, checking in, and asking about dinner nearby. Use at least five direction words, three polite requests, one past-tense sentence about what you did, and one future sentence about tomorrow. End with a short dialogue in a shop using *Quanto custa?* and a size question.
