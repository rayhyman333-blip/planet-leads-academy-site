This lesson tackles the most important verb in Hindi — hona (to be) — plus the present tense and the gender system, which lets you describe what you do every day. Hona appears in nearly every sentence, and the present tense is built from a verb stem plus a hona form, so mastering this one pattern unlocks hundreds of sentences. Hindi has two genders — masculine and feminine — and every noun, adjective, and verb ending must agree with the gender of its subject. It feels strange at first, but the patterns are regular and you will learn them by example.

HONA (to be) — memorize this table completely: main hoon (I am), tum ho (you are, informal), aap hain (you are, formal), vah hai (he/she/it is), ham hain (we are), ve hain (they are). Use it for identity, origin, description, profession, and location: Main American hoon (I am American), Vah doctor hai (She is a doctor), Ham Delhi mein hain (We are in Delhi), Ve khush hain (They are happy). Note that Hindi word order is subject-object-verb, so the verb comes last: Main Delhi mein hoon, not Main hoon Delhi mein. Also note that vah is used for both he and she — gender shows up in the verb and adjective endings instead. Aap hain is used with aap even for one person, because respect is grammatically plural in Hindi.

GENDER is the heart of Hindi grammar. Most masculine nouns end in -a: ladka (boy), ghar (house — an exception ending in a consonant but still masculine), kitaab is feminine despite ending in b. Most feminine nouns end in -i: ladki (girl), kursi (chair), nadi (river). Adjectives agree: achha ladka (good boy), achhi ladki (good girl), achhe ladke (good boys), bada ghar (big house), badi kursi (big chair). The masculine plural ending is -e, the feminine plural is usually -iyan or stays the same: ladkiyan (girls). When in doubt about a noun's gender, learn it with the word — say ek ladka, ek ladki — and the gender will stick with the vocabulary. Two high-value patterns use hona with feelings: Mujhe bhookh lagi hai (I am hungry — literally hunger has come to me) and Mujhe pyaas lagi hai (I am thirsty). Learn these as fixed phrases today, along with Mujhe garmi lag rahi hai (I feel hot) and Mujhe thand lag rahi hai (I feel cold).

Now the present tense. Take the verb stem, add -ta for masculine singular, -ti for feminine singular, -te for masculine plural or formal, and then the matching form of hona. Karna (to do): main karta hoon (m, I do), main karti hoon (f, I do), tum karte ho, aap karte hain, vah karta hai (m) / karti hai (f), ham karte hain, ve karte hain. Bolna (to speak): Main Hindi bolta hoon (m, I speak Hindi), Main Hindi bolti hoon (f). Jaana (to go): Main bazaar jaata hoon (m, I go to the market). Rahna (to live/stay): Main Delhi mein rehta hoon (m, I live in Delhi). Kaam karna (to work): Main ek bank mein kaam karta hoon (m, I work in a bank). The pattern never changes — stem plus ta/ti/te plus hona — which makes Hindi verbs wonderfully regular once you know the gender of the subject.

Essential verbs to learn as vocabulary: jaana (to go): main jaata hoon. Aana (to come): main aata hoon. Khaana (to eat): main khaata hoon. Peena (to drink): main peeta hoon. Dekhna (to see/watch): main dekhta hoon. Padhna (to read/study): main padhta hoon. Likhna (to write): main likhta hoon. Sona (to sleep): main sota hoon. Chahna (to want): main chahta hoon. Sakna (to be able to): main kar sakta hoon (m, I can do it), main kar sakti hoon (f). These appear in nearly every conversation, so they repay memorization immediately. Note that sakna follows another verb: Main Hindi bol sakta hoon (m, I can speak Hindi).

Two beginner mistakes to avoid. First, forgetting gender agreement: saying main karta hoon when you are a woman — it must be main karti hoon. The ending must match the speaker, not the listener. Second, putting the verb in the English position: Hindi is subject-object-verb, so Main khaana khaata hoon (I food eat) is correct, while Main khaata hoon khaana is wrong. Time words come early in the sentence: Main aaj bazaar jaata hoon (Today I go to the market), Main kal Delhi jaata hoon (Tomorrow I go to Delhi).

Dialogue — two coworkers at lunch:

Vikram: Aap kahan kaam karte hain? — Where do you work?
Meera: Main ek bank mein kaam karti hoon. Main cashier hoon. Aur aap? — I work at a bank. I am a cashier. And you?
Vikram: Main teacher hoon. Main ek school mein kaam karta hoon. — I am a teacher. I work at a school.
Meera: Kya aap aaj thake hue hain? — Are you tired today?
Vikram: Nahi, main theek hoon. Kya aap mere saath khaana khaana chahti hain? — No, I am fine. Do you want to eat lunch with me?
Meera: Haan, zaroor. Ham restaurant jaate hain? — Yes, of course. Shall we go to the restaurant?
Vikram: Chaliye. Mujhe bhookh lagi hai. — Let us go. I am hungry.

Practice: Conjugate in writing: karna, bolna, jaana, khaana, hona — masculine and feminine singular, all persons. Then write six sentences about yourself: two with hona (identity/origin/profession), two with feeling phrases (bhookh, pyaas, garmi, thand), two with present-tense action verbs. Say each sentence aloud, checking the gender endings. Finally, translate to Hindi: She is a doctor. She works in a hospital. She is tired today. She wants tea. Check: Vah doctor hai. Vah hospital mein kaam karti hai. Vah aaj thaki hui hai. Vah chai chahti hai.
