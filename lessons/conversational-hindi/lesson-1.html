This lesson teaches you to pronounce Hindi clearly from the start, and introduces the Devanagari script so you can recognize the letters you will see on signs, menus, and screens. Your goal in this lesson is not a perfect accent — it is clarity. If your vowels are clean and you can make the retroflex sounds, Hindi speakers will understand you even while you are still learning words. Every Hindi word in this course is written in transliteration — Roman letters — with an English translation, so you can speak from the first page. The Devanagari letters are shown alongside so you can start recognizing them, but you never need them to say a word.

Start with the vowels, which are pure and steady, unlike English vowels that glide. A sounds like the u in but: namaste (nah-mah-STAY, hello), kam (kahm, work). AA is the same sound held longer, like the a in father: naam (naahm, name), baat (baaht, talk). I sounds like the i in bit: din (din, day), dil (dil, heart). II is longer, like ee in see: neend (neend, sleep), jeena (JEE-nah, to live). U sounds like the oo in put: tum (toom, you), sun (soon, listen). UU is longer, like oo in food: door (door, far), bhool (bhool, mistake). E sounds like the ay in day: mera (MAY-rah, my), dekh (daykh, look). AI is a glide, like the i in bike: kaisa (KAY-sah, how), paisa (PAY-sah, money). O sounds like the o in go: mera dost (my friend), bol (bohl, speak). AU is a glide, like ow in cow: aur (owr, and/more), kaun (kown, who). The difference between short and long vowels matters: kam (work) versus kaam (work, with a long aa, also means work but spelled differently) — and din (day) versus deen (religion) are different words entirely. Practice each pair until your ear hears the length difference.

Now the consonants, which Devanagari organizes by where they are made in the mouth — a brilliantly logical system. The throat group (ka, kha, ga, gha): kaam (work), khaana (food), ghar (house), ghaas (grass). The palate group (cha, chha, ja, jha): chai (tea), chha (shade), jaana (to go), jhaad (broom). The retroflex group is the famous one: the tongue tip curls back to touch the roof of the mouth — ta, tha, da, dha, na with a dot underneath in transliteration. Practice: daal (lentils), thanda (cold), ladka (boy), bada (big). English speakers often say these as dental sounds; curling the tongue back even slightly makes a real difference. The dental group (ta, tha, da, dha, na) is made with the tongue on the teeth: tum (you), din (day), naam (name). The lip group (pa, pha, ba, bha, ma): paani (water), phal (fruit), baat (talk), bhaai (brother), mera (my). Then ya, ra, la, va, and the sibilants sha, sa, ha: shaam (evening), saal (year), haan (yes).

Three sound rules will save you constant confusion. First, the aspirated consonants: kha, gha, chha, tha, pha, bha are the same as ka, ga, cha, ta, pa, ba but with a strong puff of air. Hold your hand in front of your mouth: khaana (food) should blow air on your hand, kaam (work) should not. The difference matters: kaal means time or death depending on context, but khaal means skin. Second, the schwa: the short a at the end of a written word is usually dropped in speech. Namaste is written with a final a but said nah-mah-STAY, and Ram is written Raama but said Raam. Third, the nasal sounds: a dot above a vowel or the letters n and m make the vowel nasal — main (I) is said with the vowel through the nose, and gaan (to go) ends with a nasal glide.

The Devanagari script itself is simpler than it looks. It is written left to right, and each letter is a syllable: a consonant carries an inherent a sound unless a vowel sign changes it. The vowels have their own standalone letters and small marks (matras) that attach to consonants: ka becomes ki with the i mark, ku with the u mark, ke with the e mark. Consonants can join into combined letters (conjuncts) when two consonants meet with no vowel between them, as in namaste, where the s and t join. You do not need to write Devanagari to speak Hindi, but recognizing the letters helps you read signs, menus, and messages. Learn to recognize the ten most common letters first: ka, na, ma, ra, ta, ha, sa, pa, ba, ya — they appear in almost every word.

Here is a short dialogue using only sounds from this lesson. Read it aloud slowly:

Anita: Namaste! Aap ka naam kya hai? (nah-mah-STAY, aap kah naahm kyAH hay) — Hello! What is your name?
Rahul: Namaste. Mera naam Rahul hai. Aap ka? (MAY-rah naahm RAH-hool hay, aap kah) — Hello. My name is Rahul. And yours?
Anita: Mera naam Anita hai. Aap se milkar khushi hui. (MAY-rah naahm ah-NEE-tah hay, aap say mil-KAR KHOO-shee hoo-EE) — My name is Anita. Pleased to meet you.
Rahul: Mujhe bhi. Aap Hindi bolte hain? (moo-JAY bhee, aap HIN-dee BOHL-tay hayn) — Me too. Do you speak Hindi?
Anita: Haan, thoda. (haahn, THOH-dah) — Yes, a little.

Say the whole dialogue five times today; it is your first Hindi conversation.

Practice: Write out these ten words, mark the long vowels and retroflex consonants, and say each aloud three times: namaste, khaana, ghar, daal, paani, chai, ladka, thanda, mera, shaam. Next, pair up kam/kaam, din/deen, and sun/soon and say each pair until the short-versus-long difference is obvious. Finally, read the dialogue above aloud five times, then cover the pronunciation hints and read it again — you should be able to produce the sounds from the transliteration alone.
