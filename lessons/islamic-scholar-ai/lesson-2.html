This lesson teaches the six articles of faith (aqidah) that form the foundation of Islamic theology: belief in God, His angels, His books, His messengers, the Last Day, and divine decree. Muslims consider these the non-negotiable core of the religion — the creed that defines what it means to be a believer — and everything else in Islamic practice flows from them. Understanding them clearly is the prerequisite for every later lesson, because Islamic law, worship, and ethics all make sense as responses to these beliefs.

The first article is belief in God (Allah): that there is one God, without partner, who created and sustains everything. Muslims believe God is eternal, all-knowing, all-powerful, and utterly unlike His creation — the Quran's short chapter Al-Ikhlas describes Him as One, eternal, neither begetting nor begotten, with nothing comparable to Him. This strict monotheism (tawhid) is the axis of the entire faith: worship is directed to God alone, and associating partners with Him is considered the gravest wrong. Notice the practical consequence: if God alone is worthy of worship and God alone controls outcomes, then prayer, trust, and gratitude all have exactly one address.

The second article is belief in the angels: beings of light created by God who obey Him perfectly and carry out His commands. Muslims believe angels record human deeds, deliver revelation (Gabriel brought the Quran to Muhammad), and perform functions in the cosmos and in the afterlife. Belief in angels reinforces a Muslim's sense of being observed and accompanied — actions have witnesses, and no good deed goes unrecorded.

The third and fourth articles concern revelation: belief in God's books and His messengers. Muslims believe God sent guidance to humanity through scriptures — including the Torah given to Moses, the Psalms given to David, the Gospel given to Jesus — and that the Quran is the final, preserved revelation confirming what came before. Likewise, Muslims believe God sent messengers to every nation: Adam, Noah, Abraham, Moses, Jesus, and finally Muhammad, whom Muslims regard as the last prophet. This framework explains Islam's relationship with Judaism and Christianity: Muslims honor their prophets and scriptures as genuinely from God, while holding that the Quran is the final and fully preserved message.

The fifth article is belief in the Last Day: that human life does not end at death, that all people will be resurrected and judged, and that the righteous will enter Paradise and the wicked will face punishment. Islamic tradition holds that this belief gives moral weight to every choice — justice may be delayed in this world, but it is certain in the next. It is also a source of hope: suffering borne patiently is not wasted, and no oppressor escapes accountability forever.

The sixth article is belief in divine decree (qadar): that God knows and has decreed all things, while humans still possess real choice and moral responsibility. This is the subtlest article, and scholars have written volumes on how divine knowledge and human freedom coexist. For the beginner, the practical teaching is balance: trust God's decree without fatalism — plan, work, and take the means — and accept outcomes without despair, knowing that what missed you was never meant for you.

A common mistake is treating theology as trivia to memorize rather than beliefs to internalize. The articles of faith are meant to reshape how you see reality: one God worthy of worship, a watched and meaningful life, accountability beyond death. If your study of aqidah does not change your prayer and your character, it has stayed on the surface.

A second common mistake is arguing about theological fine points before the foundations are firm. The classical scholars debated subtle questions for centuries; a beginner who dives into those debates usually ends up confused or partisan. Learn the six articles solidly first — the debates can wait.

Reflection: Write each of the six articles in your own words, in one sentence each, as if explaining them to a curious friend. Then pick the article you find hardest to grasp fully and write a paragraph on why — what questions does it raise for you? Finally, choose one article and write down one concrete way it should change your daily behavior this week (for example: belief in the Last Day might change how you handle a small injustice at work). Keep these notes; you will revisit them when you study ethics in Lesson 7.
