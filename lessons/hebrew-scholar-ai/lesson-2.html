This lesson teaches you how Hebrew reading works: how letters combine into syllables, how vowel marks work, and how to sound out words in pointed text. By the end you will be able to read short Hebrew words aloud, recognize common word patterns, and understand the difference between the consonantal text and the text with vowel points added. Reading is a skill built by practice, not by memorizing rules alone, so work through the exercises slowly and aloud.

Hebrew words are built from syllables, and most syllables follow a simple pattern: a consonant followed by a vowel, or a consonant followed by a vowel and then a closing consonant. The word shalom, meaning peace or wholeness, has two syllables, sha and lom. The word torah has two syllables, to and rah. The word bereshit has three syllables, be, re, and shit. Stress usually falls on the final syllable in Hebrew, which is why transliterations in this course mark the stressed syllable with capital letters, as in sha-LOM and to-RAH. Listening for that final stress will help you recognize words you have heard before and will make your reading sound more natural.

Vowel marks, called niqqud, are small dots and lines placed under, over, or inside letters. They were added to the text centuries after it was written, by scholars called Masoretes who wanted to preserve the traditional pronunciation. The main vowel signs are: a short line under a letter for a sound like a, a dot under a letter for a sound like e, a dot under a letter with a different shape for a sound like i, a dot over a letter for a sound like o, and a dot inside a letter for a sound like u. A dot inside a letter can also mark a doubled consonant, and the letter shin has a dot on one side for sh and on the other side for s. You do not need to memorize every vowel sign before reading: learn the five main ones, and use the glossary and the reading worksheet to check the rest as you meet them.

Most printed Hebrew Bibles use pointed text, with vowel marks throughout, while handwritten scrolls and some modern texts use unpointed text, consonants only. The consonantal text is the older form, and the vowel points are a later aid to reading. This distinction matters for study: when two readings of a verse differ, the difference sometimes comes from how the vowels were understood, and scholars discuss whether the consonants support one reading or another. For your purposes now, pointed text is your friend. Read pointed words slowly, sounding out each syllable, and check your reading against the transliteration in this course until the patterns become automatic.

Three common word patterns will help you recognize words quickly. First, many Hebrew words begin with a prefix: the letter bet means in, the letter lamed means to or for, the letter mem means from, and the letter he at the start of a word can mean the. Recognizing a prefix lets you separate it from the root word that follows. Second, many words end with a suffix: the ending im usually marks a masculine plural, the ending ot usually marks a feminine plural, and the ending ayim marks a pair. Third, the definite article, the Hebrew equivalent of the word the, is the letter he attached to the front of a word. Spotting these patterns turns a long unfamiliar word into a familiar root plus recognizable additions.

Two mistakes slow down new readers. First, trying to read too fast: Hebrew reading rewards slow, syllable-by-syllable sounding out, and speed comes later with practice. Second, ignoring the vowel points and guessing from consonants alone: in pointed text the points are there to help you, so use them, and only move to unpointed reading when your vocabulary is strong enough to supply vowels from context. Read aloud every day, even for five minutes, because hearing the sounds builds memory far better than silent looking.

Practice: (1) Sound out ten words from the glossary, syllable by syllable, writing the transliteration under each word and marking the stressed syllable. (2) Take five words and separate each into prefix, root, and suffix, explaining what each part contributes. (3) Read the first verse of the Hebrew Bible in transliteration, then in pointed Hebrew if you have access to a printed text, noting where the vowel points confirm or surprise your reading. (4) In your journal, list the vowel signs you find hardest to remember, with an example word for each, and copy the reading worksheet from the materials to use in your daily practice.
