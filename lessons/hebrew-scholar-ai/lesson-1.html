This lesson introduces the Hebrew alphabet, called the alef-bet after its first two letters, alef and bet. By the end you will be able to name all twenty-two letters, recognize their shapes, pronounce them, and write them in order. The alphabet is the foundation of everything else in this course: every later lesson assumes you can recognize letters and sound out words, so take your time here and practice until the shapes feel familiar.

Hebrew is written from right to left, the opposite direction from English. That single fact changes how you approach a page: you begin at the right-hand side and move leftward, and a book opens from what an English reader would call the back. Hebrew letters are consonants. The alphabet has twenty-two letters, and most words are built from consonants alone, with vowels supplied by the reader or, in pointed text, by small marks placed around the letters. Five letters have a special final form, a different shape used when the letter ends a word: kaf, mem, nun, pe, and tsade. Learning the final forms early prevents confusion later, because a word ending in mem looks different from a word with mem in the middle.

Learn the letters in small groups rather than all at once. The first group is alef, bet, gimel, dalet, he, vav, zayin. Alef is silent or carries a vowel, and it begins the alphabet and many important words, including the word for God in some contexts and the word emet, meaning truth. Bet sounds like b or v depending on the dot inside it, and it begins the word bereshit, meaning in the beginning, the first word of the Torah. Gimel sounds like g, dalet like d, he like h, vav like v or like a vowel sound, and zayin like z. Practice this group until you can name each letter from its shape alone, then move to the second group: het, tet, yod, kaf, lamed, mem, nun. Het is a throaty h sound, tet sounds like t, yod sounds like y and also serves as a vowel marker, kaf sounds like k or kh, lamed sounds like l, mem sounds like m, and nun sounds like n. The third group is samekh, ayin, pe, tsade, qof, resh, shin, tav. Samekh sounds like s, ayin is a throaty sound with no direct English equivalent, pe sounds like p or f, tsade sounds like ts, qof sounds like k, resh sounds like r, shin sounds like sh or s, and tav sounds like t.

Two features of Hebrew deserve attention from the start. First, several letters come in pairs that sound similar to English ears but are distinct in Hebrew: kaf and qof, tet and tav, samekh and shin, alef and ayin. Ancient readers heard these as clearly different sounds, and in careful reading the distinction matters, because words that look alike can differ by one of these letters. Second, Hebrew letters also served as numbers in ancient times, with alef as one, bet as two, and so on. You do not need to memorize the numerical values now, but knowing the system exists helps you understand references you will meet in later reading about the text and its history.

Pronunciation varies between traditions. The pronunciation taught in this course follows the widely used modern Israeli standard, which is also the standard in many classrooms, while noting where older traditions differ. For example, some traditions pronounce tav without a dot as s, and some pronounce vav as w. These differences do not change meaning, and you should not worry about matching one tradition exactly. What matters is consistency: pick one pronunciation pattern, use it throughout the course, and note in your journal where you have seen a different pronunciation mentioned.

Practice: (1) Write the alphabet in order, right to left, five times, saying each letter name aloud as you write it. (2) Make a set of letter cards, one letter per card, shuffle them, and name each one until you can name all twenty-two without hesitation. (3) Learn the five final forms by writing five words that end in each final letter, using the vocabulary in the glossary material. (4) In your journal, write one paragraph describing the two features that surprised you most about the alphabet, and note any letter whose shape you keep confusing with another, with a memory aid for telling them apart.
