This lesson teaches essential Hebrew vocabulary and the root system that organizes it. By the end you will know forty key words with transliteration and translation, understand how three-letter roots connect related words, and be able to use numbers and common expressions in reading. Vocabulary learned with roots and passages sticks; vocabulary learned as isolated lists fades, so every word here is tied to a passage or a pattern you will meet again.

Hebrew builds most of its vocabulary from three-consonant roots. A root is a set of three letters that carries a core idea, and different words grow from it by adding prefixes, suffixes, and vowel patterns. The root sh-l-m carries the idea of wholeness and completion: shalom means peace or wholeness, shalem means complete, and the verb shalam means to complete or repay. The root z-kh-r carries the idea of remembering: zakhar means to remember, zekher means remembrance or memorial, and zikaron means a memorial. The root b-r-k carries the idea of blessing: barakh means to bless, berakhah means blessing, and barukh means blessed. When you meet a new word, ask what root it comes from and what other words share that root. That question turns one new word into a small family of related words.

Learn the core words in groups by meaning. Words for God and the sacred: Elohim, a name for God; Adonai, meaning Lord, the traditional spoken substitute for the divine name; qadosh, meaning holy or set apart; mikdash, meaning sanctuary; berakhah, meaning blessing. Words for people and family: ish, meaning man; ishah, meaning woman; ben, meaning son; bat, meaning daughter; av, meaning father; em, meaning mother; akh, meaning brother; akhot, meaning sister; am, meaning people or nation. Words for the land and the world: eretz, meaning land or earth; shamayim, meaning heavens or sky; mayim, meaning water; esh, meaning fire; etz, meaning tree; har, meaning mountain; derech, meaning way or road. Words for time: yom, meaning day; laylah, meaning night; shanah, meaning year; shabbat, meaning the seventh day of rest; moed, meaning appointed time or festival.

Words for action and relationship carry the text's themes. Shalom means peace, wholeness, and well-being, and it is also a greeting. Chesed means steadfast loyalty and loving-kindness within a relationship. Emet means truth, firmness, and reliability. Mishpat means justice, judgment, and legal decision. Tzedek means righteousness and rightness. Berit means covenant, a binding agreement. Mitzvah means commandment or obligation. Davar means word, thing, or matter, and its breadth explains why the book of Devarim is named for words. Torah means teaching or instruction. Ahavah means love. Yirah means awe or reverent fear. These words appear again and again in the text, and knowing them lets you hear the text's own vocabulary instead of relying entirely on a translator's choices.

Numbers are worth learning early because they appear in genealogies, censuses, and measurements. Ehad means one, shnayim means two, shloshah means three, arbaah means four, khamishah means five, shishah means six, shivah means seven, shmonah means eight, tishah means nine, asarah means ten. Twenty is esrim, thirty is shloshim, forty is arbaim, one hundred is meah, and one thousand is elef. Note that seven, shivah, shares its root with the word for oath and for fullness, a connection readers of the text have long noted. Practice counting in Hebrew until ten feels automatic, then learn the tens.

Two mistakes to avoid. First, one-gloss thinking: treating each Hebrew word as equal to a single English word. Chesed is not just kindness, torah is not just law, shalom is not just peace. Each word carries a range, and the glossary notes that range. When a word matters to your analysis, investigate its range rather than quoting one translation. Second, learning words without passages: attach each new word to a verse or story where you have met it, and write that connection in your journal. A word learned with its passage is a word you will recognize when you meet it again.

Practice: (1) Learn the words in this lesson in four sittings of ten, writing each with transliteration, translation, and one passage where you have met it. (2) Choose three roots and list every word you know from each root, explaining what the shared root contributes. (3) Count from one to ten in Hebrew aloud, then write the numbers in Hebrew letters. (4) Rewrite one paragraph from Lesson 5 of this course, or from your own reading, using at least five Hebrew terms precisely, then read it back and check that each term earns its place.
