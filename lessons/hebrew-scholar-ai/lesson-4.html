This lesson teaches the structure of the Hebrew Bible, known in Jewish tradition as the Tanakh, a name formed from the initials of its three parts. By the end you will be able to name the three parts, list the books in each part, and describe how the collection is organized and read. Knowing the structure lets you navigate any translation, follow scholarly discussion, and understand why books appear in the order they do.

The Tanakh has three parts. The first is Torah, meaning teaching or instruction, the five books studied in detail in the Torah Scholar course: Bereshit, Shemot, Vayikra, Bemidbar, and Devarim, known in English as Genesis, Exodus, Leviticus, Numbers, and Deuteronomy. The Torah is the foundation of the collection, and the other two parts constantly refer back to it. The second part is Neviim, meaning Prophets, which divides into the Former Prophets, the historical books Joshua, Judges, Samuel, and Kings, and the Latter Prophets, the books of Isaiah, Jeremiah, Ezekiel, and the Twelve Minor Prophets, a single collection of twelve shorter prophetic books from Hosea through Malachi. The third part is Ketuvim, meaning Writings, a varied collection that includes Psalms, Proverbs, Job, the Song of Songs, Ruth, Lamentations, Ecclesiastes, Esther, Daniel, Ezra and Nehemiah, and Chronicles. Together the three parts contain twenty-four books in the traditional Hebrew counting, though Christian Old Testament arrangements count the same material as thirty-nine books by dividing Samuel, Kings, Chronicles, Ezra and Nehemiah, and the Twelve.

Each part has a distinct character. The Torah combines narrative and law: stories of origins, ancestors, exodus, and wilderness journey woven together with legal collections. The Neviim continues the historical narrative from the entry into the land through the monarchy and exile, and pairs it with prophetic books that address the people in times of crisis and hope, calling for justice, warning of consequences, and speaking of restoration. The Ketuvim is the most varied part: poetry and wisdom in Psalms, Proverbs, and Job, love poetry in the Song of Songs, short narratives in Ruth and Esther, reflection on suffering in Lamentations, and historical retelling in Chronicles. Reading across the three parts, you see the same themes handled in different forms: narrative, prophecy, law, poetry, and wisdom all contribute to the whole.

The text is organized into smaller units that shape study. The weekly portions, or parashot, divide the Torah into fifty-four sections read across the year, giving any student a natural one-year reading pace for the first part. Chapters and verses, the numbered divisions printed in most translations, were added much later by editors and printers for reference; they are convenient but were not part of the text's original design, so arguments that depend heavily on where a chapter break falls should be treated cautiously. The prophetic books and the Writings have their own traditional divisions, and the Psalms are organized into five smaller books within the book, a structure worth noticing when you study them. Knowing these layers lets you move between a Hebrew edition, an English translation, and a scholarly discussion without getting lost.

A few orientation points complete the picture. The Tanakh is written almost entirely in Hebrew, with a few passages in Aramaic, a related Semitic language, in Daniel and Ezra. Its traditional text, the exact Hebrew wording, was preserved with extraordinary scribal precision over centuries by the Masoretes, who also added the vowel points you learned in Lesson 2. Translations differ in philosophy: some aim for literal word-for-word rendering, others for readable paraphrase, so comparing two translations of the same passage is itself an illuminating study exercise. Finally, the order of books in the Hebrew tradition differs from the order in many English Bibles, which follow a different arrangement inherited through Greek and Latin translations. Neither order is the original, since the books circulated separately before being collected, but knowing both orders prevents confusion when you move between editions.

Two mistakes to avoid as you study the structure. First, treating the English book order as the only order: the Hebrew order reflects how the collection was organized in Jewish tradition, and using it connects you to the commentary and study tradition. Practice naming books in the Hebrew order from the start. Second, reading the Writings as an afterthought: the Ketuvim contains some of the most widely read material in the whole collection, including the Psalms and the wisdom books, and its variety shows the range of forms the tradition valued.

Practice: (1) From memory, write the three parts of the Tanakh and the books in each part, then check and correct your list. (2) In your journal, create a table with columns for part, book, Hebrew name, English name, and one-sentence description, and fill it without looking at this lesson, then correct it. (3) Choose one book from Neviim and one from Ketuvim and write a paragraph on each, describing its form and its main subject. (4) Look up how your available translation orders the books and note three differences from the Hebrew order, writing one paragraph on why the difference matters for a reader.
