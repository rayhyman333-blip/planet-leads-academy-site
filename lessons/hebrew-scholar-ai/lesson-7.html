This lesson teaches methods for close reading of the Hebrew Bible: attention to plain meaning, word study, repetition, and structure. By the end you will be able to read a passage at several levels, trace a Hebrew word through the text, and explain how a passage's structure shapes its meaning. Method matters as much as content: two readers can look at the same verse and see different things depending on how they were taught to read, and disciplined method turns casual reading into study.

Begin with peshat, the plain or contextual meaning: what the words say in their grammar, vocabulary, and immediate context. This is the foundation, and every other level of reading builds on it. Practicing peshat means slowing down: identify the subject and verb of each sentence, notice repeated words, and ask what the passage meant to its first audience before asking what it means to a later reader. Consider the opening of Bereshit: the Hebrew syntax of the first verses has been analyzed for centuries, with readers debating how the clauses connect. That debate is peshat-level work, careful grammar in service of meaning. A useful discipline: paraphrase each verse in your own words before consulting any commentary. If you cannot paraphrase it, you have not yet understood its plain sense, and interpretation built on confusion will stay confused.

Word study is the second method, and your root knowledge from Lesson 3 makes it possible. Choose a word that repeats in a passage, find its root, and list other words from the same root. Then ask: what does the root's core idea contribute here? The word shalom and its root sh-l-m, for example, carry wholeness and completion, so a passage using shalom is saying more than a translation's single word for peace conveys. Check a word's range by looking at how it is used in different passages: chesed appears in contexts of loyalty, kindness, and obligation, and its meaning in any one verse is shaped by the whole range. Use a concordance or a word-study reference if you have access to one, and record your findings in your journal. Word study done carefully is one of the most rewarding forms of close reading, because it lets you hear the Hebrew behind the translation.

Repetition and structure are the third method. Ancient Hebrew narrative and poetry use repetition deliberately: repeated words mark themes, repeated scenes invite comparison, and repeated phrases frame a passage. When a word appears three times in a short passage, ask why. When two stories share a pattern but differ in one detail, ask what the difference signifies. Structure also carries meaning: a passage may be arranged as a chiasm, a symmetrical pattern where the first and last elements mirror each other and the center carries the emphasis, or as a sequence that builds toward a climax. Sketch the structure of a passage before interpreting it: list its scenes or stanzas in order, note repetitions and contrasts, and identify the turning point. That sketch often reveals the passage's argument more clearly than any commentary.

Comparison is the fourth method. Compare translations: place two or three renderings of the same passage side by side and note where they differ. Differences reveal ambiguities in the Hebrew or different translator philosophies, and each difference is a question worth investigating. Compare passages within the text: the text revisits events and laws more than once, and asking why the accounts differ is one of the oldest and most productive questions in the field. Compare with context: Lesson 6's historical background lets you ask what a passage's first audience would have heard. And compare commentaries: when two scholars read one verse differently, reconstruct each one's reasoning. The disagreement teaches more than either conclusion alone.

Two mistakes to avoid. First, jumping to interpretation before establishing the plain meaning: creative readings of a verse you have not parsed grammatically are built on sand. Peshat first, always, even advanced scholars circle back to it. Second, treating one translation as the text: every translation is already an interpretation, making hundreds of small decisions you cannot see. The student who reads only one translation mistakes the translator's choices for the text itself. Compare at least two, and remember that the Hebrew, which you can now approach through letters, roots, and vocabulary, is the anchor.

Practice: (1) Choose a short passage of ten to fifteen verses, for example the opening of the Joseph story or a psalm, and paraphrase each verse in your own words in your journal, peshat work with no commentaries yet. (2) Choose one repeated word in the passage, trace its root, list three other words from that root, and write a paragraph on what the root contributes to the passage. (3) Sketch the passage's structure in a simple outline, marking repetitions, contrasts, and the turning point. (4) Compare two translations of the same passage, list every difference you find, and write one question about each significant difference.
