This lesson teaches you to pronounce Spanish clearly from the start. Spanish pronunciation is one of the most consistent in the world: letters almost always sound the same way, so once you learn the rules you can read almost any word correctly. That consistency is your biggest advantage as a beginner. Your goal in this lesson is not a perfect accent — it is clarity. If your vowels are right and your stress lands on the right syllable, Spanish speakers will understand you even while you are still learning words.

Spanish has exactly five vowel sounds, and they never change. Say them now: A (ah, as in father), E (eh, as in bed), I (ee, as in see), O (oh, as in go), U (oo, as in moon). That is it — no short-vs-long games, no silent-vowel surprises. Practice these three words slowly: casa (CAH-sah, house), mesa (MEH-sah, table), luna (LOO-nah, moon). Each vowel gets the same amount of time; do not stretch one vowel longer than the others the way English habits push you to. A very common beginner mistake is reading Spanish vowels with English values — for example, saying 'si' (yes) like the English word 'sigh' instead of 'see'. When in doubt, keep every vowel short and pure.

Next, the consonants that trip up English speakers. The rolled R (rr) is the famous one: pero (but) has a single soft R, perro (dog) has the rolled rr — mixing them up changes the word, so practice matters. To learn the roll, put your tongue behind your upper teeth and blow air firmly while relaxing the tongue tip; it takes days or weeks, and that is normal. The letter J sounds like a strong English H (as in 'hot'): jueves (Thursday) is HWAY-bes. LL and Y usually sound like the English Y: lluvia (rain) is YOO-vyah, and yo (I) is YOH. The letter Ñ is the 'ny' sound in 'canyon': mañana (tomorrow) is mah-NYAH-nah. B and V sound almost identical in Spanish — a soft sound between the two, like 'vino' (wine) with the lips barely touching. Finally, C before E or I sounds like S in Latin America (gracias = GRAH-syahs), and Z is also an S sound there — one less thing to worry about in this course, which uses Latin American pronunciation throughout.

Word stress follows simple rules, and they are worth memorizing today. Rule 1: words ending in a vowel, N, or S stress the second-to-last syllable — casa (CAH-sah), hablan (AH-blahn), mesas (MEH-sahs). Rule 2: words ending in any other consonant stress the last syllable — hablar (ah-BLAHR), ciudad (see-oo-DAHD). Rule 3: a written accent mark (tilde) overrides everything — the stressed syllable is the marked one: médico (MEH-dee-koh, doctor), café (kah-FEH, coffee). Notice how médico and medico would be different words if the accent moved. A classic beginner mistake is stressing English-style, like saying 'com-PU-ta-dora' for computadora (computer) when it is com-poo-tah-DOH-rah. Trust the rules, not your English ear.

Here is a short dialogue using only sounds from this lesson. Read it aloud slowly, stressing the right syllables:

María: ¡Hola! ¿Cómo te llamas? (OH-lah koh-moh teh YAH-mahs) — Hello! What is your name?
Carlos: Me llamo Carlos. ¿Y tú? (meh YAH-moh KAHR-lohs) — My name is Carlos. And you?
María: Me llamo María. Mucho gusto. (MOO-choh GOOS-toh) — Nice to meet you.
Carlos: Mucho gusto. ¿Hablas español? (AH-blahs ess-pahn-YOHL) — Do you speak Spanish?
María: Sí, un poco. (see oon POH-koh) — Yes, a little.

Notice every vowel is short and pure, and the stress falls exactly where the rules predict. Say the whole dialogue five times today; it is your first Spanish conversation.

Practice: Write out these ten words and mark the stressed syllable in each, then say them aloud three times: ventana, teléfono, español, gracias, mañana, biblioteca, hotel, familia, zapato, universidad. Next, write your own name and three English words you use often, then 'Spanish-ize' their pronunciation using today's vowel rules and read them aloud. Finally, record yourself (any phone recorder works) reading the dialogue above, listen back, and circle any vowel that sounded English — re-say those lines until the vowels sound short and pure.
