This lesson gives you your first real social toolkit: greeting people politely, introducing yourself, and surviving conversations when you do not understand something. These phrases are the highest-value sentences in the language because they unlock every interaction — ordering food, asking for help, meeting people. Learn them cold before you worry about grammar, because even a flawless sentence is useless if you cannot open and close a conversation gracefully.

Start with greetings. Hola (OH-lah) means hello and works everywhere. Buenos días (BWAY-nohs DEE-ahs) is good morning, buenas tardes (BWAY-nahs TAHR-dehs) is good afternoon, and buenas noches (BWAY-nahs NOH-chehs) is good evening or good night. Notice that 'good' is plural here — that is just how Spanish says it, so do not 'fix' it to buen día, which sounds odd to native ears. For introductions: Me llamo... (meh YAH-moh) means 'my name is' — literally 'I call myself'. ¿Cómo te llamas? (KOH-moh teh YAH-mahs) asks someone's name informally; ¿Cómo se llama usted? is the formal version for strangers, elders, or officials. Encantado (if you are male) or encantada (if you are female) means delighted, and mucho gusto (MOO-choh GOOS-toh) — nice to meet you — works for everyone.

Now the small-talk core. ¿Cómo estás? (KOH-moh ehs-TAHS) is 'how are you' for friends; ¿Cómo está usted? is the formal version. Common answers: Muy bien, gracias (very well, thanks), Más o menos (so-so), and the wonderfully honest Estoy cansado/cansada (I am tired — masculine/feminine). ¿Y tú? (ee TOO) means 'and you?' — short and essential. De nada (deh NAH-dah) is 'you're welcome', por favor (pohr fah-VOHR) is 'please', and gracias (GRAH-syahs) is 'thank you'. A frequent beginner mistake is answering ¿Cómo estás? with a full life story; a short answer plus ¿Y tú? back keeps the conversation balanced and gives you twice the practice.

Survival phrases are your safety net — memorize these before anything else. No entiendo (noh ehn-TYEHN-doh) — I don't understand. ¿Puedes repetir, por favor? (PWEH-dehs reh-peh-TEER) — Can you repeat, please? Habla más despacio, por favor (AH-blah mahs dehs-PAH-syoh) — Speak more slowly, please. ¿Habla inglés? (AH-blah een-GLEHS) — Do you speak English? Perdón (pehr-DOHN) — excuse me / sorry. Disculpe (dees-KOOL-peh) — excuse me (to get attention, more polite). Lo siento (loh SYEHN-toh) — I'm sorry. ¿Qué significa...? (keh seeg-nee-FEE-kah) — What does ... mean? These eight phrases will rescue you in any Spanish-speaking country, and native speakers respect learners who ask for clarification instead of nodding blankly.

Watch for this common mistake: mixing up tú and usted forms at the wrong moment. Using tú with a stranger is not a grammar failure — it is a social one, and it can come across as rude with older people or in formal settings like banks and clinics. When in doubt, start with usted and switch to tú only when the other person does. A second mistake: saying 'estoy embarazado' thinking it means 'I'm embarrassed'. Embarazada means pregnant — a legendary false friend. Say tengo vergüenza (TEHN-goh behr-GWEHN-sah) for 'I'm embarrassed'.

Full dialogue — a first meeting. Read aloud with the translations:

Ana: ¡Buenos días! ¿Cómo se llama usted? — Good morning! What is your name?
Luis: Buenos días. Me llamo Luis. ¿Y usted? — Good morning. My name is Luis. And you?
Ana: Me llamo Ana. Mucho gusto. — My name is Ana. Nice to meet you.
Luis: Mucho gusto, Ana. ¿De dónde es usted? — Nice to meet you, Ana. Where are you from?
Ana: Soy de Texas. ¿Y usted? — I'm from Texas. And you?
Luis: Soy de México. ¿Habla español? — I'm from Mexico. Do you speak Spanish?
Ana: Un poco. Habla más despacio, por favor. — A little. Speak more slowly, please.
Luis: Claro. ¿Cómo está hoy? — Of course. How are you today?
Ana: Muy bien, gracias. ¿Y usted? — Very well, thanks. And you?
Luis: Muy bien. ¡Hasta luego! — Very well. See you later!

Practice: Write a self-introduction of five lines (greeting, name, where you are from, one feeling word, goodbye) and memorize it — this is your personal script for the next month. Then practice the survival phrases: cover the Spanish column, read the English, and say the Spanish from memory until you can do all eight without peeking. Finally, role-play the dialogue above twice, switching roles, saying every line aloud with the correct vowel sounds from Lesson 1.
