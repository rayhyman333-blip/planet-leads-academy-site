This lesson tackles the two most important verbs in Spanish — ser and estar, both meaning 'to be' — plus the present tense, which lets you describe what you do every day. English uses one 'to be' for everything; Spanish splits the job in two, and mixing them up is the single most common beginner error. Master this distinction now and every sentence you build later gets easier.

SER is for things that are permanent, essential, or defining: identity, origin, characteristics, time, and profession. Yo soy de Texas (I am from Texas). Ella es alta (She is tall). Son las tres (It is three o'clock). Él es médico (He is a doctor). ESTAR is for states, conditions, locations, and things in progress: Estoy cansado (I am tired). La tienda está cerrada (The store is closed). Estamos en casa (We are at home). A simple memory rule: ser = what something IS, estar = how something is or WHERE it is. Location always uses estar, even for permanent places: Madrid está en España (Madrid is in Spain).

Conjugations (memorize these shapes — they never change):
SER: yo soy, tú eres, él/ella/usted es, nosotros somos, ellos/ustedes son.
ESTAR: yo estoy, tú estás, él/ella/usted está, nosotros estamos, ellos/ustedes están.
Read them aloud now. Notice the accents on estás, está, están — the stress rule from Lesson 1 in action.

Now the present tense of regular verbs. Spanish verbs end in -ar, -er, or -ir, and each family has its own endings. For -AR verbs like hablar (to speak): hablo, hablas, habla, hablamos, hablan. For -ER verbs like comer (to eat): como, comes, come, comemos, comen. For -IR verbs like vivir (to live): vivo, vives, vive, vivimos, viven. The pattern: drop the -ar/-er/-ir and add the endings. Practice with trabajar (to work): trabajo en una oficina (I work in an office), ¿trabajas los sábados? (Do you work Saturdays?). Notice that Spanish usually drops the subject pronoun — 'hablo' already means 'I speak', so 'yo hablo' is only used for emphasis.

A few very common verbs are irregular — learn them as vocabulary: TENER (to have): tengo, tienes, tiene, tenemos, tienen. Tengo dos hermanos (I have two brothers). IR (to go): voy, vas, va, vamos, van. Voy al mercado (I go to the market). HACER (to do/make): hago, haces, hace, hacemos, hacen. Hago ejercicio (I exercise). QUERER (to want): quiero, quieres, quiere, queremos, quieren. Quiero café (I want coffee). These four irregulars appear in nearly every conversation, so they repay memorization immediately.

Two beginner mistakes to avoid. First, the ser/estar swap: saying 'estoy aburrido' when you mean 'soy aburrido' — the first means 'I am bored', the second means 'I am a boring person'. Adjectives change meaning with the verb: ser listo = to be clever, estar listo = to be ready. Second, translating 'do' word-for-word: English 'Do you speak Spanish?' is just ¿Hablas español? — Spanish does not use a helper verb for questions, so never say '¿Haces hablas español?'.

Dialogue — two coworkers at lunch:

Pedro: ¿Dónde trabajas? — Where do you work?
Sofía: Trabajo en un banco. Soy cajera. ¿Y tú? — I work at a bank. I am a cashier. And you?
Pedro: Soy profesor. Trabajo en una escuela. — I am a teacher. I work at a school.
Sofía: ¿Estás cansado hoy? — Are you tired today?
Sofía: No, estoy bien. ¿Quieres comer conmigo? — No, I'm fine. Do you want to eat with me?
Pedro: Sí, quiero. ¿Vamos al restaurante? — Yes, I want to. Shall we go to the restaurant?
Sofía: Vamos. Tengo hambre. — Let's go. I'm hungry.

(Note: tengo hambre — 'I have hunger' — is how Spanish says 'I'm hungry'. Another estar/ser trap avoided by using tener.)

Practice: Conjugate these verbs fully in writing: hablar, comer, vivir, tener, ir. Then write six sentences about yourself: two with ser (identity/origin/profession), two with estar (feeling/location), two with present-tense action verbs. Say each sentence aloud. Finally, translate to Spanish: 'She is a doctor. She works in a hospital. She is tired today. She wants coffee.' Check: Es médica. Trabaja en un hospital. Está cansada hoy. Quiere café.
