This lesson introduces the Torah as a text: what it is, how it is organized, and what each of its five books contains. By the end you will be able to name the five books in Hebrew (with transliteration) and English, summarize the contents of each, and describe how the text is divided for reading. This structural map is the foundation everything else in the course builds on.

The Torah consists of five books, traditionally regarded in Judaism as a single continuous work. In Hebrew it is called the Chamishah Chumshei Torah, meaning "the five fifths of the Torah." The word Torah itself comes from a Hebrew root associated with teaching or instruction. The five books, in order, are: Bereshit (bay-ray-SHEET), meaning "In the beginning," known in English as Genesis; Shemot (sheh-MOAT), meaning "Names," known as Exodus; Vayikra (vai-YEEK-ra), meaning "And He called," known as Leviticus; Bemidbar (beh-meed-BAR), meaning "In the wilderness," known as Numbers; and Devarim (deh-vah-REEM), meaning "Words," known as Deuteronomy. Notice that the Hebrew names come from the first significant word of each book, while the English names — derived through Greek — describe the book's contents. Both naming traditions are worth knowing, since different translations and discussions use one or the other.

Each book has a distinct character. Bereshit (Genesis) covers primeval history — creation, the first humans, the flood — and then the ancestral narratives: Abraham and Sarah, Isaac and Rebekah, Jacob and his family, and Joseph in Egypt. It is largely narrative, with genealogies structuring its sections. Shemot (Exodus) opens with the Israelites enslaved in Egypt, follows Moses' emergence as leader, the departure from Egypt, the revelation at Mount Sinai, and the giving of laws, and closes with the construction of the Tabernacle (the portable sanctuary). Vayikra (Leviticus) is the most law-concentrated book: priestly duties, sacrifices, purity regulations, the holiness code, and the festival calendar. Readers often find it the most demanding, and this course will give you tools for approaching legal texts patiently. Bemidbar (Numbers) returns to narrative: the wilderness journey, censuses (the source of the English name), rebellions, and the long approach to the promised land. Devarim (Deuteronomy) is structured as Moses' farewell discourses — restating laws, recounting the journey, and setting out blessings and consequences — closing the five-book arc.

The text is also organized into smaller reading units that shape how it has been studied. The traditional division into parashot (pah-rah-SHOHT), weekly portions, divides the Torah into fifty-four sections read across the year in synagogue — a system that has structured Jewish study life for centuries and gives any student a natural one-year reading pace. Chapters and verses, the numbered divisions most translations print, were added much later by editors and printers for reference; they are convenient but were not part of the text's original design, so arguments that depend heavily on where a chapter break falls should be treated cautiously. Knowing these layers — five books, weekly portions, chapters and verses — lets you navigate any translation and follow scholarly discussions without getting lost.

A few orientation points complete the picture. The Torah is written almost entirely in Hebrew, with a few passages in Aramaic, a related Semitic language. Its traditional text — the exact Hebrew wording — was preserved with extraordinary scribal precision over centuries, a subject scholars of textual transmission study in its own right. In Jewish tradition the Torah is the first of three sections of the Hebrew Bible, followed by the Prophets (Nevi'im) and the Writings (Ketuvim); this course focuses on the Torah proper while noting connections outward. Finally, translations differ in philosophy — some aim for literal word-for-word rendering, others for readable paraphrase — so comparing two translations of the same passage, as Lesson 2 will have you do, is itself an illuminating study exercise.

Two mistakes to avoid as you begin. First, treating the English names as the "real" ones and the Hebrew as decoration: the Hebrew names reflect how the text introduces itself, and using them connects you to the commentary tradition you will meet in Lesson 6, which discusses Bereshit, not "Genesis." Practice both from the start. Second, rushing past Vayikra (Leviticus) because it reads as dry law: the legal material is central to understanding what the text meant to its earliest audiences, and Lessons 3 and 4 will show you how to read it with context rather than skimming. Every book earns its place in the five.

Practice: (1) From memory, write the five books in order three times: once with Hebrew names and transliterations, once with English names, once with one-sentence content summaries. (2) In your study journal, create a five-row table — book, Hebrew name + transliteration, English name, primary character (narrative/law/discourse), two-sentence summary — and fill it without looking at this lesson, then correct it. (3) Look up how your available translation (online or printed) divides one book into chapters, and note where two chapters break in the middle of a continuous story — write one paragraph on why the chapter division can mislead a reader about the narrative's shape. Keep the table; you will add to it all course.
