This lesson surveys how scholars across centuries have studied the Torah: the translators, the classical commentators, and the modern critical scholars. By the end you will be able to name the major phases of interpretation, describe each phase's characteristic questions and methods, and read a classical commentary with understanding. The Torah is among the most commented-upon texts in human history; studying the commentators teaches you not only what they concluded but how disciplined minds approach a difficult text.

Interpretation began with translation. The Septuagint, the ancient Greek translation produced in the Hellenistic period, is the oldest surviving translation and a landmark in its own right: its word choices reveal how early readers understood obscure Hebrew, and it became the scriptural text for Greek-speaking Judaism and early Christianity. The Targums, Aramaic translations and paraphrases, show how synagogue readers heard the text rendered in their vernacular — often expanding with interpretive additions that reveal contemporary understanding. Studying ancient translations as witnesses to early reading is a scholarly discipline: where the Greek or Aramaic diverges from the Hebrew, you are seeing an ancient reader's decision, and asking why they decided so is genuine research.

The rabbinic and medieval periods produced the classical commentary tradition that still structures traditional study. The Midrash collections interpret verse by verse with creative, associative readings — derash in its fullest form — often addressing textual difficulties (repetitions, gaps, unusual words) with imaginative solutions. The medieval commentators brought systematic methods: Rashi (Rabbi Shlomo Yitzchaki, 11th-century France) wrote the commentary that became the standard companion to the text, prized for clarifying peshat with concision and for selecting the most illuminating midrashic readings; his successors debated him line by line. Maimonides (Rabbi Moshe ben Maimon, 12th century) brought philosophical analysis, reading the text's anthropomorphic language — descriptions of the divine in human terms — as requiring rational interpretation. Nachmanides (13th-century Spain) combined peshat sensitivity, philosophical depth, and attention to the text's spiritual dimensions. These scholars disagreed vigorously and cited each other by name — the tradition is a centuries-long argument conducted in the margins, and learning to follow the argument is learning to think.

The modern period added critical scholarship: the historical and literary study of the text's composition, language, and ancient context. Scholars analyze the Hebrew's linguistic strata, compare the Torah's laws and narratives with ancient Near Eastern parallels (Lesson 3's material), and debate how the five books reached their final form. This scholarship asks different questions than the classical commentators — about history and composition rather than meaning and application — and its conclusions remain debated. The academically honest posture, which this course maintains, is to present the major positions and their evidence without declaring settled what scholars still dispute. Classical and critical approaches need not be enemies: many contemporary students read Rashi for peshat insight and modern scholarship for historical context in the same study session.

Learn to read a commentary the way you learned to read the text: actively and critically. First identify the commentator's question — what difficulty in the verse prompted this comment? Then reconstruct the reasoning — what evidence, linguistic or contextual, supports the reading? Then consider alternatives — how did other commentators handle the same verse, and whose reasoning persuces you? Finally, separate the levels (Lesson 2): is this peshat, derash, or something else, and does the commentator signal which? A commentary is not an answer key; it is a senior study partner whose arguments you evaluate. The tradition's greatest gift is modeling intellectual seriousness: no verse too small for attention, no difficulty evaded, no disagreement suppressed.

Two mistakes to avoid. First, treating commentators as authorities to memorize rather than thinkers to engage: "Rashi says" ends inquiry where it should begin — ask why he says it and whether the reasoning holds. Second, anachronism in either direction: dismissing medieval commentators for lacking modern knowledge misses what they offer (unparalleled Hebrew sensitivity and centuries of accumulated reading), while dismissing critical scholars for "undermining tradition" misses what they offer (historical and linguistic evidence). Each phase answers its own questions well; judge each by its own standards.

Practice: (1) Choose one verse with a well-known interpretive difficulty (the opening verses of Bereshit, or a verse with an unusual word). Find how two different commentators or translations handle it — many study editions print commentary alongside the text — and write a page reconstructing each one's question, reasoning, and conclusion. (2) Label each reading with its PaRDeS level from Lesson 2. (3) Write a paragraph stating which reading you find most persuasive and why, based on the reasoning rather than the reputation. (4) In your journal, begin a "commentators" reference page: name, period, characteristic method, one representative insight — you will add to it as your studies continue.
