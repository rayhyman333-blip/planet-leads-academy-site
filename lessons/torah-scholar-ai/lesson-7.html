This lesson teaches essential Hebrew terms that unlock deeper reading of the Torah. By the end you will know thirty key terms with transliteration, translation, and usage notes, understand how Hebrew's root system connects words, and be able to use the terms precisely in your study writing. You do not need to learn the Hebrew alphabet for this course — transliteration carries you — but these terms give you direct access to concepts that English translations can only approximate.

Hebrew builds most of its vocabulary from three-consonant roots, and recognizing shared roots reveals connections invisible in translation. The root sh-l-m, for example, underlies shalom (shah-LOHM, peace, wholeness, well-being) and related words of completion and wholeness; when the text uses these words, Hebrew readers hear the resonance. The root b-r-k underlies berakhah (beh-rah-KHAH, blessing) and barukh (bah-ROOKH, blessed). The root z-kh-r underlies zakhar (zah-KHAHR, to remember) and zekher (ZAY-kher, remembrance, memorial) — and "remembering" in the Torah is active, covenantal memory, not passive recall. Train yourself to notice when your translation's different English words might render one Hebrew root; that suspicion, checked against a lexicon or word study, is the beginning of philological reading.

The glossary below organizes terms by domain. Study ten at a time across three sittings rather than cramming all thirty — spaced repetition builds durable vocabulary. For each term, learn three things: how to say it (the transliteration's capitalized syllable carries the stress), what it means (including the range, not just one gloss), and where you have met it (link each term to a passage from earlier lessons). Terms learned attached to passages stick; terms learned as lists evaporate.

CORE TEXT TERMS: Torah (toh-RAH) — teaching, instruction; the five books. Mitzvah (meetz-VAH; plural mitzvot) — commandment, obligation. Berit (beh-REET) — covenant, binding agreement. Davar (dah-VAHR; plural devarim) — word, thing, matter — its breadth explains the book title Devarim. Emet (eh-MET) — truth, firmness, reliability. Chesed (KHEH-sed) — steadfast loyalty, loving-kindness within relationship. Mishpat (meesh-PAHT) — justice, judgment, legal decision. Tzedek (TSEH-dek) — righteousness, rightness. Shalom (shah-LOHM) — peace, wholeness, well-being. Kadosh (kah-DOHSH) — holy, set apart, distinct.

RITUAL AND TIME TERMS: Shabbat (shah-BAHT) — the seventh day of rest. Mikdash (meeg-DAHSH) — sanctuary, sacred space. Korban (kohr-BAHN) — offering, that which is brought near (note: not "sacrifice" in the modern sense of loss, but of drawing near). Tahor (tah-HOHR) / tamei (tah-MAY) — ritually pure / impure, states concerning sanctuary access, not moral judgments. Moed (moh-AYD) — appointed time, festival. Teshuvah (teh-shoo-VAH) — return, the concept of turning back.

STUDY TERMS: Peshat (peh-SHAHT) — plain, contextual meaning. Derash (deh-RAHSH) — interpretive, inferential reading. Remez (REH-mez) — hinted, allegorical reading. Sod (SOHD) — hidden, mystical dimension. Midrash (meed-RAHSH) — interpretive exposition; also the collections of such readings. Halakhah (hah-lah-KHAH) — the way of practice, religious law as lived. Aggadah (ah-gah-DAH) — narrative, non-legal tradition: stories, ethics, theology. Parashah (pah-rah-SHAH; plural parashot) — weekly Torah portion.

Use the terms in your writing from now on — precisely, not decoratively. Write "the passage's peshat" when you mean its plain sense; write "a derash reading" when you mean an inferential one. Precision with these terms signals genuine understanding and connects your journal to the scholarly conversation. But avoid the beginner's trap of sprinkling Hebrew to sound learned: one exact term beats five approximate ones, and English remains your primary language of analysis. The terms are tools for clarity, and clarity is the standard they are judged by.

Two mistakes to avoid. First, one-gloss thinking: treating each term as equal to a single English word. Chesed is not just "kindness," tamei is not "dirty," torah is not just "law" — each carries a range that the glossary notes and that passages illustrate. When a term matters to your analysis, investigate its range rather than quoting one translation. Second, mispronunciation anxiety: transliteration is an approximation, and good-faith effort is all anyone expects. Say the terms aloud as you learn them — the physical act of pronunciation aids memory — without worrying about perfection.

Practice: (1) Learn the thirty terms in three sittings of ten; for each, write the transliteration, the meaning range, and one passage where you have encountered it. (2) Choose five terms central to your interests and write a paragraph on each explaining what the Hebrew adds beyond the English translation — cite the specific passages. (3) Rewrite one paragraph of your Lesson 4 theme analysis using at least four Hebrew terms precisely, then read it back: does each term earn its place? (4) Add a "key terms" section to your journal's reference pages, organized by domain, leaving space to add terms as your reading continues. The full glossary is also included in your materials packet for quick reference.
