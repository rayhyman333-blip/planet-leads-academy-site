This lesson teaches the principal methods readers have used to study the Torah, from careful attention to plain meaning through layered interpretive approaches. By the end you will be able to read a passage at four levels, compare translations critically, and ask the kinds of questions that drive productive study. Method matters as much as content: two readers can look at the same verse and see different things depending on how they were taught to read.

Begin with peshat (peh-SHAHT), the plain or contextual meaning: what the words say in their grammar, vocabulary, and immediate context. This is the foundation — every other level of reading builds on it, and skilled readers return to it constantly. Practicing peshat means slowing down: identify the subject and verb of each sentence, notice repeated words, and ask what the passage meant to its first audience before asking what it means to you. Consider the opening of Bereshit: the Hebrew syntax of the first verses has been analyzed for centuries, with readers debating how the clauses connect — that debate is peshat-level work, careful grammar in service of meaning. A useful discipline: paraphrase each verse in your own words before consulting any commentary. If you cannot paraphrase it, you have not yet understood its plain sense, and interpretation built on confusion will stay confused.

The classical tradition describes additional levels, famously summarized as PaRDeS (pahr-DESS), an acronym for four approaches: peshat (plain meaning), remez (REH-mez, hinted or allegorical meaning — what the text suggests beyond the literal), derash (deh-RAHSH, interpretive or homiletical meaning — what careful inference draws out), and sod (SOHD, the mystical or hidden dimension explored in later traditions). You do not need to master all four now; the value of PaRDeS for a beginning student is the reminder that texts support multiple legitimate readings and that responsible readers distinguish which level they are operating on. When a commentary offers a creative interpretation, ask: is this peshat, or is it derash? Both can be valuable, but confusing them produces muddled thinking. Lesson 6 will show you commentators working explicitly at these different levels.

Comparison is the engine of study. Compare translations: place two or three renderings of the same passage side by side and note where they differ — differences reveal ambiguities in the Hebrew or different translator philosophies, and each difference is a question worth investigating. Compare passages within the text: the Torah revisits events and laws more than once (the creation accounts, the Sabbath commandment in two versions), and asking why the accounts differ is one of the oldest and most productive questions in the field. Compare with context: Lesson 3's historical background lets you ask what a passage's first audience would have heard. And compare commentaries: when two scholars read one verse differently, reconstruct each one's reasoning — the disagreement teaches more than either conclusion alone.

Develop the habit of asking structured questions of every passage. Who is speaking, and to whom? What just happened before this passage, and what happens right after? Which words repeat, and what might the repetition emphasize? What is surprising — what would you not have expected the text to say? What is missing — whose perspective is absent? Write your questions in your journal before seeking answers; the questions are the study, and premature answers close down curiosity. A page of good questions about ten verses represents more learning than a page of borrowed answers.

Two mistakes to avoid. First, jumping to interpretation before establishing the plain meaning: creative readings of a verse you have not parsed grammatically are built on sand. Peshat first, always — even advanced scholars circle back to it. Second, treating one translation as the text: every translation is already an interpretation, making hundreds of small decisions you cannot see. The student who reads only one translation mistakes the translator's choices for the text itself. Compare at least two, and remember that the Hebrew (which you can approach gradually through Lesson 7's key terms) is the anchor.

Practice: (1) Choose a short narrative passage (ten to fifteen verses — the opening of the Joseph story or the crossing of the sea are good candidates). Paraphrase each verse in your own words in your journal — peshat work, no commentaries yet. (2) Compare two translations of the same passage; list every difference you find and write one question about each significant one. (3) Write ten structured questions about the passage using the question list above. (4) Identify one place where a remez or derash reading might go beyond the plain sense, and write one paragraph distinguishing what the text says (peshat) from what the extended reading suggests — labeling each clearly. Save this exercise; it is your template for every study session.
