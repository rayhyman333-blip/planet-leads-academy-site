Full Lessons — Conversational Dutch

Full lesson text, Academy-built. This is the complete 8-lesson course text for this course. Prices and course links are on the course page.

Speak practical everyday Dutch from day one: clear pronunciation, core grammar, and real conversations.

Lesson 1: Pronunciation — Dutch Vowels, Diphthongs, and the Guttural G

This lesson teaches you to pronounce Dutch clearly from the start. Dutch spelling looks unfamiliar at first, but the sounds follow patterns you can learn in one sitting. Your goal in this lesson is not a perfect accent — it is clarity. If your vowels are clean and you can make the guttural g without choking on it, Dutch speakers will understand you even while you are still learning words. Dutch is a Germanic language, so if you know English or German, many words will look familiar: huis (house), water (water), kind (child), nacht (night). The spelling is far more regular than English, which is good news: once you learn the rules in this lesson, you can read almost any Dutch word aloud.

Start with the short vowels, which are quick and crisp. A sounds like the a in cat: kat (cat), man (man). E sounds like the e in bed: bed (bed), met (with). I sounds like the i in bit: dit (this), ik (I). O sounds like the o in hot: op (on), top (top). U sounds like the u in put, with rounded lips: hut (hut), bus (bus). Now the long vowels, written double or in open syllables: AA sounds like the a in father: maan (moon), water (water). EE sounds like the ay in day: meer (lake, more), thee (tea). OO sounds like the o in go: boot (boat), rood (red). UU is the famous hard one: round your lips as if to say oo but say ee instead — vuur (fire), muur (wall). IE sounds like ee in see: vier (four), lief (sweet). The difference between short and long vowels matters: man (man) versus maan (moon), bed (bed) versus beet (beet). Practice each pair until your ear hears the length difference.

Now the diphthongs — sounds that glide from one vowel to another. IJ and EI sound the same, roughly like the ay in say: mijn (my), tijd (time), klein (small), ei (egg). UI is the trickiest Dutch sound: start with a short e and round your lips toward oo — huis (house), buiten (outside), fruit (fruit). Many learners say it like ou, which turns huis (house) into something closer to hous — drill it slowly. OU and AU sound like ow in cow: oud (old), koud (cold), vrouw (woman). EU sounds like the u in the French word tu with a slight glide: leuk (nice, fun), deur (door), neus (nose). A useful trick: learn one anchor word for each diphthong and say it every day — mijn, huis, oud, leuk — and the sounds will settle into your mouth.

The consonants hold the most famous Dutch sound: the guttural G. The Dutch G and CH are made at the back of the throat, like a soft clearing of the throat: goed (good), groot (big), nacht (night), licht (light). Do not panic — make a gentle hissing sound at the back of your throat, softer than a cough. In the south of the Netherlands and in Belgium the g is softer, almost like an English h, and either version will be understood. The combination SCH is an s followed by that same throaty sound: school (school), schip (ship), misschien (maybe). The R is often rolled or made at the back of the throat: rood (red), graag (gladly). The V is softer than in English, close to an f: van (from), veel (much). The W is like an English v: water (water), wat (what). The J sounds like the y in yes: ja (yes), jij (you). The NG at the end of a word is one sound, as in English sing: lang (long), jong (young).

Two spelling rules will save you constant confusion. First, final devoicing: at the end of a word, voiced consonants become voiceless — D sounds like T, B like P, V like F, Z like S. So hond (dog) is

pronounced hont, heb (have) sounds like hep, and huis (house) keeps its s. This is why spelling and sound sometimes disagree at word endings. Second, Dutch word stress usually falls on the first syllable: tafel (table), water (water), morgen (morning). Longer words borrowed from other languages often stress a later syllable — restaurant, telefoon (telephone) — but native Dutch words are first-syllable stressed, which gives the language its steady, even rhythm.

Here is a short dialogue using only sounds from this lesson. Read it aloud slowly:

Anna: Goedemorgen! Hoe gaat het met je? (khoo-duh-MOR-khen, hoo khaat hut met yuh) — Good morning! How are you? Pieter: Goed, dank je. En met jou? (khoot, dahnk yuh, en met yow) — Good, thanks. And with you? Anna: Goed. Ik heet Anna. Hoe heet jij? (ik hayt AH-nah, hoo hayt yay) — Good. My name is Anna. What is your name? Pieter: Ik heet Pieter. Leuk je te ontmoeten. (ik hayt PEE-tur, luuk yuh tuh ont-MOO-tun) — My name is Pieter. Nice to meet you. Anna: Leuk je te ontmoeten. Tot ziens! (luuk yuh tuh ont-MOO-tun, tot zeens) — Nice to meet you. Goodbye!

Say the whole dialogue five times today; it is your first Dutch conversation.

Practice: Write out these ten words, mark the long vowels and diphthongs, and say each aloud three times: huis, mijn, leuk, goed, nacht, water, vuur, school, buiten, vrouw. Next, pair up man/maan, bed/beet, and kat/kaart and say each pair until the short-versus-long difference is obvious. Finally, read the dialogue above aloud five times, then cover the pronunciation hints and read it again — you should be able to produce the sounds from the spelling alone.

Lesson 2: Greetings, Introductions, and Survival Phrases

This lesson gives you your first real social toolkit: greeting people politely, introducing yourself, and surviving conversations when you do not understand something. These phrases are the highest-value sentences in the language because they unlock every interaction — ordering food, asking for help, meeting people. Learn them cold before you worry about grammar, because even a flawless sentence is useless if you cannot open and close a conversation gracefully. Dutch people are famously direct, and they appreciate learners who try Dutch first, even for a few sentences, before switching to English.

Start with greetings. Hallo (hello) works any time. Goedemorgen (good morning) is used until about noon, goedemiddag (good afternoon) from noon until about six, and goedenavond (good evening) after six. Hoi and doei are informal hi and bye among friends — never use them with a shopkeeper or official on first meeting. Tot ziens (until we see each other) is the standard goodbye, tot straks (see you later today) is for later the same day, and tot morgen (until tomorrow) is for tomorrow. Fijne dag (have a nice day) is what shopkeepers say as you leave. For introductions: Ik heet... (my name is) or Mijn naam is... (my name is). Hoe heet jij? asks someone's name informally; Hoe heet u? is the formal version. Aangenaam (pleased to meet you) and Leuk je te ontmoeten (nice to meet you) both work after introductions.

Now the small-talk core. Hoe gaat het? (How are you?) is the standard question; the casual short form is Hoe gaat ie? (How is it going?). Common answers: Goed, dank je (Good, thanks), Het gaat goed (It is going well), Prima (Fine, great), Niet zo goed (Not so good), Ik ben moe (I am tired). En met jou? (And with you?) or En met u? (formal) keeps the conversation balanced. Alstublieft is please (formal), alsjeblieft the informal version; dank je wel is thank you (informal), dank u wel is thank you (formal), heel erg bedankt is thank you very much, and graag gedaan (gladly done) or geen probleem (no problem) is you are welcome. A frequent beginner mistake is answering Hoe gaat het? with a long life story; a short answer plus En met jou? gives you twice the practice and sounds natural.

Survival phrases are your safety net — memorize these before anything else. Ik begrijp het niet (I do not understand). Kunt u dat herhalen, alstublieft? (Can you repeat that, please?). Spreek wat langzamer, alstublieft (Speak a bit more slowly, please). Spreekt u Engels? (Do you speak English?). Pardon (excuse me / sorry). Het spijt me (I am sorry). Wat betekent dat? (What does that mean?). Kunt u me helpen? (Can you help me?). Ik spreek een beetje Nederlands (I speak a little Dutch). These phrases will rescue you in any Dutch-speaking place, and people respect learners who ask for clarification instead of nodding blankly.

Watch for this social mistake: Dutch runs on the je/jij versus u distinction. Use u with strangers, shopkeepers, elders, and in any formal setting; switch to je or jij only when the other person does or with clear peers and friends. Using je with a stranger is not a grammar failure — it is a social one, and it can come across as too familiar. When in doubt, start with u. A second classic trap: saying ik ben warm to mean I am hot (temperature). Dutch uses hebben for that feeling — ik heb het warm (I have it warm) — which you will meet properly in Lesson 3. Saying ik ben warm sounds like something else entirely, so learn ik heb het warm and ik heb het koud (I am cold) as fixed phrases today.

Full dialogue — a first meeting. Read aloud with the translations:

Sanne: Goedemorgen! Hoe heet u? — Good morning! What is your name? Tom: Goedemorgen. Ik heet Tom. En u? — Good morning. My name is Tom. And you? Sanne: Ik heet Sanne. Aangenaam. — My name is Sanne. Pleased to meet you. Tom: Aangenaam, Sanne. Waar komt u vandaan? — Pleased to meet you, Sanne. Where are you from? Sanne: Ik kom uit Londen. En u? — I am from London. And you? Tom: Ik kom uit Utrecht. Spreekt u Nederlands? — I am from Utrecht. Do you speak Dutch? Sanne: Een beetje. Spreek wat langzamer, alstublieft. — A little. Speak a bit more slowly, please. Tom: Natuurlijk. Hoe gaat het vandaag? — Of course. How are you today? Sanne: Goed, dank u. En met u? — Good, thanks. And with you? Tom: Goed. Tot ziens! — Good. Goodbye!

Practice: Write a self-introduction of five lines (greeting, name, where you are from, one feeling word, goodbye) and memorize it — this is your personal script for the next month. Then practice the survival phrases: cover the Dutch column, read the English, and say the Dutch from memory until you can do all eight without peeking. Finally, role-play the dialogue above twice, switching roles, saying every line aloud with the guttural g and diphthongs from Lesson 1.

Lesson 3: Zijn, Hebben, and the Present Tense

This lesson tackles the two most important verbs in Dutch — zijn (to be) and hebben (to have) — plus the present tense, which lets you describe what you do every day. Zijn and hebben are the backbone of the language: they appear in nearly every sentence, and hebben doubles as the helper verb for the past tense you will meet in Lesson 6. Master their shapes now and everything later gets easier. Dutch verb conjugation is simpler than in many European languages: there are only a few forms to learn, and the plural forms are all the same.

ZIJN (to be) — memorize this table completely: ik ben (I am), jij bent (you are, informal), hij/zij is (he/she is), wij zijn (we are), jullie zijn (you are, plural), zij zijn (they are). Use it for identity, origin, description, profession, and location: Ik ben Amerikaans (I am American), Zij is dokter (She is a doctor), Wij zijn in Amsterdam (We are in Amsterdam), Zij zijn blij (They are happy). Note that Dutch drops the article with professions, just like with identity — Zij is dokter, not a dokter. Also note that ik ben takes ben while jij bent takes bent: the t appears in the second and third person singular, which is a pattern you will see again with regular verbs.

HEBBEN (to have) — equally essential: ik heb (I have), jij hebt (you have), hij/zij heeft (he/she has), wij hebben (we have), jullie hebben (you have), zij hebben (they have). Dutch uses hebben where English uses to be for hunger, thirst, temperature, and need: Ik ben twintig jaar oud (I am twenty years old — this one does use zijn), but Ik heb honger (I am hungry), Ik heb dorst (I am thirsty), Ik heb het warm (I am hot), Ik heb het koud (I am cold), Ik heb ... nodig (I need...). This is the single most useful hebben pattern for daily life — learn ik heb honger, ik heb dorst, ik heb het warm, ik heb het koud as fixed phrases today. Also learn Ik heb geen tijd (I have no time) and Ik heb een vraag (I have a question), both of which you will use constantly.

Now the present tense of regular verbs. Dutch verbs are refreshingly simple. Take the infinitive, drop -en to get the stem, and add endings: ik + stem, jij/hij + stem + t, wij/jullie/zij + infinitive. Werken (to work): ik werk, jij werkt, hij werkt, wij werken, jullie werken, zij werken. Wonen (to live): Ik woon in Amsterdam (I live in Amsterdam), Wij wonen in een huis (We live in a house). Praten (to talk): Ik praat Nederlands (I speak Dutch), Zij praat veel (She talks a lot). Note that the ik form is the bare stem — ik werk, ik woon, ik praat — which makes the first person the easiest form in the whole language. One spelling wrinkle: if the stem ends in -v or -z, it changes to -f or -s in the ik and jij forms because of final devoicing — leven becomes ik leef, lezen becomes ik lees.

Essential irregular verbs to learn as vocabulary: GAAN (to go): ik ga, jij gaat, hij gaat, wij gaan, jullie gaan, zij gaan. Ik ga naar de markt (I go to the market). DOEN (to do): ik doe, jij doet, hij doet, wij doen, jullie doen, zij doen. Ik doe sport (I do sports). WILLEN (to want): ik wil, jij wilt, hij wil, wij willen, jullie willen, zij willen. Ik wil een koffie (I want a coffee). KUNNEN (to be able to): ik kan, jij kunt, hij kan, wij kunnen, jullie kunnen, zij kunnen. Ik kan je helpen (I can help you). ZULLEN (will/shall): ik zal, jij zult, hij zal, wij zullen, jullie zullen, zij zullen. These appear in nearly every conversation, so they repay memorization immediately. Also learn komen (to come): ik kom, jij komt, hij komt, wij komen — Ik kom uit Engeland (I come from England).

Two beginner mistakes to avoid. First, translating English do questions word-for-word: English Do you speak Dutch? is simply Spreek je Nederlands? or Spreekt u Nederlands? with rising intonation — Dutch needs no helper verb. Second, forgetting Dutch word order: in a main clause the conjugated verb is always in second position. Ik werk morgen in Amsterdam (I work tomorrow in Amsterdam) —

morgen comes before the place, because Dutch order is time, then manner, then place. Morgen werk ik in Amsterdam is also correct and means the same thing, with morgen moved to the front for emphasis.

Dialogue — two coworkers at lunch:

Jeroen: Waar werk jij? — Where do you work? Emma: Ik werk in een bank. Ik ben kassière. En jij? — I work at a bank. I am a cashier. And you? Jeroen: Ik ben leraar. Ik werk op een school. — I am a teacher. I work at a school. Emma: Ben je moe vandaag? — Are you tired today? Jeroen: Nee, het gaat goed. Wil je met me lunchen? — No, I am fine. Do you want to have lunch with me? Emma: Ja, graag. Gaan we naar het restaurant? — Yes, gladly. Shall we go to the restaurant? Jeroen: Laten we gaan. Ik heb honger. — Let us go. I am hungry.

Practice: Conjugate in writing: werken, wonen, zijn, hebben, gaan, doen. Then write six sentences about yourself: two with zijn (identity/origin/profession), two with hebben (needs/feelings), two with present-tense action verbs. Say each sentence aloud. Finally, translate to Dutch: She is a doctor. She works in a hospital. She is tired today. She wants a coffee. Check: Zij is dokter. Zij werkt in een ziekenhuis. Zij is moe vandaag. Zij wil een koffie.

Lesson 4: Numbers, Time, Days — The Everyday Vocabulary Core

This lesson builds your everyday number vocabulary: counting, telling time, and using days, months, and dates. Numbers are pure utility — prices, phone numbers, addresses, schedules — and telling the time unlocks appointments, opening hours, and travel. Dutch numbers are built on a simple pattern, with one famous quirk: numbers from 21 onward are said backwards, ones before tens, just like in German. This lesson is built for repetition and out-loud drilling, because numbers only become useful when they are automatic.

Numbers 0–20 (memorize these exactly): 0 nul, 1 een, 2 twee, 3 drie, 4 vier, 5 vijf, 6 zes, 7 zeven, 8 acht, 9 negen, 10 tien, 11 elf, 12 twaalf, 13 dertien, 14 veertien, 15 vijftien, 16 zestien, 17 zeventien, 18 achttien, 19 negentien, 20 twintig. Tens: 30 dertig, 40 veertig, 50 vijftig, 60 zestig, 70 zeventig, 80 tachtig, 90 negentig. 100 = honderd, 200 = tweehonderd, 1,000 = duizend. Now the backwards quirk: 21 is eenentwintig (one-and-twenty), 25 is vijfentwintig, 37 is zevenendertig, 48 is achtenveertig, 99 is negenennegentig. It feels strange for a week, then becomes automatic. When you hear eenentwintig, train your ear to catch the een first and the twintig second. Phone numbers are read digit by digit or in pairs: nul zes, een twee, drie vier.

Telling time: Hoe laat is het? (What time is it?). Het is een uur (1:00), Het is twee uur, Het is drie uur. Minutes: Het is tien over vier (4:10), Het is vijf voor half drie (2:25 — literally five before half three), Het is kwart over zes (6:15), Het is kwart voor acht (7:45). Here is the famous Dutch trap: half drie does NOT mean 3:30 — it means 2:30, half way to three. Half vier is 3:30, half vijf is 4:30. Every beginner gets caught by this at least once, so drill it hard: half + the coming hour = thirty minutes before that hour. For appointments: De afspraak is om drie uur (The appointment is at three o'clock). Twelve uur is noon, middernacht is midnight. Dutch often uses the 24-hour clock in writing — 15.30 for 3:30 PM — so learn to read it: achttien uur = 6 PM.

Days of the week (never capitalized in the middle of a sentence, but often written with a capital at the start): maandag, dinsdag, woensdag, donderdag, vrijdag, zaterdag, zondag. Use op for on: op maandag = on Monday, op maandag as a habit = on Mondays. Months: januari, februari, maart, april, mei, juni, juli, augustus, september, oktober, november, december. Dates: vijf mei, twintig oktober. To say what day it is: Welke dag is het vandaag? — Het is dinsdag. What is the date? Wat is de datum? — Het is twee november. Note the order: day first, then month — 2 november, never november 2. Today is vandaag, tomorrow is morgen, yesterday is gisteren, this week is deze week, next week is volgende week, last week is vorige week.

Practical money and shopping vocabulary, since numbers live there: Hoeveel kost dit? (How much does this cost?), Dit kost tien euro (This costs ten euros), Dat is te duur (That is too expensive), Dat is goedkoop (That is cheap), Heeft u wisselgeld? (Do you have change?), Mag ik de rekening? (Can I have the check?). In shops you will hear prices as bare numbers — vijfenveertig for 45 — so number fluency pays off immediately. Dutch shops often round cash payments to the nearest five cents, and many places prefer cards, so learn Kan ik met een kaart betalen? (Can I pay by card?). The euro is the currency, and prices are written with a comma for decimals: 2,50 euro.

Dialogue — at a market:

Verkoper: Goedemorgen! Wat wilt u? — Good morning! What would you like? Klant: Ik wil graag twee kilo appels, alstublieft. — I would like two kilos of apples, please. Verkoper: Dat is twaalf euro. Nog iets? — That is twelve euros. Anything else? Klant: Hoeveel kosten de sinaasappels? — How much are the oranges? Verkoper: Vijf euro per kilo. — Five euros a kilo. Klant: Geef me drie kilo. Hoe laat sluit u? — Give me three kilos. What time do you close? Verkoper: Wij sluiten om acht uur. — We close at eight. Klant: Dank u. Heeft u wisselgeld van twintig? — Thanks. Do you have change for twenty? Verkoper: Ja, natuurlijk. — Yes, of course.

Practice: Count aloud from 0 to 100 without stopping — including the backwards 21s, 30s, and 40s — then backwards from 50 to 0. Write today's date, your birthday, and three appointment times in Dutch. Then drill time: say the current time in Dutch every 15 minutes for two hours (Hoe laat is het? — Het is...), paying special attention to the half-hour forms. Finally, memorize the market dialogue's price lines and role-play a shopping trip where you buy three different items, asking Hoeveel kost dit? each time.

Lesson 5: Asking Questions — Directions, Shopping, and Small-Talk Dialogues

This lesson turns you into someone who can get answers: how to form every kind of question, ask for directions, handle a full shopping conversation, and keep small talk going. Up to now you have mostly made statements; questions are what make you independent in a Dutch-speaking place. The good news: Dutch questions are simple. You already know the main pattern from the dialogues so far, and this lesson makes it automatic.

Dutch has two main ways to ask a question. 1) Inversion: flip the subject and verb — Spreek je Nederlands? (Do you speak Dutch?), Kom je morgen? (Are you coming tomorrow?). This is the most common pattern in speech. 2) Question word first, then verb, then subject: Waar woon je? (Where do you live?), Hoeveel kost dit? (How much does this cost?), Wanneer ga je? (When are you going?). In both patterns the verb stays in second position, which is the golden rule of Dutch word order. The core question words: wat (what — Wat is dat? = What is that?), wie (who), welke (which), waar (where), wanneer (when), waarom (why), hoe (how), hoeveel (how much/many). Note that waarom (why) and daarom (therefore) are a pair — Waarom ben je laat? Daarom ben ik laat is not natural, but you will hear Daarom... (That is why...) as an answer starter.

Directions vocabulary is survival-level. Waar is...? (Where is...?), Hoe kom ik bij...? (How do I get to...?), Is het dichtbij? (Is it near?), Is het ver? (Is it far?), rechts (to the right), links (to the left), rechtdoor (straight ahead), op de hoek (on the corner), tussen... en... (between... and...). When someone answers, listen for: ga rechtdoor (go straight), sla rechts af (turn right), sla links af (turn left), steek over (cross). Polite opener: Pardon, waar is het station, alstublieft? (Excuse me, where is the station, please?). And the lifesaver follow-up: Kunt u het me op de kaart laten zien? (Can you show me on the map?). Dutch towns are full of bikes, so also learn: Pas op voor de fietsers! (Watch out for the cyclists!) — you will hear it, and you will need it.

Shopping questions beyond prices: Heeft u...? (Do you have...?), Is er...? (Is there...? / Are there...?), Mag ik het passen? (Can I try it on?), Heeft u een grotere maat / kleinere maat? (Do you have a bigger/smaller size?), Kan ik met een kaart betalen? (Can I pay by card?), Hoe laat opent u? (What time do you open?). Small-talk questions that keep conversations alive: Waar komt u vandaan? (Where are you from?), Wat doet u voor werk? (What do you do for work?), Bent u hier al lang? (Have you been here long?), Vindt u de stad leuk? (Do you like the city?), Wat raadt u aan? (What do you recommend?). Two mistakes to avoid: saying Wat is uw naam? instead of Hoe heet u? (nobody says the first), and answering a question you only half understood — use Ik begrijp het niet goed, kunt u het herhalen? instead of guessing.

Dialogue 1 — asking directions:

Toerist: Pardon, waar is het museum, alstublieft? — Excuse me, where is the museum, please? Voorbijganger: Het is dichtbij. Ga rechtdoor en sla links af. — It is near. Keep going straight and turn left. Toerist: Is het ver te voet? — Is it far on foot? Voorbijganger: Nee, het is vijf minuten. Het is tussen de bank en de apotheek. — No, it is five minutes. It is between the bank and the pharmacy. Toerist: Heel erg bedankt. Hoe heet deze straat? — Thank you very much. What is this street called? Voorbijganger: Dit is de Prinsengracht. — This is the Prinsengracht.

Dialogue 2 — small talk at a café:

Lotte: Waar komt u vandaan? — Where are you from? Mark: Ik kom uit België. En u? — I am from Belgium. And you? Lotte: Ik kom hier vandaan, uit Amsterdam. Bent u hier al lang? — I am from here, from Amsterdam. Have you been here long? Mark: Twee weken. Ik vind de stad heel leuk. — For two weeks. I like the city a lot. Lotte: Wat raadt u aan om te bezoeken? — What do you recommend visiting? Mark: Ik wil morgen naar het park. Waarom komt u niet mee? — I want to go to the park tomorrow. Why do you not come along? Lotte: Goed idee! Hoe laat? — Good idea! At what time? Mark: Om tien uur. We zien elkaar hier. — At ten. See you here.

Practice: Write ten questions — two each for directions, shopping, time, personal info, and opinions — and say each aloud with inversion. Then memorize the direction-giving sequence (rechtdoor, rechts, links, op de hoek) and give directions from your front door to the nearest shop, in Dutch, out loud. Finally, role-play Dialogue 2 with a partner or alone, then change three details (city, time, place) and run it again — this is how small talk becomes automatic.

Lesson 6: Talking About the Past — The Perfect Tense

This lesson gives you the past tense — the perfect tense — so you can say what you did, where you went, and what happened. Without a past tense you are stuck in the eternal present; with it, you can tell stories, explain your day, and answer the most common small-talk question in any language: Wat heb je gisteren gedaan? (What did you do yesterday?). Dutch conversation uses the perfect tense for almost all past talk, which is good news: learn this one tense well and you can handle nearly every past situation you will meet.

The perfect tense has a beautifully simple formula: HEBBEN (or ZIJN) in the present + the past participle. For regular weak verbs, the past participle is ge- + stem + t or d: werken → gewerkt, wonen → gewoond, praten → gepraat, koken → gekookt, spelen → gespeeld. So: ik heb gewerkt (I worked), jij hebt gewoond (you lived), hij heeft gepraat (he talked), wij hebben gespeeld (we played). The choice between t and d follows the stem: if the stem ends in a voiceless sound, use t; if it ends in a voiced sound, use d. The Dutch school trick is the word t kofschip — stems ending in t, k, f, s, ch, or p take t, and all others take d. Common irregular past participles to memorize now: gedaan (doen), gezegd (zeggen), genomen (nemen), gezien (zien), gegeten (eten), gedronken (drinken), gekomen (komen), gegaan (gaan), gekocht (kopen), gevonden (vinden), gesproken (spreken), geschreven (schrijven). Time words anchor the past: gisteren (yesterday), gisteravond (last night), vorige week (last week), vorig jaar (last year), vanochtend (this morning).

A small set of verbs — mostly verbs of movement and change of state — use ZIJN instead of hebben as the helper. The main ones: gaan (gegaan), komen (gekomen), blijven (gebleven — stayed), worden (geworden — became), zijn (geweest — been), aankomen (aangekomen — arrived), vertrekken (vertrokken — departed), vallen (gevallen — fallen), vergeten (vergeten — forgotten). So: ik ben gegaan (I went), zij is gekomen (she came), wij zijn gebleven (we stayed), hij is geworden (he became). With zijn there is no participle agreement like in some other languages — the participle never changes form, which makes Dutch easier here. With hebben there is no agreement either: zij heeft gegeten, zij hebben gewerkt. This is one of the kindest features of Dutch grammar — learn the helper, learn the participle, and you are done.

Negation goes after the helper verb: ik heb niet gewerkt (I did not work), zij is niet gekomen (she did not come). Questions work as in Lesson 5: Heb je gegeten? / Heb je gegeten? with rising intonation is the everyday form. Two beginner warnings. First, Dutch has a second past tense — the simple past, or verleden tijd — used more in writing and storytelling, and this course teaches only the perfect tense, which is enough for everyday conversation. Using the perfect tense for everything at first is understandable and will not block communication. Second, the helper trap: writing ik heb gegaan (wrong helper) instead of ik ben gegaan. Check one thing every time: is the verb a movement or change-of-state verb? If yes, use zijn; otherwise use hebben.

Dialogue — talking about the weekend:

Lotte: Wat heb je dit weekend gedaan? — What did you do this weekend? Bas: Ik ben naar het strand gegaan met mijn familie. — I went to the beach with my family. Lotte: Hoe was het? Was het mooi weer? — How was it? Was the weather nice? Bas: Ja, het was heel mooi weer. We hebben gezwommen en vis gegeten. — Yes, it was very nice weather. We swam and ate fish. Lotte: Geweldig! Ik ben thuis gebleven. Ik heb een film gekeken en een boek gelezen. — Great! I stayed home. I watched a movie and read a book. Bas: Vond je de film leuk? — Did you like the movie? Lotte: Ja, ik

vond hem geweldig. En zondag, wat heb je gedaan? — Yes, I loved it. And Sunday, what did you do? Bas: Ik heb lang geslapen en mijn ouders bezocht. — I slept in and visited my parents.

Practice: Write a paragraph of 8–10 sentences about what you did yesterday, using at least five different perfect-tense verbs (mix hebben and zijn helpers). Read it aloud twice. Then conjugate in writing: werken, wonen, gaan, komen, eten — full perfect tense, all persons, with correct helpers for the zijn verbs. Finally, answer these aloud in complete Dutch sentences: Wat heb je gisteren gegeten? Hoe laat ben je opgestaan? Wat heb je vorig weekend gedaan? Heb je deze week een film gezien?

Lesson 7: Making Plans — Future Expressions + Restaurant and Travel Dialogues

This lesson teaches you to talk about the future — making plans, accepting invitations, and handling two of the most useful real-world conversations: ordering in a restaurant and navigating travel. Future talk is what turns you from a tourist into someone who can actually live in Dutch: booking, inviting, promising, and planning.

Dutch has two easy ways to express the future. The everyday one is GAAN + INFINITIVE: Ik ga studeren (I am going to study), Wij gaan reizen (We are going to travel), Ga je mee? (Are you coming along?). Just conjugate gaan (ik ga, jij gaat, hij gaat, wij gaan, jullie gaan, zij gaan) and add any infinitive — this one structure covers most future needs. The second is ZULLEN + INFINITIVE: ik zal bellen (I will call), het zal regenen (it will rain), wij zullen komen (we will come). Zullen is used for promises, predictions, and formal plans: Ik zal je morgen bellen (I will call you tomorrow), Het zal morgen mooi weer zijn (It will be nice weather tomorrow). For this level, default to gaan + infinitive in conversation and recognize zullen when you hear it. Dutch also often uses the present tense for near-future plans: Morgen ga ik naar Amsterdam is present tense, but Morgen werk ik means tomorrow I work — the present tense with a future time word is completely normal.

Restaurant vocabulary and phrases. De menukaart (the menu), het voorgerecht (starter), het hoofdgerecht (main course), het toetje or het dessert (dessert), de drank (drink). Ordering: Ik wil graag... (I would like... — polite), Voor mij... (For me...), Wat raadt u aan? (What do you recommend?). During the meal: Het is heerlijk (It is delicious), Kunt u me ... brengen? (Can you bring me...?), Mag ik de rekening? (Can I have the check, please?). Common mistake: barking ik wil at the waiter is grammatically fine but socially blunt — ik wil graag or ik zou graag willen sounds much better. Tipping: service is included in Dutch restaurants, so leaving a tip is optional and small — rounding up the bill is the usual gesture. Dutch food to know: broodje (sandwich roll), kaas (cheese), patat (fries), stroopwafel (syrup waffle), bitterballen (fried meatballs), haring (herring), poffertjes (mini pancakes).

Travel phrases. Op de luchthaven (at the airport): Waar is de gate...? (Where is gate...?), Mijn vlucht vertrekt om... (My flight leaves at...), Ik heb een reservering (I have a reservation). In het hotel: Ik wil graag een tweepersoonskamer (I would like a double room), Hoe laat is het ontbijt? (What time is breakfast?), Heeft u wifi? (Do you have wifi?). Problems: Ik ben mijn paspoort kwijt (I lost my passport), Ik heb hulp nodig (I need help), Waar is een apotheek? (Where is a pharmacy?). At the station: Op welk perron vertrekt de trein? (From which platform does the train leave?), Een kaartje naar Amsterdam, alstublieft (A ticket to Amsterdam, please). Note how much of this reuses Lessons 2–5 — the course is compounding.

Dialogue 1 — at a restaurant:

Ober: Goedenavond. Heeft u een reservering? — Good evening. Do you have a reservation? Claire: Ja, op naam van Claire. — Yes, under the name Claire. Ober: Deze kant op, alstublieft. Wilt u de menukaart zien? — This way, please. Would you like to see the menu? Claire: Ja, dank u. Wat is het gerecht van de dag? — Yes, thanks. What is the dish of the day? Ober: Dat is kip met rijst. Het is heerlijk. — It is chicken with rice. It is delicious. Claire: Ik wil dat graag, en een salade. En jij, Jeroen? — I would like that, and a salad. And you, Jeroen? Jeroen: Voor mij de vis. Kunt u me water brengen, alstublieft? — For me, the fish. Can you bring me water, please? Ober: Meteen. — Right away. (later) Claire: Mag ik de rekening? — Can I have the check, please? Ober: Dat is vijfendertig euro. — That is

thirty-five euros.

Dialogue 2 — making weekend plans:

Niels: Wat ga je zaterdag doen? — What are you going to do Saturday? Emma: Ik ga mijn zus bezoeken. En jij? — I am going to visit my sister. And you? Niels: Ik ga zaterdagochtend naar de markt. Wil je mee? — I am going to go to the market on Saturday morning. Do you want to come? Emma: Ja, graag. Hoe laat zien we elkaar? — Yes, gladly. What time shall we meet? Niels: Om negen uur. Daarna gaan we samen lunchen. — At nine. Afterwards we will have lunch together. Emma: Perfect. Ik zal je vrijdag bellen om te bevestigen. — Perfect. I will call you Friday to confirm.

Practice: Write three plans for next week using gaan + infinitive, and three promises using zullen + infinitive. Then write a full restaurant dialogue (8+ lines) where you order a starter, main, drink, and dessert, ask for the check, and react to the food. Say it all aloud. Finally, plan a real weekend in Dutch: where you will go, who you will see, what you will eat — five sentences minimum, out loud.

Lesson 8: Conversation Practice Drills + Your 30-Day Practice Plan

This final lesson does two things: it gives you structured conversation drills that combine everything from Lessons 1–7, and it hands you a concrete 30-day plan so your Dutch keeps growing after the course ends. A course can teach you the material, but only a routine turns material into fluency — so treat this lesson as the most important one to actually do. Dutch rewards speakers who try: even a short Dutch sentence earns goodwill, and every conversation you attempt makes the next one easier.

Drill 1 — The Two-Minute Monologue. Set a timer for two minutes and talk about your day — present for routines (Ik sta om zeven uur op...), perfect tense for what already happened (Ik heb koffie gedronken...), future for what is next (Ik ga werken...). Do not stop to look up words; describe around them (het ding om mee te schrijven = the thing for writing, if you forget pen). Fluency is built by keeping going, not by being perfect. Do this drill daily — it is the single highest-value exercise in this course.

Drill 2 — Question Tennis. Write 20 question words and topics on slips of paper (Waar...?, Wanneer...?, Waarom...?). Draw one and answer in two full sentences, then ask a follow-up question. Example: draw Waar? → Waar ben je geboren? Ik ben geboren in Ohio. Draw Waarom? → Waarom leer je Nederlands? Ik leer Nederlands omdat ik wil reizen. If you practice with a partner, rally: they answer your question, then ask you one back. Ten minutes of question tennis covers more ground than an hour of passive review.

Drill 3 — Situation Cards. Write real situations on cards: Your hotel has no hot water — complain politely, Ask a stranger for directions to the pharmacy, Order dinner for two including a dietary restriction, Small talk with a taxi driver for five minutes. Draw a card and perform it aloud, both roles. These force you to combine vocabulary, grammar, and politeness under mild pressure — exactly what real conversations feel like. Rotate through all your cards weekly.

Two mistakes that stall learners at this stage. First, the perfection trap: refusing to speak until every conjugation is certain. Dutch speakers are generous with beginners who try; they cannot help beginners who stay silent. Speak with 70% accuracy today rather than 100% accuracy never. Second, passive consumption: re-reading lessons and calling it study. Reading feels productive but builds recognition, not production. Every minute of speaking aloud counts triple compared to silent review — your mouth needs the reps. A third Dutch-specific trap: switching to English the moment someone answers in English. It is tempting, and Dutch people switch fast, but say Ik oefen mijn Nederlands, mag ik in het Nederlands doorgaan? (I am practicing my Dutch, may I continue in Dutch?) and most people will happily stay in Dutch.

Your 30-day practice plan. Week 1 — Foundations lock-in: 20 minutes daily. Days 1–2: re-read Lessons 1–2 dialogues aloud. Days 3–4: conjugate zijn/hebben/present verbs from Lesson 3 out loud. Days 5–7: number and time drills from Lesson 4 until 0–100 is automatic, including the backwards eenentwintig forms and the half-hour trap. Week 2 — Conversation ignition: 25 minutes daily. Daily two-minute monologue plus question tennis; re-do the Lesson 5 and 6 dialogues with changed details. Week 3 — Real situations: 30 minutes daily. One situation card per day performed aloud; write a daily journal of five Dutch sentences about your day (present + perfect + future mix). Week 4 — Live fire: 30+ minutes daily. Have at least three real conversations — with a tutor, a language-exchange partner, a Dutch-speaking neighbor, or even ordering food in Dutch. Record one, listen back, and note your three most frequent errors to drill next month.

Track it simply: a notebook page per day with the date, minutes practiced, and one line about what felt easier than last week. If you miss a day, never miss twice — the streak matters more than any single session. After 30 days, repeat the cycle with harder situation cards and longer monologues; you now own a system, not just a course.

Practice: Do Drill 1 right now — two full minutes about your day in Dutch, out loud, no stopping. Then write out your personal Week 1 schedule with exact times (e.g., 7:30 AM, 20 minutes, Lessons 1–2 dialogues). Finally, write five situation cards of your own based on your life — travel you plan, restaurants you visit, people you meet — and perform the first one today. Veel succes — you are a Dutch speaker now. Keep speaking.
