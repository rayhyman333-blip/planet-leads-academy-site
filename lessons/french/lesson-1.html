This lesson teaches you to pronounce French clearly from the start. French spelling looks intimidating, but the sounds follow patterns you can learn in one sitting. Your goal in this lesson is not a perfect accent — it is clarity. If your vowels are clean and you respect the nasal sounds and silent letters, French speakers will understand you even while you are still learning words.

Start with the pure vowels, which are steadier than English vowels. A sounds like 'ah' in father: papa (pah-PAH, dad). E has several sounds: the closed E sounds like 'ay' in day — café (kah-FAY, coffee); the open E sounds like 'eh' in bed — lait (leh, milk); and the unstressed E in the middle of words is often a soft 'uh' or dropped entirely — fromage (froh-MAHZH, cheese). I sounds like 'ee' in see: merci (mehr-SEE, thank you). O sounds like 'oh' in go: bonjour (bohn-ZHOOR, hello). U is the famous hard one: round your lips as if to say 'oo' but say 'ee' instead — tu (tew, you), rue (rew, street). The letter combination OU is the regular 'oo' sound: vous (voo, you formal), bonjour's 'jour'. Practice the pair tu (tew) versus tout (too) until your mouth feels the difference.

Now the nasal vowels — the sounds that make French sound like French. A nasal vowel is produced by letting air flow through your nose while the vowel is voiced. Do not pronounce the N or M fully; they just signal the nose. AN/EN: en (ahn) — enfant (ahn-FAHN, child), vent (vahn, wind). IN/AIN/EIN: vin (vahn — yes, it sounds like vent's cousin, thinner) — vin (wine), pain (pahn, bread). ON: on (ohn) — bon (bohn, good), maison (meh-ZOHN, house). UN: un (uhn) — brun (bruhn, brown). Train each one: hold the vowel, then let your nose join in, and stop before your tongue touches the roof of your mouth for the N. Beginners often pronounce the N like an English N — bon becomes 'bonn' — and it instantly sounds wrong, so drill this daily.

Silent letters are everywhere in French, and they are your friends once you know the rules. Final consonants are usually silent: petit is pronounced puh-TEE (the T is silent), and parlez drops its Z sound to become pahr-LAY. But C, R, F, and L are often pronounced at the end — remember 'CaReFuL': sac (sahk, bag), mer (mehr, sea), vif (veef, lively), cheval (shuh-VAHL, horse). The H is always silent in French: hôtel (oh-TEHL, hotel), homme (ohm, man). The E at the end of words is silent too: table (tahbl, table), porte (pohrt, door). Liaison is the elegant connecting rule: when a word ends in a normally silent consonant and the next word starts with a vowel, you link them — les amis is pronounced lay-ZAH-mee (the friends), nous avons becomes noo-ZAH-vohn (we have). You will hear this constantly.

French word stress is simple: it almost always falls on the LAST syllable of a phrase group, lightly and evenly. Bonjour is bohn-ZHOOR, merci is mehr-SEE, restaurant is reh-stoh-RAHN. Do not stress French words the English way — never say 'RES-tau-rant' in French. The rhythm is flatter and more even than English; think of each syllable getting roughly equal time with a gentle lift at the end.

Here is a short dialogue using only sounds from this lesson. Read it aloud slowly:

Marie: Bonjour ! Comment vous appelez-vous ? (bohn-ZHOOR koh-mahn voo zah-play VOO) — Hello! What is your name?
Pierre: Je m'appelle Pierre. Et vous ? (zhuh mah-pehl pee-YEHR ay voo) — My name is Pierre. And you?
Marie: Je m'appelle Marie. Enchantée. (zhuh mah-pehl mah-REE ahn-shahn-TAY) — My name is Marie. Delighted to meet you.
Pierre: Enchanté. Vous parlez français ? (ahn-shahn-TAY voo pahr-LAY frahn-SEH) — Delighted. Do you speak French?
Marie: Oui, un peu. (wee uhn puh) — Yes, a little.

Say the whole dialogue five times today; it is your first French conversation.

Practice: Write out these ten words, mark the nasal vowels, and say each aloud three times: enfant, bon, maison, vin, pain, comment, chanson, temps, pont. Next, pair up tu/tout, rue/roue, dessus/dessous and say each pair until the U versus OU difference is obvious. Finally, read the dialogue above aloud five times, then cover the pronunciation hints and read it again — you should be able to produce the sounds from the spelling alone.
