This lesson builds your everyday number vocabulary: counting, telling time, and using days, months, and dates. Numbers are pure utility — prices, phone numbers, addresses, schedules — and telling the time unlocks appointments, opening hours, and travel. French numbers have a few famous quirks (hello, quatre-vingts), so this lesson is built for repetition and out-loud drilling.

Numbers 0–20 (memorize these exactly): 0 zéro, 1 un/une, 2 deux, 3 trois, 4 quatre, 5 cinq, 6 six (sees), 7 sept (seht), 8 huit (weet), 9 neuf (nuhf), 10 dix (dees), 11 onze, 12 douze, 13 treize, 14 quatorze, 15 quinze, 16 seize, 17 dix-sept, 18 dix-huit, 19 dix-neuf, 20 vingt (vahn). Tens: 30 trente, 40 quarante, 50 cinquante, 60 soixante, 70 soixante-dix (sixty-ten!), 80 quatre-vingts (four-twenties!), 90 quatre-vingt-dix (four-twenty-ten!). Yes, 70–99 are built on 60 and 80: 71 = soixante-et-onze, 77 = soixante-dix-sept, 85 = quatre-vingt-cinq, 99 = quatre-vingt-dix-neuf. It feels strange for a week, then becomes automatic. 100 = cent, 200 = deux cents, 1,000 = mille. Un becomes une before feminine nouns: un café, une table.

Telling time: Quelle heure est-il ? (What time is it?). Il est une heure (1:00), Il est deux heures, Il est trois heures... Minutes: Il est quatre heures dix (4:10), Il est six heures et demie (6:30), Il est huit heures moins le quart (7:45 — 'eight minus a quarter', very common). Midi is noon, minuit is midnight. For appointments: le rendez-vous est à trois heures (the appointment is at three). French often uses the 24-hour clock in writing — 15h30 for 3:30 PM — so learn to read it: dix-huit heures = 6 PM. A classic mistake is forgetting heures is plural after one: Il est une heure but Il est deux heures.

Days of the week (masculine, never capitalized): lundi, mardi, mercredi, jeudi, vendredi, samedi, dimanche. Use le for 'on': le lundi = on Monday, le lundi as a habit = on Mondays. Months: janvier, février, mars, avril, mai, juin, juillet, août, septembre, octobre, novembre, décembre. Dates: le cinq mai, le vingt octobre. To say what day it is: Quel jour sommes-nous ? — Nous sommes mardi. What is the date? Quelle est la date ? — C'est le deux novembre. Note the order: day first, then month — le 2 novembre, never 'novembre 2'. Exception: the first of the month is le premier — le premier janvier.

Practical money and shopping vocabulary, since numbers live there: Combien ça coûte ? (How much does it cost?), Ça coûte dix euros (It costs ten euros), C'est très cher (It's very expensive), C'est bon marché (It's cheap), Vous avez la monnaie ? (Do you have change?), L'addition, s'il vous plaît (The check, please). In shops you will hear prices as bare numbers — 'quarante-cinq' for 45 — so number fluency pays off immediately. Phone numbers are read in pairs: 06 12 34 56 78 = zéro-six, douze, trente-quatre...

Dialogue — at a market:

Vendeur: Bonjour ! Vous désirez ? — Hello! What would you like?
Client: Je voudrais deux kilos de pommes, s'il vous plaît. — I'd like two kilos of apples, please.
Vendeur: Ça fait douze euros. Autre chose ? — That's twelve euros. Anything else?
Client: Combien coûtent les oranges ? — How much are the oranges?
Vendeur: Cinq euros le kilo. — Five euros a kilo.
Client: Donnez-m'en trois kilos. Vous fermez à quelle heure ? — Give me three kilos. What time do you close?
Vendeur: Nous fermons à huit heures. — We close at eight.
Client: Merci. Vous avez la monnaie de vingt ? — Thanks. Do you have change for twenty?
Vendeur: Oui, bien sûr. — Yes, of course.

Practice: Count aloud from 0 to 100 without stopping — including the tricky 70s, 80s, and 90s — then backwards from 50 to 0. Write today's date, your birthday, and three appointment times in French. Then drill time: say the current time in French every 15 minutes for two hours (Quelle heure est-il ? — Il est...). Finally, memorize the market dialogue's price lines and role-play a shopping trip where you buy three different items, asking Combien ça coûte ? each time.
