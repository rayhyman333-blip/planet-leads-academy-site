This final lesson does two things: it gives you structured conversation drills that combine everything from Lessons 1–7, and it hands you a concrete 30-day plan so your French keeps growing after the course ends. A course can teach you the material, but only a routine turns material into fluency — so treat this lesson as the most important one to actually do.

Drill 1 — The Two-Minute Monologue. Set a timer for two minutes and talk about your day — present for routines (Je me lève à sept heures...), passé composé for what already happened (J'ai pris un café...), future for what is next (Je vais travailler...). Do not stop to look up words; describe around them (la chose pour écrire = the thing for writing, if you forget 'pen'). Fluency is built by keeping going, not by being perfect. Do this drill daily — it is the single highest-value exercise in this course.

Drill 2 — Question Tennis. Write 20 question words and topics on slips of paper (Où...?, Quand...?, Pourquoi...?). Draw one and answer in two full sentences, then ask a follow-up question. Example: draw 'Où ?' → Où êtes-vous né ? Je suis né en Ohio. Draw 'Pourquoi ?' → Pourquoi apprenez-vous le français ? J'apprends le français parce que je veux voyager. If you practice with a partner, rally: they answer your question, then ask you one back. Ten minutes of question tennis covers more ground than an hour of passive review.

Drill 3 — Situation Cards. Write real situations on cards: 'Your hotel has no hot water — complain politely', 'Ask a stranger for directions to the pharmacy', 'Order dinner for two including a dietary restriction', 'Small talk with a taxi driver for five minutes'. Draw a card and perform it aloud, both roles. These force you to combine vocabulary, grammar, and politeness under mild pressure — exactly what real conversations feel like. Rotate through all your cards weekly.

Two mistakes that stall learners at this stage. First, the perfection trap: refusing to speak until every conjugation is certain. French speakers are generous with beginners who try; they cannot help beginners who stay silent. Speak with 70% accuracy today rather than 100% accuracy never. Second, passive consumption: re-reading lessons and calling it study. Reading feels productive but builds recognition, not production. Every minute of speaking aloud counts triple compared to silent review — your mouth needs the reps.

Your 30-day practice plan. Week 1 — Foundations lock-in: 20 minutes daily. Days 1–2: re-read Lessons 1–2 dialogues aloud. Days 3–4: conjugate être/avoir/present verbs from Lesson 3 out loud. Days 5–7: number and time drills from Lesson 4 until 0–100 is automatic. Week 2 — Conversation ignition: 25 minutes daily. Daily two-minute monologue plus question tennis; re-do the Lesson 5 and 6 dialogues with changed details. Week 3 — Real situations: 30 minutes daily. One situation card per day performed aloud; write a daily journal of five French sentences about your day (present + passé composé + future mix). Week 4 — Live fire: 30+ minutes daily. Have at least three real conversations — with a tutor, a language-exchange partner, a French-speaking neighbor, or even ordering food in French. Record one, listen back, and note your three most frequent errors to drill next month.

Track it simply: a notebook page per day with the date, minutes practiced, and one line about what felt easier than last week. If you miss a day, never miss twice — the streak matters more than any single session. After 30 days, repeat the cycle with harder situation cards and longer monologues; you now own a system, not just a course.

Practice: Do Drill 1 right now — two full minutes about your day in French, out loud, no stopping. Then write out your personal Week 1 schedule with exact times (e.g., '7:30 AM, 20 minutes, Lessons 1–2 dialogues'). Finally, write five situation cards of your own based on your life — travel you plan, restaurants you visit, people you meet — and perform the first one today. Bonne chance — you are a French speaker now. Keep speaking.
