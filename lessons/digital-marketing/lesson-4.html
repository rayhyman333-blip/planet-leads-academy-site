This lesson teaches email marketing: building a list of people who asked to hear from you and writing emails they actually open. By the end you will create an ethical signup offer, set up a simple welcome sequence, write emails using a proven structure, and follow the basic rules that keep you out of spam folders and on the right side of the law. Email is the least fashionable channel and among the most effective: unlike social followers, your list is an audience you own — no algorithm decides whether they see your message.

People join lists in exchange for something valuable, so start with a signup offer (often called a lead magnet): a free resource so useful your audience would reasonably pay for it. For the meal-planning example: "the 30-minute dinner starter pack — five recipes plus a printable shopping list." The offer must connect directly to your paid product — a freebie that attracts the wrong audience is worse than none. Place signup opportunities where attention already flows: at the end of your best content, on your profile link page, and on your website if you have one. Ask only for the email address at first; every extra field reduces signups. And the non-negotiable rule: never add people without clear permission, never buy lists. Permission is the entire foundation — one bought list poisons your sender reputation and can get your account suspended by your email provider.

The welcome sequence is the highest-leverage email you will ever write: a short series sent automatically to each new subscriber over their first week. Email 1 (immediately): deliver the promised freebie, introduce yourself in two sentences, and set expectations ("one useful email every Tuesday"). Email 2 (day 2): your best single tip or the most common beginner mistake in your topic — pure value, no selling. Email 3 (day 4): your story or your method — why you do this and how you think about the problem — which builds the trust that selling requires. Email 4 (day 7): introduce your offer naturally, as the next step for people who liked the free material. Write these four before you worry about anything else; they work for you around the clock from the day your list starts.

Ongoing emails follow a simple structure that respects the reader's time. Subject line: specific and honest — "3 dinners under 30 minutes" beats "You won't believe this!!!" and never misleads about the contents. Opening: one line connecting to the reader's situation. Body: one idea, scannable, with short paragraphs and one clear point — emails that try to say five things say nothing. Call to action: one link, one action, stated plainly. Sign-off: your name, like a person wrote it. Send on a consistent schedule you can sustain — weekly is the standard for beginners — because regularity builds the habit of opening. Write in a conversational voice; the emails people love read like a knowledgeable friend, not a brochure.

Two mistakes to avoid. First, emailing only when you have something to sell: subscribers who hear from you three times a year, always with a pitch, unsubscribe or ignore you. The ratio that works is roughly four value emails to one promotional email — earn the right to sell by being useful between offers. Second, ignoring the technical basics: always include a working unsubscribe link and your contact information (required by anti-spam law in most countries), never use deceptive subject lines, and watch your provider's spam guidance — high complaint rates from bought or surprised lists get you filtered to spam for everyone. Permission, value, and consistency are the whole game.

Practice: (1) Design your signup offer: name it, describe exactly what the subscriber receives, and write the three-sentence pitch for it. (2) Write your four-email welcome sequence in full — each email with subject line, body under 250 words, and one call to action. (3) Draft two regular weekly emails on topics from your Lesson 2 content list, following the subject-opening-body-action structure. (4) Write your sending schedule and your personal rules: frequency, value-to-promotion ratio, and the unsubscribe and contact lines you will include. Read every email aloud — if it sounds like a person, send it; if it sounds like a corporation, rewrite it.
