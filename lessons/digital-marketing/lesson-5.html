This lesson teaches how search engines work and what you can honestly do to have your pages found by people searching for what you offer. By the end you will understand how search engines discover and rank pages, research the phrases your audience actually searches, and optimize a page using durable principles rather than tricks. Search traffic is uniquely valuable: the searcher arrives with intent — they typed a question because they want an answer — which makes them far more likely to become a customer than someone who scrolled past an ad.

Search engines do three things: crawl (software visits pages by following links), index (they store and understand what each page is about), and rank (for each search, they order the indexed pages by relevance and quality). You cannot control the ranking directly, but you can make your pages easy to crawl, clear to understand, and genuinely deserving of a high rank. That last phrase is the entire honest philosophy of search optimization: the engine's goal is to satisfy searchers, so pages that best satisfy searchers tend to rank. Tactics that try to look satisfying without being satisfying — keyword stuffing, copied content, fake links — work briefly at best and get pages penalized. Everything in this lesson is the slow honest path, which is the only one that lasts.

Keyword research means learning the actual phrases your audience types. Start with your Lesson 2 question list — those questions are searches waiting to happen. Expand them by typing the beginnings into a search engine and noting the suggestions it offers; those suggestions are real searches by real people. For each phrase, judge two things: relevance (would the searcher want my page?) and competition (are the current top results strong, established pages I cannot beat yet?). Beginners should target specific longer phrases — "30-minute vegetarian weeknight dinners" rather than "recipes" — because fewer strong pages compete for them and the searchers' intent is clearer. Organize your targets in a simple table: phrase, what the searcher wants, which page of yours will serve them, and whether that page exists yet. One page per phrase, one phrase per page — a page trying to rank for ten things ranks for none.

On-page optimization is making each page unmistakably about its phrase. Put the phrase in the page title (the headline shown in search results), in the main heading, and naturally in the first paragraph — naturally is the key word; write for humans first and the phrase will appear on its own. Write the best answer to the searcher's question that you honestly can: complete, specific, with examples — search engines measure satisfaction through behavior like whether searchers click your result and stay. Use descriptive headings that break the page into sections, add helpful images with descriptive filenames and alt text, and link to your other relevant pages so crawlers and readers can navigate. Finally, make the page fast and readable on phones: a large share of searches happen on mobile, and slow or broken mobile pages lose regardless of content quality.

Two things beyond the page matter enormously. First, links from other sites: when reputable pages link to yours, search engines read it as a vote of confidence. Earn links the honest way — create the best resource on the topic so people reference it, contribute genuine expertise to discussions in your field, and build relationships with others who publish. Never buy links; search engines penalize it. Second, patience and maintenance: new pages typically take weeks or months to rank, and rankings shift as competitors improve. Review your key pages twice a year: update outdated facts, strengthen thin sections, and confirm the page still serves its phrase better than anything else you have seen.

Two mistakes to avoid. First, writing for the engine instead of the searcher: awkward keyword repetition that no human would say. If a sentence sounds strange read aloud, rewrite it — the engines have long rewarded natural writing. Second, expecting fast results and quitting: search is a six-to-twelve-month channel. Pair it with social and email (which work immediately) while search compounds in the background, and judge it on the trend of your target phrases, not week-to-week wiggles.

Practice: (1) Build your keyword table: twenty phrases from your audience's questions, each with searcher intent, competition judgment, and the page that will serve it. (2) Pick your three best opportunities and outline each page: title, main heading, the question it answers completely, and its sections. (3) Write one full page (700+ words) following the on-page principles. (4) List five honest ways you could earn links to it — specific sites, communities, or people, with what value you would offer them first. Save the table; Lesson 8's plan will schedule this work across the quarter.
