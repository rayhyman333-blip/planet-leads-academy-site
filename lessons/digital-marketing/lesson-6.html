This lesson teaches paid advertising: how ad platforms work, how to set a budget you can afford, and how to write ads that earn clicks from the right people. By the end you will understand the auction model, calculate what a click is worth to you, structure a small test campaign on paper, and know the warning signs of wasted spend. Paid ads are the fastest channel — traffic starts the day you launch — and the most dangerous for beginners, because every mistake costs money by the click. This lesson keeps you in paper exercises; spend real money only after Lesson 7's measurement skills are in place.

Ad platforms run auctions: when a user loads a page, advertisers' ads compete, and the winner's ad shows. The winner is not simply the highest bidder — platforms multiply your bid by a quality score reflecting how relevant and engaging your ad is, because showing bad ads drives users away. This is good news for beginners: a highly relevant ad from a small budget routinely beats a lazy ad from a big one. You pay per click (each visitor costs you) or per thousand impressions (each batch of views costs you); beginners should start with per-click, since you pay only for actual visitors. Costs vary enormously by industry and keyword — competitive terms cost many dollars per click, specific niche terms cost cents — which is why the math below comes before any spending.

The math decides whether ads can work for you at all. Work backwards from your offer: if your product sells for $50 with $30 profit per sale, and historically one in fifty visitors buys (a 2% conversion rate), then each visitor is worth $30 × 2% = $0.60 to you. That is your break-even cost per click — pay less and you profit, pay more and you lose money on every visitor. If you have no history, estimate conservatively and plan a small test purely to measure your real conversion rate. Set a test budget you can afford to lose completely — for most beginners learning, that means a small fixed amount like the cost of a dinner out, spent over one to two weeks — because the first campaign's job is buying information, not profit. Never fund ads with money you need, never "scale up" a campaign that is losing money on the math, and set the platform's daily budget cap on day one so a mistake cannot spend more than you planned.

Structure a campaign like a scientist. One campaign, one audience, one offer — beginners who advertise five products to three audiences in one campaign learn nothing because no result can be traced to a cause. Write three ad variations testing one difference (three headlines, same body), send all clicks to one dedicated page matching the ad's promise, and run for at least a week or one hundred clicks before judging — whichever comes later — because small samples lie. The dedicated page matters enormously: an ad promising "30-minute dinners" that lands on your homepage converts a fraction of one landing on a page headlined "30-minute dinners" with the signup right there. Message match between ad and page is the cheapest conversion improvement in advertising.

Read results with the funnel: impressions → clicks → page actions. Click-through rate (clicks divided by impressions) measures your ad: below expectations means the ad or targeting is wrong — rewrite the headline, tighten the audience. Conversion rate (actions divided by clicks) measures your page: good click volume with no signups means the page fails the ad's promise — fix the page before spending more. Cost per acquisition (total spend divided by customers) measures the business: compare it to your break-even math from the start. Kill quickly what the numbers condemn — pausing a losing ad after the test budget is discipline, not failure — and put more behind what the numbers bless, increasing budgets gradually while watching that costs do not creep up as you scale.

Two mistakes to avoid. First, sending ad traffic to a generic homepage: every extra scroll and competing link bleeds conversions, and beginners blame the ad when the page was the problem. One ad, one page, one action. Second, optimizing for cheap clicks instead of customers: the ad that gets the most clicks is often the most sensationally misleading, attracting visitors who never buy. Judge ads by cost per customer, never by click volume alone — a quiet ad that brings buyers beats a loud ad that brings tourists.

Practice (on paper, no spending): (1) Compute your break-even cost per click for your running product: profit per sale × estimated conversion rate. Write the numbers down. (2) Set a hypothetical test budget you could afford to lose, with a daily cap. (3) Write three ad variations — same offer, three different headlines — each under the platform's typical length, each honest about what the click leads to. (4) Outline the dedicated landing page: headline matching the ad, three outcome-focused bullet points, one signup form, nothing else. (5) Write your kill rules in advance: "if after 100 clicks the cost per signup exceeds $X, I pause and rewrite." Deciding the rules before spending is what keeps advertising a tool instead of a gamble.
