This lesson teaches you to read written music: the staff, the treble clef, and how note values and rhythm work. Reading is simply a map - it tells you which key to press, how long to hold it, and when. You do not need to memorize everything today; you need to understand the system and be able to work a short line of music out slowly. Every piece in this course is written down, so the reading skills you build here unlock everything that follows.

The staff is the set of five horizontal lines that music is written on, with four spaces between them. In the treble clef - the curly symbol at the start that wraps around the second line - the lines from bottom to top are E, G, B, D, F, which many players remember with the phrase "Every Good Boy Does Fine." The spaces from bottom to top spell F, A, C, E, which spells the word "face." Notes can also sit on short extra lines above or below the staff, called ledger lines; middle C, for example, sits on a ledger line just below the treble staff. Point to random lines and spaces and name them until the answers come without hesitation.

Now rhythm. A whole note is an open oval held for four beats. A half note is an open oval with a stem, held for two beats. A quarter note is a filled oval with a stem, held for one beat. An eighth note looks like a quarter note with a flag or beam and lasts half a beat - two of them fit into one beat. Rests are the symbols for silence, and they follow the same values: a whole rest hangs from a line and means four beats of silence, a quarter rest means one beat. The time signature at the start of a piece tells you the beat: 4/4 means four beats per measure, with the quarter note getting one beat. Count every rhythm out loud before you ever play it.

Time for drills. First, note-naming: point at a printed staff or draw one yourself, and name thirty notes in a row on lines and spaces, aiming for no pauses. Second, clap-and-count: clap the rhythm "quarter, quarter, half, whole" while counting "1, 2, 3-4, 1-2-3-4" out loud, and repeat it ten times until the counting feels automatic. Third, mixed rhythm reading: clap a line that mixes quarter notes, eighth-note pairs, and half notes, counting steadily, five times through. If you lose the count, slow down rather than stopping - a steady slow beat beats a fast broken one every time.

Extend your reading a little further with ledger lines above the staff. Just as middle C sits on a ledger line below, the notes continue upward: the space above the top line is G, the ledger line above that is A, and the space above that is B. Practice these the same way - point, name, check - adding ten high notes to your thirty-note naming drill. Then try the say-and-play method: look at a note, say its letter name out loud, and only then play it. This two-step process feels slow, but it builds the direct eye-to-key connection that fluent reading requires. After a week of say-and-play, drop the spoken step and let your eyes go straight to your fingers.

Two common mistakes. The first is ignoring rhythm while reading pitches: beginners focus so hard on which note to play that they hold every note for the same length. Always learn the rhythm first - clap it - then add the notes. The second is not counting out loud. Silent counting collapses under pressure; your voice keeps your timing honest. Count every beat, even when a note is held or a rest is silent, until steady counting becomes a habit you no longer have to think about.

Practice: Draw or print a short eight-measure line in 4/4 using only quarter notes, half notes, and whole notes on the treble staff. First clap and count the full rhythm three times. Then name every pitch out loud from start to finish. Finally, play the line on the piano at a slow, steady tempo, counting aloud as you play, four times through without stopping.
