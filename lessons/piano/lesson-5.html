This lesson gives your left hand a real job: playing bass notes and simple accompaniment patterns. Until now your right hand has done most of the work, but in real music the left hand provides the foundation - the low notes that anchor the harmony and the rhythmic patterns that make a piece move. You will also meet the bass clef, the staff your left hand reads, and by the end of the lesson you will play three accompaniment patterns under the C-G-Am-F progression.

The bass clef is the symbol that looks like a backward C with two dots, and it wraps around the fourth line, which is the note F - that line is sometimes called the F line. From bottom to top, the bass clef lines are G-B-D-F-A ("Good Boys Do Fine Always") and the spaces are A-C-E-G ("All Cows Eat Grass"). Middle C sits on a ledger line just above the bass staff. Take ten minutes to name bass-clef notes the same way you learned the treble clef: point, name, and check, thirty notes in a row.

Start with the simplest left-hand job: root notes. Play the root of each chord - C under the C chord, G under G, A under Am, F under F - as single whole notes, one per measure, while your right hand plays the chords. Listen to how the low note grounds the sound. Next, the root-fifth pattern: under each chord, alternate the root and the fifth above it (for C: C then G; for G: G then D; for Am: A then E; for F: F then C), playing half notes. This two-note bounce is the backbone of countless accompaniments. Finally, the broken-chord pattern: play the three chord tones one at a time, low to high and back (C-E-G-E), as quarter notes. Three patterns, one progression - this is real accompanying.

Time for drills. First, left hand alone: play the root-fifth pattern under C-G-Am-F, four beats per chord, six times through, listening for even tone between the two notes. Second, hands together in stages: play the progression with the left hand on roots and the right hand on block chords, four times; then switch the left hand to the root-fifth pattern, four times; then to broken chords, four times. Third, the quiet-left challenge: play the whole loop and deliberately make the left hand softer than the right - the bass should support, never cover, the chords above it.

Add one more pattern to your toolkit: the waltz bass. In 3/4 time - three beats per measure - the classic left-hand pattern is root on beat one, then the fifth on beats two and three: C (beat 1), G (beat 2), G (beat 3). This strong-weak-weak feel is the heartbeat of every waltz. Practice it under each chord of the progression: play C-G-Am-F with the waltz pattern, one measure per chord, six times through, emphasizing beat one slightly. Then try it hands together with simple block chords in the right hand on beats two and three only - the left hand carries beat one alone, which teaches your hands true rhythmic independence.

Two common mistakes. The first is a left hand that plays too loudly: the bass notes are naturally heavier, and your left hand presses harder without realizing it. Consciously lighten the left hand until you can hear the right-hand chords clearly on top. The second is a stiff left wrist from the unfamiliar patterns: if the wrist locks, the pattern becomes mechanical and the timing wobbles. Stop, shake the hand loose, and restart the pattern slower.

Practice: With the left hand alone, play the broken-chord pattern (root-third-fifth-third) under C-G-Am-F, four beats per chord, eight times through at a slow, steady tempo. Then add the right-hand block chords on top and play the full hands-together loop four times. Count every beat out loud until the patterns feel automatic. As a final challenge, play the loop once with the root-fifth pattern and once with broken chords, alternating, for four full cycles without stopping. Write down which pattern felt weaker and make it the first drill of your next session.
