Lesson 1: What Data Analytics Is — Turning Numbers into Decisions

Data analytics is the practice of examining data to answer a question and support a decision. It is not collecting as many numbers as possible, and it is not producing impressive charts. It is a disciplined way of thinking: start with a question that matters, find the data that can answer it, check whether that data can be trusted, analyse it carefully, and communicate what you found honestly — including what you do not know. Done well, analytics replaces guessing with evidence. Done badly, it replaces guessing with confident-looking guessing, which is worse.

Every useful analysis follows the same basic journey, even if the tools differ. First, define the question. "How are sales doing?" is too vague to analyse well. "Which product category grew most in the last quarter, and was that growth driven by more customers or by existing customers buying more?" can be answered. Second, gather the relevant data. Third, clean and prepare it — fixing errors, handling missing values, and putting it in a usable shape. Fourth, explore and analyse it, looking for patterns and testing explanations. Fifth, share the findings in a way the decision-maker can act on. Skipping the first step is the most common cause of wasted analysis: beautiful work that answers a question nobody asked.

Consider a worked example. A small shop owner notices revenue fell last month and worries the business is failing. A careful analysis breaks revenue into its parts: number of orders, average order value, and returns. The data shows orders stayed steady, but average order value fell because a popular higher-priced item was out of stock for two weeks, pushing customers to cheaper alternatives. The problem is not demand; it is stock availability. The action is to fix ordering for that item, not to discount everything in panic. Without breaking the number down, the owner might have made the wrong decision with confidence.

Data comes in types that shape how you handle it. Numbers you can measure, like price and quantity, behave differently from categories, like product type or region, and from dates, which let you study trends over time. Text, such as customer comments, needs different methods again. Knowing your data types prevents silly errors, like averaging category codes or treating dates as plain numbers. It also helps you choose the right summary: counts and shares for categories, averages and ranges for measurements, trends over time for dates.

Common mistakes begin before any analysis. Mistake one: trusting data without checking it. Duplicates, misspelled categories, missing entries, and wrong units can all distort results. Always look at a sample of raw data and check totals against a known source. Mistake two: confusing correlation with causation — two things rising together does not prove one caused the other. Ice cream sales and sunburn rise together in summer; neither causes the other. Mistake three: cherry-picking results that support a preferred decision while ignoring the rest. Good analysts report inconvenient findings too, because a decision based on half the evidence is a gamble.

Practice exercise: Choose a decision you or a business faces — for example, whether to extend opening hours, which product to promote, or where to focus advertising. Write the decision as one question, list three pieces of data that would genuinely help answer it, and note where each piece might come from and one reason it might be unreliable. If you cannot state the question clearly, do that first; data cannot rescue an unclear question.

A further distinction worth learning early is between descriptive, diagnostic, predictive, and prescriptive analysis. Descriptive analysis says what happened; diagnostic analysis explores why it happened; predictive analysis estimates what is likely to happen next, based on past patterns; prescriptive analysis recommends what to do. Most everyday business decisions need only the first two, done well. Predictive work is valuable when you have enough good historical data and a stable pattern, but it should always carry its uncertainty openly. Do not let impressive-sounding prediction hide a weak description of what actually happened.

Lesson 2: Asking Good Questions and Planning an Analysis

A good analysis is decided before a single chart is drawn. The planning step — sometimes called framing — turns a vague worry into a question, a method, and a definition of what a useful answer looks like. Analysts who skip framing often spend days producing work that is technically correct and practically useless, because they answered the wrong question, used the wrong time period, or defined a key term differently from the decision-maker.

Start by naming the decision and the decision-maker. Who will act on this analysis, what choices do they face, and by when must they decide? A shop owner deciding next month's stock needs different detail from a manager reporting annual performance. Next, write the question in a form that can be answered with data. Vague questions — "Are customers happy?" — become analysable when you specify what happiness means here: repeat purchase rate, ratings, complaints, or survey responses? Each measure tells a slightly different story, so choose deliberately and state your choice. If stakeholders disagree about the definition, settle it now, not after results arrive.

Then plan the data and method at a high level. What data do you need, what time period, and what groups will you compare? Will you look at totals, averages, shares, or changes over time? What comparisons will make the result meaningful — this period versus last period, one store versus another, buyers versus non-buyers? Comparisons are what give numbers meaning. A single number, like "500 visitors," says little until you know whether that is high, low, rising, or falling compared with something relevant.

Worked example: a course provider asks, "Is our new email course working?" Framing turns this into: "Among subscribers who joined in the last three months, what proportion completed all four lessons, and how does that compare with subscribers who joined under the previous format in the prior three months?" The decision is whether to keep the new format. The data are sign-up dates, format, and completion records. The comparison is new versus old format over similar periods. The answer will be a completion rate for each group, plus a note on sample size — if only twelve people joined under the new format, the result is suggestive, not conclusive. That honesty is part of the analysis.

Also plan for limits. State what the data cannot show. Website visits cannot prove why someone did not buy. Sales data alone cannot show whether customers are satisfied. Naming limits in advance prevents over-claiming later and helps the decision-maker use the result appropriately. A useful analysis often ends with "the data suggest X, but we still need Y to be sure" — that is a strength, not a failure.

Common mistakes: accepting the first question as stated without probing what decision it serves; defining terms loosely, so two people read the same chart differently; and planning an analysis so large it cannot finish before the decision date. Start with the smallest analysis that could change the decision, deliver it, and expand only if needed.

Practice exercise: Take the question from Lesson 1 and write a one-page analysis plan: decision and decision-maker, exact question, definitions of key terms, data needed, time period, comparisons, and the form the answer will take. Show it to the decision-maker (or a peer playing that role) and ask: "If I deliver exactly this, will it help you decide?" Revise until the answer is yes.

It is also worth agreeing, during framing, how the result will be judged and who can challenge it. Will a single chart be enough, or does the decision need a written recommendation? Who reviews the analysis before it reaches the decision-maker, and what happens if the reviewer disagrees? Setting these expectations early prevents the common situation where analysis is produced, doubted in the meeting, and then redone under pressure. A short framing note, shared and approved before analysis begins, protects both the analyst's time and the decision's quality.

Lesson 3: Finding, Cleaning, and Preparing Data You Can Trust

Analysis is only as good as its data, and real data is almost always messy. Cleaning and preparing data — checking it, fixing errors, and shaping it for analysis — often takes more time than the analysis itself. Beginners sometimes resent this stage and rush it, but it is where trustworthy analysis is made. A careful analyst treats cleaning as real work, documents what was changed, and never silently alters data to suit a preferred result.

Start by understanding where your data came from and what each field means. A spreadsheet column headed "sales" might mean orders placed, orders paid, or orders shipped, and those are different numbers. Check for a data dictionary or ask the data owner. Look at the data itself: how many rows, what date range, which fields are often empty, and whether values look sensible. A customer's age of 250, a negative quantity, or a sale dated in the future are all signals to investigate, not values to average blindly.

Common problems have standard treatments. Duplicates: the same order recorded twice will inflate totals; identify duplicates by a unique identifier, like an order number, and keep one record, noting what you removed. Missing values: decide case by case. If a few prices are missing, you might exclude those rows from price analysis but keep them for counting orders, and say so. If many values are missing in a pattern — for example, a whole month's region field is blank — that pattern itself is information, and filling it with guesses would hide a collection problem. Inconsistent categories: "UK," "U.K.," "United Kingdom," and "GB" may all mean the same place; standardise them to one form and record the mapping. Wrong types: numbers stored as text, or dates stored as text, must be converted properly before calculations, or totals will silently fail.

Worked example: a shop's monthly sales file contains 1,000 rows, but totals do not match the accounting report. Inspection finds: 20 duplicate order numbers, 15 rows where the quantity is blank, and 30 rows where the product category is spelled three different ways. The analyst removes exact duplicates, excludes blank-quantity rows from revenue calculations while counting them separately, and standardises the categories. The corrected total now matches accounting within a small, explainable difference (a few refunds recorded differently). The analyst writes a short cleaning note listing each fix. That note is what makes the final analysis auditable — another person could repeat it and get the same result.

Also think about privacy and care. Data about people should be handled only for the purpose it was collected for, kept secure, and not shared more widely than needed. Where possible, analyse aggregated results — totals and averages for groups — rather than sharing individual records. Remove or mask details you do not need. Good data practice is both an ethical duty and a practical one: careless handling destroys trust and can break rules.

Common mistakes: editing the original file instead of working on a copy, so errors cannot be undone; fixing values by guessing rather than checking the source; and cleaning without documenting changes. Always keep the original untouched, work on a copy, and keep a short log: what you changed, why, and how many records were affected. That log turns cleaning from invisible tidying into verifiable method.

Practice exercise: Take any small dataset you can access — for example, a list of your own expenses, a class attendance list, or a sample file. Check it for duplicates, missing values, inconsistent categories, and implausible values. Write a cleaning log with each issue found, the action taken, and the number of records affected. Then compute one total before and after cleaning and note the difference. That difference is why this lesson matters.

Lesson 4: Describing Data — Summaries, Distributions, and What "Average" Hides

Before hunting for complex patterns, describe your data plainly. Descriptive analysis answers: what does this data look like, what is typical, how much does it vary, and are there unusual values? These simple summaries prevent most misreadings, because they show the shape of the data before you draw conclusions from it. Averages without context are especially dangerous: they can hide wide variation, skew, and outliers that change the decision entirely.

For numbers, the most useful summaries are the count, the mean (average), the median (middle value when sorted), the range, and the spread. The mean and median tell different stories when data is skewed. Consider monthly incomes in a small team: nine people earn modest amounts and one founder earns a great deal. The mean will look high and suggest everyone is well paid; the median will show the typical person's pay more honestly. Neither is wrong; they answer different questions. Reporting both, plus the range, gives a fuller picture. For categories, counts and percentages are the core summaries: how many in each group, and what share of the whole does each group represent?

Distributions show how values spread out. A simple way to see this is to group numbers into ranges and count how many fall in each — for example, order values grouped as under 10, 10–25, 25–50, and over 50. That table, or a chart of it, reveals whether most orders cluster at low values with a few large ones, or spread evenly. Outliers — values far from the rest — deserve investigation, not automatic deletion. A single order of 10,000 in a shop where typical orders are 30 might be a genuine bulk purchase, a data entry error (an extra zero), or a test record. Each explanation leads to a different action, so check before deciding.

Worked example: a tutoring business reports an "average session rating" of 4.6 out of 5 and celebrates. A fuller description shows: 200 ratings, median 5, but 30 ratings of 1, all from one month when a booking system failed and sessions started late. The average hid a specific, fixable problem and a group of unhappy customers. The business fixes the booking process and contacts the affected customers. The average alone would have suggested nothing needed doing. Always ask: what does the distribution look like, and who sits at the extremes?

Percentages also need care. A "50 percent increase" from 2 to 3 is a small absolute change; a "5 percent increase" from 10,000 to 10,500 is a large one. Report both the percentage and the underlying numbers, and state the base: "from 200 to 300, a 50 percent increase." Watch for small samples: a rate based on four people will swing wildly with one person's change, so report the sample size alongside the rate. A 100 percent success rate from two trials is not the same evidence as 95 percent from two thousand trials.

Common mistakes: reporting only the mean; deleting outliers without investigation; comparing percentages with different bases; and confusing the mode, median, and mean. Keep a habit: for any headline number, ask "typical for whom, how spread out, based on how many, and what about the extremes?" If you cannot answer, the description is not finished.

Practice exercise: Take the dataset from Lesson 3 and write a one-page description: for one numeric field, report count, mean, median, minimum, and maximum, and group the values into ranges with counts. For one category field, report counts and percentages. Write two sentences about what is typical, one sentence about variation or outliers, and one sentence about a limit of these summaries. That page is the foundation every later analysis should stand on.

Lesson 5: Exploring Patterns — Comparisons, Trends, and Relationships

Once you can describe data, you can explore it: compare groups, follow trends over time, and examine relationships between variables. Exploration is where insights emerge, but it is also where false patterns tempt you. The discipline is to compare fairly, look for alternative explanations, and treat early patterns as hypotheses to check, not conclusions to announce.

Comparisons need a fair basis. Comparing total sales between a large store and a small store tells you little; comparing sales per day open, or per customer, is fairer. Comparing this December with last December accounts for seasonality; comparing December with November may just show Christmas. When you compare groups, check that the groups are similar in other important ways, or note the differences. If one marketing campaign ran during a holiday and another did not, the holiday — not the campaign — may explain the difference. Good comparisons state what was held constant and what was not.

Trends over time are powerful but easy to misread. Plot values in order and look for direction, seasonality (regular ups and downs, like weekend peaks), sudden changes, and gradual drift. A sudden change often points to a real event — a price change, a stock outage, a new competitor — and is worth investigating. But do not overreact to a single point. Short-term wiggles are normal; a trend needs several points in the same direction. Also beware of choosing a starting point that flatters the story. Showing growth "since our worst month" is technically true and deeply misleading. Show a long enough period for the reader to judge fairly.

Relationships between variables are examined with care. A scatter of two numbers — for example, advertising spend and sales by week — can suggest whether they move together. If they do, that is correlation, not proof of causation. Perhaps both rise in busy seasons, or perhaps sales drive advertising (a successful product gets more promotion) rather than the other way around. To move from correlation toward causation, look for additional evidence: does the relationship hold across different periods and groups? Does a change in one precede a change in the other? Is there a plausible mechanism? Could a third factor explain both? Without those checks, the honest statement is "these move together," not "this causes that."

Worked example: a website owner finds that visitors who watch a product video buy more often than those who do not, and considers forcing everyone to watch the video. Exploration reveals an alternative explanation: visitors who already intend to buy are more likely to watch the video to check details. The video may help, but the comparison mixes intent with video-watching. A fairer test is to compare similar visitors — for example, new visitors arriving from the same source — split randomly between seeing the video prominently and not seeing it, and then compare purchase rates. That is the step from exploration to testing, covered in Lesson 6.

Common mistakes: comparing unlike with unlike; reading a trend from two points; announcing causation from correlation; and hunting through many variables until something looks significant by chance. If you test twenty relationships, one may look strong purely by chance. Pre-register your main question (Lesson 2) before exploring widely, and treat surprise patterns as leads for further checking, not as findings.

Practice exercise: Using your dataset, make three fair comparisons: two groups compared on a like-for-like basis, one trend over time with at least six points, and one relationship between two variables. For each, write one sentence stating the pattern and one sentence stating an alternative explanation. If you cannot think of an alternative explanation, you have not yet examined the pattern critically enough.

Lesson 6: Testing Ideas and Avoiding False Conclusions

Exploration suggests ideas; testing checks them. You do not need advanced statistics to test an idea well. You need a clear prediction made before you look at the test result, a fair comparison, enough data to judge, and honesty about uncertainty. The most practical test in business and everyday analytics is a simple controlled comparison: change one thing for one group, keep it unchanged for a comparable group, and compare outcomes.

The logic is straightforward. Suppose you believe a shorter sign-up form will increase completions. Split new visitors randomly into two groups: group A sees the current form, group B sees the shorter form. Random assignment matters because it balances other differences — device, time of day, motivation — between the groups on average. After a planned period, compare completion rates. If group B completes more often by a meaningful amount, and the groups were large enough, you have evidence the shorter form helps. If the difference is tiny, or the groups were tiny, the honest conclusion is "not proven," not "it works a little."

Sample size and chance deserve plain-language attention. Small samples produce noisy results: with twenty visitors per group, a difference of one or two completions could easily be luck. Larger samples stabilise the result. As a practical rule, decide in advance how long the test will run or how many people it needs, and do not stop the moment the result looks favourable — stopping early when results look good systematically exaggerates effects. If you must report an early result, label it as early and uncertain. Where formal statistics are used, they estimate how surprising a result would be if there were actually no effect; they do not prove a cause, and a "significant" label does not mean the effect is large or important. Always report the size of the difference in real terms — for example, "completion rose from 20 percent to 26 percent" — alongside any statistical statement.

Worked example: an online shop tests two email subject lines. After sending each to 1,000 subscribers, subject A is opened by 22 percent and subject B by 24 percent. The difference is small and could plausibly be chance variation at this sample size, so the shop does not declare a winner and switch permanently. Instead, it retests with a larger group and checks clicks and purchases, not just opens, because an opened email that nobody acts on is a weak win. That restraint prevents a permanent change based on noise.

Common mistakes: testing without a control group, so you cannot tell whether the change or something else caused the result; changing several things at once, so you cannot tell which mattered; peeking at results repeatedly and stopping at the best moment; and confusing a test on one audience with proof for all audiences. A result for new visitors in one month may not hold for returning customers or in a different season. State the test's scope when you report it.

Practice exercise: Design a test for one idea from your earlier work. Write: the prediction, the two groups and how they will be assigned, the one outcome you will measure, how long the test will run or how many people it needs, and what result would count as "works," "unclear," or "does not work" — decided before the test starts. That pre-commitment is the single habit that most separates testing from storytelling.

Finally, remember that not every important question can or should be answered with a formal test. Some decisions are too urgent, too small, or too dependent on judgement to justify a controlled experiment, and in those cases a careful descriptive analysis plus a small, reversible trial is a sensible approach. The principle remains the same: state what you expect, try the change in a limited way, observe honestly, and be willing to reverse it. Testing is a mindset of making predictions checkable, not a ritual that only counts when it uses formal statistics.

Lesson 7: Visualising Data and Communicating Findings Honestly

A chart or a report is not decoration for an analysis; it is the analysis made understandable. Decision-makers rarely read raw tables, and they act on what they understand. Good visualisation therefore follows a simple rule: choose the visual that answers the question most directly, label it completely, and do not distort the message for effect. A clear, honest chart beats a spectacular, misleading one every time.

Match the chart to the question. To compare amounts across categories, a bar chart works well because lengths are easy to compare. To show a trend over time, a line chart works because the eye follows direction. To show shares of a whole, use a bar or a simple share chart, and only when the parts genuinely add to a whole — a pie-style chart with many tiny slices helps nobody. To show a relationship between two numbers, a scatter plot works. Avoid three-dimensional effects, decorative icons that change apparent sizes, and chart types chosen for novelty rather than clarity. If a table answers the question better, use a table.

Honesty in charts is mostly about scales and labels. Start bar charts at zero, because cutting off the bottom exaggerates differences. Label axes with units and time periods. State the sample size and the date range on or under the chart. Do not cherry-pick a time window that flatters the story; show enough context for a fair judgement. Use colour to highlight the point, not to decorate: one highlighted bar among neutral bars draws the eye to what matters, while a rainbow of colours draws it everywhere and nowhere. Keep text readable, avoid jargon in titles, and write titles as findings where appropriate: "Repeat orders rose after the loyalty launch" is more useful than "Chart 4: Orders."

Communicating findings follows the same discipline. Lead with the answer to the original question, then the evidence, then the limits and the recommended action. A useful structure is: question, answer in one or two sentences, key evidence (with numbers and sample sizes), what could still be wrong or unknown, and what to do next. Keep the main report short; put detailed tables and methods in an appendix so a sceptical reader can check your work without cluttering the decision. Always distinguish fact ("sales were X in March"), interpretation ("this suggests demand rose"), and recommendation ("we should therefore..."). Blurring those three invites overconfidence.

Worked example: an analyst must report that a new product launch underperformed. A dishonest report would show only the best week, start the axis above zero to hide the gap, and blame the season. An honest report shows the full period, compares against the forecast and a comparable launch, notes that a stock outage affected two weeks, and recommends fixing availability before judging the product. The honest version is harder to deliver, but it leads to a better decision — and it preserves the analyst's credibility for the next report.

Common mistakes: charts without titles, units, or sources; truncated axes; too many metrics on one chart; and reports that bury the answer on page six. Another common error is presenting a finding as more certain than it is. Use language that matches the evidence: "suggests," "is consistent with," and "shows" mean different things, and choosing correctly is a professional skill.

Practice exercise: Take one finding from your earlier work and present it twice: first as a chart with a full title, labelled axes, sample size, and source note, and second as a half-page written summary using the structure above (question, answer, evidence, limits, recommendation). Show both to someone unfamiliar with the data and ask them to state the finding and the recommended action. If they cannot, revise the title and the opening sentences first.

Lesson 8: Putting It Together — A Complete Analysis and Responsible Practice

This final lesson brings the course together into a complete, repeatable analysis workflow, and into the responsibilities that come with working with data. A good analyst is not just technically skilled; they are trustworthy. They document their work, respect privacy, report limits, and refuse to bend numbers to please an audience. Those habits are what make analysis useful over time, because decision-makers learn they can rely on it even when the news is unwelcome.

The workflow, from start to finish, is: frame the question and the decision (Lesson 2); gather and clean the data, keeping the original untouched and logging changes (Lesson 3); describe the data before interpreting it (Lesson 4); explore comparisons, trends, and relationships fairly (Lesson 5); test key ideas with a fair comparison where possible (Lesson 6); and communicate the answer honestly with clear visuals (Lesson 7). At each stage, write things down: the question, definitions, cleaning log, methods, and results. Documentation is not bureaucracy; it is what lets someone else — or future you — repeat the analysis, check it, and update it when new data arrives.

Responsible practice includes several commitments. Use data only for its proper purpose and with appropriate permission. Protect personal data: work with aggregates where possible, secure files, and do not share individual records unnecessarily. Watch for bias: data can reflect past inequalities or collection gaps, and an analysis that ignores those gaps can recommend unfair actions. For example, if feedback was collected only from customers who complained, it does not represent all customers. Name the gap and, where possible, seek more representative data before acting. Finally, be transparent about uncertainty. A range, a sample size, and a stated limit are not weaknesses; they are the marks of analysis a decision-maker can actually use.

Consider a complete worked example. A small business wants to decide whether to keep a new delivery option. Framing: "Did the new option increase repeat orders among first-time buyers in the last quarter, compared with the prior quarter?" Cleaning reveals duplicate orders and a week of missing delivery data, both logged. Description shows typical order values and the share using the new option. Exploration shows repeat orders higher among new-option users, but those users also spent more initially, suggesting they may differ in other ways. A fairer comparison matches users by first-order value band and still shows a modest advantage. The report states the advantage in real terms, notes the missing week and the modest sample, and recommends keeping the option for another quarter with continued monitoring rather than declaring a permanent win. That is analytics as a decision aid, not as a verdict machine.

Common mistakes at this stage are mostly about stopping too early: delivering a chart without a recommendation, or a recommendation without evidence, or evidence without limits. The final check before sharing any analysis is a short list: does it answer the original question? Can the decision-maker act on it? Are numbers, bases, and sample sizes stated? Are limits named? Could someone else repeat it from your notes? If any answer is no, fix that before sending.

Your final practice exercise is a complete mini-analysis. Choose a real question, follow all seven stages, and produce a two-page report: question and decision, data and cleaning note, description, key comparisons or trends, a test or a clearly labelled exploratory finding, one chart, limits, and a recommendation. Keep it to two pages — brevity forces clarity. That report, honestly done, is the skill this course teaches: not fancy tools, but clear questions, clean data, fair comparisons, honest communication, and decisions that are better because evidence, not guesswork, informed them.
