Lesson 1: Hangul, Sounds, and Greetings

Korean is written in Hangul, an alphabet created in the 15th century that is systematic enough to learn in a focused session, and learning it from the start — rather than relying only on romanisation — pays off in every later lesson. Hangul has 14 basic consonants and 10 basic vowels, combined into syllable blocks. A syllable block always starts with a consonant (using the placeholder ㅇ when there is no initial sound) and contains a vowel, optionally followed by a final consonant: 가 (ga), 나 (na), 한 (han), 국 (guk), 김 (gim). Read blocks left to right, top to bottom, within each block: 한 is ㅎ + ㅏ + ㄴ, 국 is ㄱ + ㅜ + ㄱ.

Basic consonants: ㄱ (g/k, like the *g* in "go" at the start of a word, closer to *k* in some positions), ㄴ (n), ㄷ (d/t), ㄹ (r/l, a tap between vowels, closer to *l* at the end), ㅁ (m), ㅂ (b/p), ㅅ (s, becoming *sh* before ㅣ, ㅑ, ㅕ, ㅛ, ㅠ), ㅇ (silent at the start, *ng* at the end), ㅈ (j), ㅊ (ch, aspirated), ㅋ (k, aspirated), ㅌ (t, aspirated), ㅍ (p, aspirated), ㅎ (h). Doubled consonants — ㄲ, ㄸ, ㅃ, ㅆ, ㅉ — are tense, not just louder: 딸 (daughter) is not 달 (moon), and 발 (foot) is not 빨 (quick, as in 빨리, quickly). Basic vowels: ㅏ (a, as in "father"), ㅓ (eo, like the *u* in "cup" but further back), ㅗ (o, as in "more" without glide), ㅜ (u, as in "rule"), ㅡ (eu, an unrounded high back vowel with no close English equivalent — lips relaxed, tongue high and back), ㅣ (i, as in "machine"), ㅐ (ae, as in "bed"), ㅔ (e, as in "they" without glide). Compound vowels add a *y* glide (ㅑ ya, ㅕ yeo, ㅛ yo, ㅠ yu) or a *w* glide (ㅘ wa, ㅝ wo, ㅢ ui).

Pronunciation rules change sounds at syllable boundaries, and recognising them helps listening even before you produce them perfectly. A final ㄱ, ㄷ, or ㅂ before another consonant or pause becomes unreleased (*k, t, p* held, not exploded): 국 (guk) ends in a held *k*. Voicing happens between vowels: the ㄱ in 아기 (baby) sounds closer to *g* than the ㄱ in 가. Nasalisation: a final ㄱ, ㄷ, or ㅂ before ㄴ or ㅁ becomes ㅇ, ㄴ, or ㅁ: 한국말 (Korean language) is pronounced closer to "hangungmal" than a letter-by-letter reading suggests. These are listening rules first; do not try to memorise every assimilation before speaking simple sentences.

Greetings and politeness levels are grammatical, not just social. The polite informal style ending in -요 is the safest default for most learners: 안녕하세요 (annyeonghaseyo, hello), 감사합니다 (gamsahamnida, thank you — formal polite) or 고마워요 (gomawoyo, thank you — polite informal), 죄송합니다 (joesonghamnida, I am sorry — formal) or 죄송해요 (joesonghaeyo, polite informal), 안녕히 가세요 (annyeonghi gaseyo, goodbye to someone leaving) versus 안녕히 계세요 (annyeonghi gyeseyo, goodbye to someone staying). 안녕 (annyeong) alone is informal, for friends and peers only. Introductions: 저는 ___입니다 (jeoneun ___imnida, I am ___), 제 이름은 ___입니다 (je ireumeun ___imnida, My name is ___), 만나서 반갑습니다 (mannaseo bangapseumnida, Pleased to meet you), 어디에서 오셨어요? (eodieseo osyeosseoyo? Where did you come from?), 저는 서울에서 왔어요 (jeoneun seoureseo wasseoyo, I came from Seoul). Note 저는 (jeoneun) is the contracted form of 저 + 는, and 입니다 is the formal polite copula.

A common mistake is relying on romanisation with English vowel values, turning 어 into "oh" or 으 into "oo." Learn Hangul and practise ㅓ and ㅡ as distinct sounds. Another is using 안녕 with strangers or teachers; use 안녕하세요 by default. A third is confusing 가세요 and 계세요 at parting; check who is leaving and who is staying.

**Practice exercise:** Write the Hangul for these syllables from memory — 가, 나, 다, 라, 마, 바, 사, 아, 자, 차, 카, 타, 파, 하 — then read these words aloud: 한국 (Korea), 서울 (Seoul), 감사합니다, 안녕하세요, 김치 (kimchi). Write a five-line introduction in Hangul with romanisation underneath, using 안녕하세요, your name with 입니다, and 만나서 반갑습니다.

Lesson 2: 입니다, 있어요/없어요, and Describing Yourself

Korean sentences end with the verb, and the copula (the "to be" verb) changes with politeness level. The formal polite copula is 입니다 (imnida) after a consonant and 입니다 after a vowel as well in modern standard usage for nouns (the older -ㅂ니다 distinction applies to verbs, not the copula in this form): 저는 학생입니다 (jeoneun haksaengimnida, I am a student), 이것은 책입니다 (igeoseun chaegimnida, This is a book), 저는 한국 사람입니다 (jeoneun hanguk saramimnida, I am a Korean person). The polite informal copula is -이에요 after a consonant and -예요 after a vowel: 학생이에요 (haksaengieyo), 의사예요 (uisayeyo, [I] am a doctor — 의사 ends in a vowel). Negation of the copula uses 아니에요 / 아닙니다: 학생이 아니에요 (haksaengi anieyo, [I] am not a student), 의사가 아닙니다 (uisaga animnida, [He/she] is not a doctor). Questions need no word-order change: 학생이에요? (Are you a student?) with rising intonation, or with a question word: 누구예요? (Who is it?), 뭐예요? (What is it?).

Existence and possession use 있어요 (isseoyo, there is / I have) and 없어요 (eopseoyo, there is not / I do not have) in the polite informal style, and 있습니다 / 없습니다 in the formal style. The pattern is: place or possessor + 에 (for location) or the topic/subject marker + noun + 있어요. Examples: 집에 고양이가 있어요 (jibe goyangiga isseoyo, There is a cat at home / At home there is a cat), 저는 동생이 있어요 (jeoneun dongsaengi isseoyo, I have a younger sibling — literally "As for me, a younger sibling exists"), 돈이 없어요 (doni eopseoyo, I don't have money / There is no money), 시간이 없어요 (sigani eopseoyo, I don't have time). This existence construction is how Korean expresses "to have" for most things; the verb 가지다 (gajida, to possess) exists but is less common in everyday "I have" sentences than the 있어요 pattern.

Particles mark the role of a noun, and they are the grammar of Korean. The topic marker 은/는 (eun/neun: 은 after a consonant, 는 after a vowel) marks what the sentence is about: 저는 (jeoneun, as for me), 책은 (chaegeun, as for the book). The subject marker 이/가 (i/ga: 이 after a consonant, 가 after a vowel) marks the grammatical subject, often for new information or with 있어요/없어요: 고양이가 있어요 (a cat exists — new information), 비가 와요 (biga wayo, It is raining — literally "rain comes"). The object marker 을/를 (eul/reul: 을 after a consonant, 를 after a vowel) marks the direct object: 책을 읽어요 (chaegeul ilgeoyo, [I] read a book), 밥을 먹어요 (babeul meogeoyo, [I] eat rice/a meal). The location marker 에 (e) marks a place or a time: 집에 (at home), 학교에 (at school), 아침에 (in the morning). The direction marker (으)로 (euro/ro) and the source marker 에서 (eseo) appear in later lessons.

Adjectives in Korean behave like verbs (they are often called descriptive verbs) and take the same endings: 크다 (keuda, to be big), 작다 (jakda, to be small), 좋다 (jota, to be good), 나쁘다 (nappeuda, to be bad), 예쁘다 (yeppeuda, to be pretty), 덥다 (deopda, to be hot, of weather), 춥다 (chupda, to be cold). In the polite informal present: 커요 (keoyo, It is big — note the stem change: 크다 + 어요 → 커요), 작아요 (jagayo), 좋아요 (joayo), 나빠요 (nappayo), 예뻐요 (yeppeoyo), 더워요 (deowoyo), 추워요 (chuweyo). Age uses the counting system with 살 (sal, years of age): 스무 살이에요 (seumu sarieyo, [I] am twenty years old) — the native Korean number 스무 (twenty) is used here, not the Sino-Korean 이십, a distinction covered in Lesson 4. Saying 나이는… (nai) for age is also common: 저는 스물다섯 살이에요 (I am twenty-five years old).

Common mistakes include dropping particles entirely in every sentence (Korean allows omission in casual speech, but learners should practise them explicitly first), confusing 은/는 with 이/가 (topic versus subject — a distinction that becomes clearer with examples, not with a single rule), and translating "I have" as a direct verb instead of the 있어요 pattern. Another is using 입니다 in every sentence regardless of politeness level; -요 forms are the everyday default with peers and strangers in most service situations, while -ㅂ니다/습니다 is formal.

**Practice exercise:** Write ten sentences about yourself and a friend: five with -이에요/-예요 or 입니다 (including one negative with 아니에요), and five with 있어요/없어요 (including a location sentence with 에 and a possession sentence with 저는…). Then rewrite the same sentences changing the politeness level from -요 to -ㅂ니다/습니다 and check every ending.

Lesson 3: Present Tense Verbs and Word Order

Korean verb stems end in a vowel or consonant, and the polite informal present adds -아요/-어요 (or -해요 for 하다 verbs) according to vowel harmony: if the last vowel of the stem is ㅏ or ㅗ, use -아요; otherwise use -어요. Examples: 가다 (gada, to go) → 가요 (gayo), 오다 (oda, to come) → 와요 (wayo — 오 + 아요 contracts to 와요), 먹다 (meokda, to eat) → 먹어요 (meogeoyo), 읽다 (ikda, to read) → 읽어요 (ilgeoyo), 자다 (jada, to sleep) → 자요 (jayo), 보다 (boda, to see/watch) → 봐요 (bwayo — 보 + 아요 contracts), 배우다 (baeuda, to learn) → 배워요 (baewoyo), 일하다 (ilhada, to work) → 일해요 (ilhaeyo), 공부하다 (gongbuhada, to study) → 공부해요 (gongbuhaeyo), 좋아하다 (joahada, to like) → 좋아해요 (joahaeyo). The dictionary form ends in -다; remove -다 to get the stem, then add the ending, applying contractions: ㅗ + 아요 → ㅘ요, ㅜ + 어요 → ㅝ요, ㅣ + 어요 → ㅕ요 (마시다 → 마셔요, to drink), ㅡ drops before -아요/-어요 in many verbs (쓰다 → 써요, to write/use; 크다 → 커요, to be big).

Word order is subject–object–verb, with the verb always last: 저는 밥을 먹어요 (jeoneun babeul meogeoyo, I eat rice/a meal), 학생이 책을 읽어요 (haksaengi chaegeul ilgeoyo, The student reads a book), 우리는 한국어를 배워요 (urineun hangugeoreul baewoyo, We learn Korean). Time and place usually come before the object: 저는 아침에 학교에서 공부해요 (jeoneun achime hakgyoeseo gongbuhaeyo, I study at school in the morning). Adverbs of frequency and manner come before the verb: 잘 (jal, well), 빨리 (ppalli, quickly), 천천히 (cheoncheonhi, slowly), 자주 (jaju, often), 가끔 (gakkeum, sometimes), 항상 (hangsang, always). Examples: 한국어를 잘해요 (hangugeoreul jalhaeyo, [I] speak Korean well — literally "do Korean well"), 빨리 가요 (ppalli gayo, [I] go quickly), 천천히 말해요 (cheoncheonhi malhaeyo, [I] speak slowly).

Negation has two common patterns. Short negation puts 안 (an, not) before the verb: 안 먹어요 (an meogeoyo, [I] don't eat), 안 가요 (an gayo, [I] don't go). Long negation uses -지 않다: 먹지 않아요 (meokji anhayo, [I] don't eat), 가지 않아요 (gaji anhayo, [I] don't go). Both are correct; 안 is more common in speech, -지 않다 is more common in writing and formal contexts. For nouns and the copula, use 아니에요 as in Lesson 2. For "cannot" (inability), use 못 (mot): 못 가요 (mot gayo, [I] can't go), 못 먹어요 (mot meogeoyo, [I] can't eat), or -지 못하다: 먹지 못해요. The distinction between 안 (do not, by choice or habit) and 못 (cannot, by inability or circumstance) matters: 안 가요 means "I don't go / I'm not going," 못 가요 means "I can't go."

Questions need no word-order change: 밥을 먹어요? (Do you eat / Are you eating?), 어디에 가요? (Where are you going?), 무엇을 해요? (What are you doing?). Question words: 누구 (nugu, who), 무엇/뭐 (mueot/mwo, what), 어디 (eodi, where), 언제 (eonje, when), 왜 (wae, why), 어떻게 (eotteoke, how), 얼마 (eolma, how much). With -요 questions, intonation rises; in writing, the question mark does the work. The question particle -까 is used in formal questions (갑니까? — formal polite), which learners recognise before producing.

Common mistakes include keeping English word order and putting the verb in the middle (*저는 먹어요 밥을* is wrong; it must be *저는 밥을 먹어요*), forgetting vowel harmony (*먹아요* is wrong; it is *먹어요* because 먹's vowel ㅓ is not ㅏ/ㅗ), and using 안 with nouns (*안 학생* is wrong; negation of nouns uses 아니에요). Another is overusing pronouns: Korean drops the subject when context is clear, so 밥을 먹어요 alone is a complete, natural sentence meaning "I/you/he/she eats," depending on context.

**Practice exercise:** Conjugate 가다, 먹다, 읽다, 공부하다, and 좋아하다 in the polite informal present, then write a paragraph about your daily routine using at least six verbs, including one negative sentence with 안 and one with -지 않다, and one frequency adverb. Swap the subject from 저는 to 우리는 throughout and check that no verb ending changes (Korean verbs do not conjugate for person — only the particle and context change).

Lesson 4: Numbers, Counters, and Time

Korean has two number systems, and using the wrong one is the most recognisable beginner error. Sino-Korean numbers (from Chinese) are used for money, dates, phone numbers, addresses, and minutes: 일 (il, 1), 이 (i, 2), 삼 (sam, 3), 사 (sa, 4), 오 (o, 5), 육 (yuk, 6), 칠 (chil, 7), 팔 (pal, 8), 구 (gu, 9), 십 (sip, 10), 십일 (sibil, 11), 십이 (sibi, 12), 이십 (isip, 20), 삼십 (samsip, 30), 백 (baek, 100), 천 (cheon, 1,000), 만 (man, 10,000). Native Korean numbers are used for age, counting people and objects (with counters), and hours: 하나 (hana, 1), 둘 (dul, 2), 셋 (set, 3), 넷 (net, 4), 다섯 (daseot, 5), 여섯 (yeoseot, 6), 일곱 (ilgop, 7), 여덟 (yeodeol, 8), 아홉 (ahop, 9), 열 (yeol, 10), 열하나 (yeolhana, 11), 열둘 (yeoldul, 12), 스물 (seumul, 20), 서른 (seoreun, 30). Before a counter, 하나, 둘, 셋, 넷, and 스물 shorten to 한, 두, 세, 네, 스무: 한 명 (han myeong, one person), 두 개 (du gae, two things), 세 살 (se sal, three years old), 네 시 (ne si, four o'clock), 스무 살 (seumu sal, twenty years old).

Counters follow the number and specify what is counted: 명 (myeong, people, polite), 개 (gae, general things), 잔 (jan, cups/glasses), 권 (gwon, books), 장 (jang, sheets of paper/tickets), 병 (byeong, bottles), 마리 (mari, animals), 층 (cheung, floors), 번 (beon, times). Examples: 사람 두 명 (saram du myeong, two people), 책 세 권 (chaek se gwon, three books), 커피 두 잔 (keopi du jan, two cups of coffee), 물 한 병 (mul han byeong, one bottle of water). Money uses Sino-Korean numbers plus 원 (won): 오천 원 (ocheon won, 5,000 won), 만 원 (man won, 10,000 won), 이만 원 (iman won, 20,000 won). Prices are often written with commas in Arabic numerals, but spoken prices use Sino-Korean numbers.

Time combines both systems: hours use native Korean numbers + 시 (si), minutes use Sino-Korean numbers + 분 (bun). Examples: 한 시 (han si, 1 o'clock), 두 시 반 (du si ban, 2:30 — 반 means "half"), 세 시 십 분 (se si sip bun, 3:10), 열두 시 (yeoldu si, 12 o'clock). Dates: months use Sino-Korean numbers + 월 (wol), days use Sino-Korean numbers + 일 (il): 일월 일일 (irwol iril, January 1st), 삼월 십오일 (samwol siboiril, March 15th). Days of the week: 월요일 (woryoil, Monday), 화요일 (hwayoil, Tuesday), 수요일 (suyoil, Wednesday), 목요일 (mogyoil, Thursday), 금요일 (geumyoil, Friday), 토요일 (toyoil, Saturday), 일요일 (iryoil, Sunday). Time expressions: 오늘 (oneul, today), 내일 (naeil, tomorrow), 어제 (eoje, yesterday), 아침 (achim, morning), 점심 (jeomsim, midday/lunch), 저녁 (jeonyeok, evening/dinner), 지금 (jigeum, now), 나중에 (najunge, later).

Common mistakes: using Sino-Korean numbers for age (*이십 살* is wrong; it is *스무 살*), using native numbers for money (*다섯 원* for 5 won is wrong in standard usage; money uses Sino-Korean: *오 원*), forgetting the shortened forms before counters (*하나 개* is wrong; it is *한 개*), and saying *두 시 십 분* with the wrong system for minutes (minutes are Sino-Korean: *십 분* is correct here because 십 is Sino-Korean, but *열 분* would be wrong). Another is confusing 시 (o'clock) with 시간 (sigan, hours of duration): 두 시간 (du sigan, two hours long) versus 두 시 (du si, 2 o'clock).

**Practice exercise:** Write out, in Hangul, your age (native number + 살), today's date (month + 월, day + 일), three times (hours native, minutes Sino-Korean), and five counted items with correct counters (people, books, cups, bottles, sheets). Then write three prices in won using Sino-Korean numbers and read them aloud.

Lesson 5: Past and Future Tenses

The Korean past tense in the polite informal style adds -았어요/-었어요 (or -했어요 for 하다 verbs) to the stem, following the same vowel harmony as the present: if the last vowel is ㅏ or ㅗ, use -았어요; otherwise use -었어요. Examples: 가다 → 갔어요 (gasseoyo, went), 오다 → 왔어요 (wasseoyo, came), 먹다 → 먹었어요 (meogeosseoyo, ate), 읽다 → 읽었어요 (ilgeosseoyo, read), 자다 → 잤어요 (jasseoyo, slept), 보다 → 봤어요 (bwasseoyo, saw/watched), 하다 → 했어요 (haesseoyo, did), 공부하다 → 공부했어요 (gongbuhaesseoyo, studied), 만나다 → 만났어요 (mannasseoyo, met). Contractions apply: 오 + 았어요 → 왔어요, 보 + 았어요 → 봤어요, 마시 + 었어요 → 마셨어요 (drank), 쓰 + 었어요 → 썼어요 (wrote/used). The formal past uses -았습니다/-었습니다/-했습니다: 갔습니다, 먹었습니다, 공부했습니다.

Time expressions anchor the past: 어제 (yesterday), 그저께 (the day before yesterday), 지난주 (last week), 지난달 (last month), 작년 (last year), 아까 (a little while ago), 전에 (before). Examples: 어제 영화를 봤어요 (eoje yeonghwareul bwasseoyo, Yesterday I watched a film), 지난주에 서울에 갔어요 (jinanjue seoure gatsseoyo, Last week I went to Seoul), 아침에 커피를 마셨어요 (achime keopireul masyeosseoyo, In the morning I drank coffee). Negation in the past uses 안 or -지 않았다: 안 먹었어요 (didn't eat), 먹지 않았어요 (didn't eat). The past tense covers both English "went" and "have gone" in most conversation; Korean does not mark that distinction the same way.

The future has two main patterns. The most common spoken future uses -(으)ㄹ 거예요 (will / am going to): after a vowel, -ㄹ 거예요; after a consonant, -을 거예요. Examples: 갈 거예요 (gal geoyeyo, [I] will go), 먹을 거예요 (meogeul geoyeyo, [I] will eat), 공부할 거예요 (gongbuhal geoyeyo, [I] will study), 할 거예요 (hal geoyeyo, [I] will do). The more formal or planned future uses -(으)ㄹ 예정이에요 (plan to) or -겠어요 (formal intention/promise, and also supposition): 가겠어요 (gagesseoyo, I will go — formal promise), 비가 오겠어요 (biga ogesseoyo, It will probably rain — supposition). Beginners should master -(으)ㄹ 거예요 first for plans, and recognise -겠어요 for promises and weather forecasts. Present tense with a future time expression is also natural: 내일 서울에 가요 (naeil seoure gayo, Tomorrow I go to Seoul / I'm going to Seoul tomorrow).

The progressive (action in progress) uses -고 있다: 먹고 있어요 (meokgo isseoyo, [I] am eating), 공부하고 있어요 (gongbuhago isseoyo, [I] am studying), 자고 있어요 (jago isseoyo, [He/she] is sleeping). This is distinct from the simple present, which can mean habit or near future: 밥을 먹어요 can mean "I eat rice" (habit) or "I'll eat" (with a future time word), while 밥을 먹고 있어요 specifically means "I am eating right now." The distinction matters for listening: a Korean speaker saying 지금 공부하고 있어요 means "I am studying right now," not "I study in general."

Common mistakes: using the wrong vowel harmony in the past (*먹았어요* is wrong; it is *먹었어요* because 먹's vowel is ㅓ), forgetting contractions (*오았어요* is wrong; it is *왔어요*), and confusing -겠어요 (promise/supposition) with -(으)ㄹ 거예요 (plain future plan). Another is placing the time expression after the verb; Korean time expressions come before the verb, usually early in the sentence: 어제 영화를 봤어요, not a verb-first order.

**Practice exercise:** Write a diary entry for yesterday using ten past-tense verbs (including two 하다 verbs and one negative past sentence), then write tomorrow's plans using five -(으)ㄹ 거예요 futures and two present-tense near-future sentences. Add one progressive sentence about what you are doing right now, and check every vowel-harmony choice.

Lesson 6: Food, Ordering, and Quantities

Korean food vocabulary is immediately usable, and restaurant language rewards a few polite set phrases. Key words: 밥 (bap, rice/a meal), 김치 (gimchi, kimchi), 고기 (gogi, meat), 생선 (saengseon, fish), 채소 (chaeso, vegetables), 과일 (gwail, fruit), 빵 (ppang, bread), 국 (guk, soup), 찌개 (jjigae, stew), 면 (myeon, noodles), 물 (mul, water), 커피 (keopi, coffee), 차 (cha, tea), 맥주 (maekju, beer), 소주 (soju, soju), 디저트 (dijeoteu, dessert), 계산서 (gyesanseo, the bill). Meals: 아침 (achim, breakfast/morning), 점심 (jeomsim, lunch/midday), 저녁 (jeonyeok, dinner/evening). Ordering a meal is often 밥을 먹다 (to eat a meal) regardless of the food: 점심을 먹었어요 (jeomsimeul meogeosseoyo, I ate lunch).

Useful phrases: ordering politely uses 주세요 (juseyo, please give me) after the item: 커피 한 잔 주세요 (keopi han jan juseyo, Please give me one cup of coffee), 물 주세요 (mul juseyo, Water, please), 이거 주세요 (igeo juseyo, Please give me this one). *주세요* is the polite request form and is the single most useful restaurant word. Other phrases: 메뉴판 주세요 (menyupan juseyo, The menu, please), 추천해 주세요 (chucheonhae juseyo, Please recommend something), 매운 거 잘 못 먹어요 (maeun geo jal mot meogeoyo, I can't eat spicy food well), 고기를 안 먹어요 (gogireul an meogeoyo, I don't eat meat), 알레르기가 있어요 (allereugiga isseoyo, I have an allergy), 견과류 알레르기가 있어요 (gyeon gwaryu allereugiga isseoyo, I have a nut allergy). To pay: 계산서 주세요 (gyesanseo juseyo, The bill, please), 카드로 계산할게요 (kadeuro gyesanhalgeyo, I'll pay by card), 따로따로 계산해 주세요 (ttarottaro gyesanhae juseyo, Please split the bill) or 같이 계산해 주세요 (gachi gyesanhae juseyo, Please put it together). Note 계산할게요 uses -ㄹ게요, a promise/intention form ("I'll…") that is natural when announcing what you will do.

Quantities use counters from Lesson 4: 한 잔 (one cup), 두 병 (two bottles), 한 그릇 (han geureut, one bowl), 한 접시 (han jeopsi, one plate), 조금 (jogeum, a little), 많이 (manhi, a lot). Examples: 밥 한 그릇 주세요 (bap han geureut juseyo, One bowl of rice, please), 물 두 병 주세요 (mul du byeong juseyo, Two bottles of water, please), 조금만 주세요 (jogeumman juseyo, Just a little, please). In shops and markets: 이거 얼마예요? (igeo eolmayeyo? How much is this?), 너무 비싸요 (neomu bissayo, It's too expensive), 깎아 주세요 (kkakka juseyo, Please give me a discount — used in markets, not in fixed-price shops), 이걸로 할게요 (igeollo halgeyo, I'll take this one).

Taste and preference: 맛있다 (masitda, to be tasty) → 맛있어요 (masisseoyo), 맛없다 (madeopda, to be tasteless/bad) → 맛없어요, 달다 (dalda, to be sweet) → 달아요, 짜다 (jjada, to be salty) → 짜요, 맵다 (maepda, to be spicy) → 매워요, 쓰다 (sseuda, to be bitter) → 써요, 시다 (sida, to be sour) → 셔요. Liking uses 좋아하다 (to like) or the adjective 좋다 with a topic: 저는 김치를 좋아해요 (jeoneun gimchireul joahaeyo, I like kimchi), 저는 매운 음식을 좋아해요 (I like spicy food), 김치가 좋아요 (gimchiga joayo, Kimchi is good / I like kimchi — the topic construction). *잘 먹겠습니다* (jal meokgesseumnida, I will eat well) is said before a meal, and *잘 먹었습니다* (jal meogeosseumnida, I ate well) after — polite, standard phrases, not literal translations of "enjoy your meal."

Common mistakes: saying 주세요 without a counter when ordering a drink (커피 주세요 is understood, but 커피 한 잔 주세요 is more natural and precise), using 먹고 싶어요 (want to eat) to a waiter when 주세요 is expected, and translating "I like" only as 좋아하다 while missing the adjective pattern 김치가 좋아요. Another is asking for 우유 (uyu, milk) expecting a milky coffee; order 카페라테 (kaperate, café latte) or 커피 with 우유 added if that is what you want.

**Practice exercise:** Write a full restaurant dialogue for two people: greeting, table request, drinks, mains, a dietary restriction, dessert, and paying. Include 주세요 three times with different counters, a 좋아하다 or 좋다 sentence, a quantity phrase, and a polite recommendation question. Read it aloud, checking every particle and counter.

Lesson 7: Future Plans, Modal Expressions, and the Weather

Korean expresses ability, permission, obligation, and desire with set patterns built on the verb stem, and learning them as patterns — rather than as separate verbs — is the fastest route to useful speech. Ability and possibility use -(으)ㄹ 수 있다 (can / is possible): 갈 수 있어요 (gal su isseoyo, [I] can go), 한국어를 말할 수 있어요 (hangugeoreul malhal su isseoyo, [I] can speak Korean), 먹을 수 없어요 (meogeul su eopseoyo, [I] can't eat [it]). Permission uses -아도/어도 되다 (may / it's okay to): 가도 돼요 (gado dwaeyo, You may go / It's okay to go), 사진 찍어도 돼요? (sajin jjigeodo dwaeyo? May I take a photo?), 여기에 앉아도 돼요? (yeogie anjado dwaeyo? May I sit here?). Obligation uses -아야/어야 하다 (must / have to): 가야 해요 (gaya haeyo, [I] must go / have to go), 공부해야 해요 (gongbuhaeya haeyo, [I] must study), 일찍 일어나야 해요 (iljjik ireonaya haeyo, [I] must get up early). Prohibition uses -(으)면 안 되다 (must not): 가면 안 돼요 (gamyeon an dwaeyo, You must not go), 여기서 사진을 찍으면 안 돼요 (yeogiseo sajineul jjigeumyeon an dwaeyo, You must not take photos here). Desire uses -고 싶다 (want to): 가고 싶어요 (gago sipeoyo, [I] want to go), 먹고 싶어요 (meokgo sipeoyo, [I] want to eat), 한국에 가고 싶어요 (hanguge gago sipeoyo, [I] want to go to Korea). Suggestion uses -(으)ㄹ까요? (shall we?): 갈까요? (galkkayo? Shall we go?), 영화를 볼까요? (yeonghwareul bolkkayo? Shall we watch a film?), or -자 (informal proposal, for friends): 가자 (gaja, Let's go).

Plans and intentions also use -(으)ㄹ 예정이에요 (plan to): 내일 서울에 갈 예정이에요 (naeil seoure gal yejeongieyo, I plan to go to Seoul tomorrow), and 생각이에요 patterns for thoughts: 갈 생각이에요 (gal saenggagieyo, I'm thinking of going). The promise form -ㄹ게요 (I'll…) announces your own action: 갈게요 (galgeyo, I'll go), 전화할게요 (jeonhwahalgeyo, I'll call you), 내일 말할게요 (naeil malhalgeyo, I'll tell you tomorrow). This form is used only for your own actions, not for others' — a common error is using -ㄹ게요 for someone else's action.

Weather vocabulary: 날씨가 어때요? (nalssiga eottaeyo? How is the weather?), 날씨가 좋아요 (The weather is good), 날씨가 나빠요 (The weather is bad), 더워요 (It's hot), 추워요 (It's cold), 따뜻해요 (ttatteuthaeyo, It's warm), 시원해요 (siwonhaeyo, It's cool/refreshing), 맑아요 (malgayo, It's clear/sunny), 흐려요 (heuryeoyo, It's cloudy), 비가 와요 (biga wayo, It's raining — literally "rain comes"), 눈이 와요 (nuni wayo, It's snowing — literally "snow comes"), 바람이 불어요 (barami bureoyo, It's windy — literally "wind blows"), 안개가 껴요 (angaega kkyeoyo, It's foggy). Seasons: 봄 (bom, spring), 여름 (yeoreum, summer), 가을 (gaeul, autumn), 겨울 (gyeoul, winter). Temperatures: 이십 도예요 (isip doyeyo, It's twenty degrees — Sino-Korean numbers with 도, degrees), 영하 십 도예요 (yeongha sip doyeyo, It's minus ten degrees).

Common mistakes: confusing -아도 돼요 (permission, "it's okay to") with -아야 해요 (obligation, "must"), using -고 싶어요 for someone else's desire without changing the construction (third-person desire is usually reported differently: 가고 싶어 해요), and using -ㄹ게요 for another person's action. Another is translating "It is raining" word-for-word with an adjective; Korean says 비가 와요 (rain comes), a verb construction to learn as a unit.

**Practice exercise:** Write your plans for next week, day by day, using five -(으)ㄹ 거예요 futures, three modal sentences (one permission question with -아도 돼요, one obligation with -아야 해요, one desire with -고 싶어요), and a three-day weather forecast using 비가 와요, 눈이 와요, and 맑아요 correctly. Then rewrite one plan as a suggestion with -(으)ㄹ까요? and explain how the tone changes.

Lesson 8: Travel, Directions, and Everyday Conversation

Travel Korean brings together particles, counters, politeness levels, and past and future tenses. Transport words: 기차 (gicha, train), 버스 (beoseu, bus), 지하철 (jihacheol, underground), 택시 (taeksi, taxi), 비행기 (bihaenggi, plane), 기차역 (gichayeok, train station), 공항 (gonghang, airport), 승강장 (seunggangjang, platform), 표 (pyo, ticket), 왕복 표 (wangbok pyo, return ticket), 정류장 (jeongnyujang, stop). Key phrases: 서울행 표 한 장 주세요 (seoulhaeng pyo han jang juseyo, One ticket to Seoul, please — note 장 for tickets), 기차가 몇 시에 출발해요? (gichaga myeot sie chulbalhaeyo? What time does the train depart?), 몇 번 승강장이에요? (myeot beon seunggangjangieyo? Which platform is it?), 직행이에요? (jikhaengieyo? Is it direct?), 어디에서 갈아타야 해요? (eodieseo garataya haeyo? Where do I have to transfer?), 얼마나 걸려요? (eolmana geollyeoyo? How long does it take?), 실례합니다, 기차역이 어디예요? (sillyehamnida, gichayeogi eodiyeyo? Excuse me, where is the train station?).

Directions use location words and imperative/request forms: 오른쪽 (oreunjjok, to the right), 왼쪽 (oenjjok, to the left), 직진 (jikjin, straight ahead), 가까워요 (gakkawoyo, It's near), 멀어요 (meoreoyo, It's far), 맞은편 (majeunpyeon, opposite), 옆 (yeop, next to), 사이 (sai, between), 모퉁이 (motungi, corner). Polite directions: 직진하세요 (jikjinhaseyo, Go straight), 오른쪽으로 가세요 (oreunjjogeuro gaseyo, Go to the right), 두 번째 길에서 왼쪽으로 가세요 (du beonjjae gireseo oenjjogeuro gaseyo, At the second street, go to the left). The request form -아/어 주세요 softens instructions: 천천히 말해 주세요 (cheoncheonhi malhae juseyo, Please speak slowly), 도와주세요 (dowajuseyo, Please help me). Location with 에서 versus 에: 학교에서 공부해요 (I study at school — action takes place there, 에서) versus 학교에 있어요 (I am at school — existence/location, 에). This 에서/에 distinction for actions versus existence is a key listening point.

Everyday conversation fills in with small, high-frequency expressions: 실례합니다 (excuse me, formal), 죄송합니다 / 죄송해요 (I'm sorry), 감사합니다 / 고마워요 (thank you), 천만에요 (cheonmaneyo, you're welcome — formal, less common in speech than 아니에요 or 괜찮아요 in casual thanks), 괜찮아요 (gwaenchanayo, It's okay / You're welcome / I'm fine — extremely versatile), 네 (ne, yes), 아니요 (aniyo, no), 알겠어요 (algesseoyo, I understand / Got it), 정말요? (jeongmallyo? Really?), 그렇군요 (geureokgunyo, I see / Is that so?), 글쎄요 (geulsseyo, Well… / I'm not sure…), 그래서 (geuraeseo, So… / Therefore…), 그런데 (geunde, But… / By the way…). Shopping phrases: 그냥 구경하고 있어요 (geunyang gugyeonghago isseoyo, I'm just looking around), 입어 봐도 돼요? (ibeo bwado dwaeyo? May I try it on?), 더 큰 사이즈 있어요? (deo keun saijeu isseoyo? Do you have a bigger size?), 더 작은 사이즈 있어요? (smaller size?), 이거 얼마예요? (How much is this?), 너무 비싸요 (It's too expensive), 이걸로 할게요 (I'll take this one).

Numbers beyond twenty in Sino-Korean: 이십일 (21), 이십이 (22), 삼십 (30), 사십 (40), 오십 (50), 육십 (60), 칠십 (70), 팔십 (80), 구십 (90), 백 (100), 천 (1,000), 만 (10,000). Prices: 만 원이에요 (man wonieyo, It's 10,000 won), 오천 원이에요 (5,000 won). Note the large-unit structure: 25,000 won is 이만 오천 원 (iman ocheon won, literally "two-ten-thousand five-thousand won"), not a direct translation of "twenty-five thousand."

Common mistakes: asking 어디예요? without 실례합니다 first, which can sound abrupt; using informal imperatives (가, 봐) with strangers instead of -세요 forms; and confusing 오른쪽 with 직진. Another is translating "I'm looking for" as 찾고 있어요 with the wrong particle; the natural pattern is …을/를 찾고 있어요 (…eul/reul chajgo isseoyo, I'm looking for…) with the object marker: 호텔을 찾고 있어요 (I'm looking for a hotel).

**Practice exercise:** Write a travel day in Korean: arriving at a station, buying a ticket, asking directions to a hotel, checking in, and asking about dinner nearby. Use at least five direction words, three polite requests with 주세요 or -아/어 주세요, one past-tense sentence about what you did, and one future sentence about tomorrow. End with a short shop dialogue using 이거 얼마예요? and a size question.
