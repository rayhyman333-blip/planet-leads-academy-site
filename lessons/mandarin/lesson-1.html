This lesson teaches you to pronounce Mandarin clearly from the start: the pinyin romanization system and the four tones that change word meaning. Tones are the single most important skill in Mandarin — the same syllable "ma" means four different things depending on its tone. Your goal in this lesson is not perfect tones on day one; it is understanding the system and training your ear and mouth with deliberate drills.

Pinyin (拼音, pīn yīn) writes Mandarin sounds with the Roman alphabet. Every syllable has an initial (the starting consonant), a final (the vowel part), and a tone. The initials: b, p, m, f; d, t, n, l; g, k, h; j, q, x; zh, ch, sh, r; z, c, s. Most match English expectations, but learn these carefully: q sounds like "ch" in "cheese" (qǐ, 起), x sounds like "sh" in "she" (xī, 西), zh is a buzzed "j" with the tongue curled back (zhōng, 中), c sounds like "ts" in "cats" (cài, 菜), and r is a soft buzzed sound between English r and the "s" in "measure" (rén, 人). The finals include a, o, e, i, u, ü and combinations like ai, ei, ao, ou, an, en, ang, eng. Three spelling rules: syllables starting with i or u get a y or w written in front (yi, wu, yu); ü is written as u after j, q, x (ju, qu, xu — still pronounced with rounded lips); and e after y sounds like "eh" (yě, 也).

Now the tones — the heart of Mandarin. First tone (mā, 妈): high and flat, like singing a sustained note. Second tone (má, 麻): rising, like asking "what?" in English. Third tone (mǎ, 马): dipping — start mid, drop low, then rise slightly. Fourth tone (mà, 骂): falling sharply, like a firm command "Stop!" Neutral tone (ma, 吗): short, light, no tone mark — used on grammar particles. The classic demonstration: mā (妈, mother), má (麻, hemp), mǎ (马, horse), mà (骂, scold). Same syllable, four meanings — this is why tones are not decoration. Practice the set slowly, exaggerating each tone: your mouth is learning new muscle movements, and exaggeration now becomes accuracy later.

Two tone-change rules make your speech sound natural. Rule 1: when two third tones meet, the first becomes a second tone. 你好 (nǐ hǎo, hello) is written with two third tones but pronounced ní hǎo — say it that way from day one. Rule 2: 不 (bù, not) becomes bú before a fourth tone — 不是 (bú shì, is not). 一 (yī, one) becomes yí before a fourth tone (一个, yí gè, one) and yì before first, second, or third tones (一般, yì bān, ordinary). These changes are automatic for fluent speakers; learn them as rules now and they become habit.

Common beginner mistakes to fix early. First, speaking Mandarin with English sentence melody — flat or randomly musical. Every syllable needs its tone, every time. Second, mumbling the neutral tone as a full tone: 谢谢 (xiè xie, thank you) has a light, quick second syllable — don't give it a full fourth tone. Third, ignoring the difference between z/c/s and zh/ch/sh — zi (字, character) and zhi (知, know) are different words, and mixing them confuses listeners.

Practice: Say the mā/má/mǎ/mà set ten times, exaggerating each tone. Then drill these tone pairs out loud: 妈妈 (mā ma, mom), 你好 (ní hǎo, hello), 谢谢 (xiè xie, thank you), 中国 (Zhōng guó, China), 北京 (Běi jīng, Beijing), 上海 (Shàng hǎi, Shanghai). Next, read these pinyin words and guess the tone from the mark before checking: xué, shū, shuǐ, fàn, chē. Finally, write the four tones over "ma" from memory and say each with its meaning (mother, hemp, horse, scold) — this one set teaches your brain that tones carry meaning.
