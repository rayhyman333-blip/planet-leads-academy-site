This lesson teaches you rhythm: how long each note lasts and how beats are organized into measures. By the end you will read note values from whole notes to sixteenths, understand rests, dots, and ties, count in the common time signatures, and clap a rhythm accurately. Pitch tells you which notes to play; rhythm tells you when. A melody with perfect notes and wrong rhythm is unrecognizable — rhythm deserves the same respect as pitch.

Start with the basic note values, each lasting half as long as the previous one. A whole note (an open oval with no stem) lasts four beats. A half note (open oval with a stem) lasts two beats. A quarter note (filled oval with a stem) lasts one beat — it is the basic pulse of most music. An eighth note (filled oval, stem, and one flag or beam) lasts half a beat. A sixteenth note (filled oval, stem, two flags or beams) lasts a quarter of a beat. So one whole note equals two half notes equals four quarter notes equals eight eighth notes equals sixteen sixteenth notes. Every note value has a matching rest — a silence of the same length: whole rest, half rest, quarter rest, eighth rest, sixteenth rest. Rests are not empty time; they are composed silence, and counting through them is what keeps you in time.

Beats are grouped into measures (also called bars) by bar lines, and the time signature at the start tells you the grouping. It looks like a fraction but is read as two separate numbers: the top number says how many beats per measure, the bottom says which note value gets one beat. 4/4 (four beats per measure, quarter note gets the beat) is the most common in popular music — "four-four time". 3/4 is three beats per measure (the waltz feel: ONE-two-three, ONE-two-three). 2/4 is two beats per measure (marches). 6/8 is six eighth-note beats per measure, usually felt as two groups of three (ONE-two-three-FOUR-five-six) — the rolling feel of many ballads and jigs. A 4/4 measure must contain exactly four beats of notes and rests: four quarter notes, or two half notes, or one whole note, or eight eighth notes, or any combination that adds to four — checking this is how you verify your counting.

Two modifiers change note lengths. A dot after a note adds half its value: a dotted half note lasts three beats (2 + 1), a dotted quarter lasts one and a half beats. A tie is a curved line connecting two notes of the same pitch — you play the first and hold through the second without re-attacking, which lets notes sustain across bar lines. Do not confuse a tie with a slur: a slur is the same curved shape connecting notes of different pitches, meaning "play smoothly", while a tie only ever connects identical pitches.

To count rhythms aloud, give every beat a syllable. In 4/4 at a comfortable speed, count quarter notes as "1 2 3 4", eighth notes as "1-and-2-and-3-and-4-and", and sixteenth notes as "1-e-and-a-2-e-and-a". Clap on each syllable while counting — if you can clap it and count it, you can play it. Tempo is the speed of the beat, measured in beats per minute (BPM): 60 BPM is one beat per second, 120 BPM is twice as fast. A metronome — a device or app that clicks steady beats — is the standard practice tool; set it slow, get the rhythm right, then speed up.

Two mistakes dominate this lesson. First, rushing the rests: beginners play the notes and skip the silences, which destroys the rhythm. Count rests out loud exactly like notes. Second, misreading beamed eighth notes: two eighth notes joined by a beam still total one beat, not two. Always add up each measure's beats — if a 4/4 measure does not total four, something is misread.

Practice: Clap and count these 4/4 measures aloud: (1) quarter, quarter, half; (2) eight eighth notes; (3) dotted quarter, eighth, quarter, quarter; (4) quarter, quarter rest, quarter, quarter. Then write a two-measure rhythm of your own in 3/4 using at least one rest and one dotted note, verify each measure totals three beats, and clap it with a metronome at 80 BPM.
