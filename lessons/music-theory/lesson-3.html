This lesson teaches you scales — the ordered note collections that give music its home key — and key signatures, the shorthand that shows which notes are sharp or flat throughout a piece. By the end you will spell any major scale, spell natural minor scales, read key signatures, and understand relative major and minor keys. Scales are the single most useful theory tool: they tell you which notes belong, which chords to expect, and which notes will sound right in a melody or solo.

The major scale follows one pattern of whole steps (W) and half steps (H): W-W-H-W-W-W-H. Apply it starting on C, using only white keys: C-D (W), D-E (W), E-F (H), F-G (W), G-A (W), A-B (W), B-C (H). So C major is spelled C D E F G A B C — no sharps, no flats. Now start on G and keep the same pattern: G-A (W), A-B (W), B-C (H), C-D (W), D-E (W), E-F# (W — F must be raised to keep the whole step), F#-G (H). G major is G A B C D E F# G. Start on F: F-G (W), G-A (W), A-Bb (H — B must be lowered, because A to B-natural would be a whole step), Bb-C (W), C-D (W), D-E (W), E-F (H). F major is F G A Bb C D E F. The pattern never changes; the sharps and flats are whatever the pattern demands. This is why scales are spelled with one of each letter name — G major uses F#, never Gb, because the scale needs an F of some kind in the seventh position.

Key signatures collect a piece's sharps or flats at the start of each staff so they are not written before every note. Sharps always appear in this order: F#, C#, G#, D#, A#, E#, B#. Flats always appear in this order: Bb, Eb, Ab, Db, Gb, Cb, Fb. (Notice the flat order is the sharp order backwards — a handy memory aid.) One sharp means F# everywhere, which is the key of G major. Two sharps (F#, C#) is D major. One flat (Bb) is F major. Two flats (Bb, Eb) is Bb major. To name a major key from sharps, go one half step up from the last sharp: two sharps, last is C#, half step up is D — D major. For flats, the second-to-last flat names the key: three flats (Bb, Eb, Ab) — the second-to-last is Eb, so Eb major. (With one flat, just memorize: F major.)

The natural minor scale has its own pattern: W-H-W-W-H-W-W. Start on A using white keys: A-B (W), B-C (H), C-D (W), D-E (W), E-F (H), F-G (W), G-A (W). A minor is A B C D E F G A — no sharps or flats, the same notes as C major. Keys that share a key signature like this are called relative major and minor: A minor is the relative minor of C major, and C major is the relative major of A minor. To find a relative minor, go down three half steps (a minor third) from the major tonic: C down to A. Every major key has a relative minor, and the minor tonic is the sixth note of the major scale. E minor, the relative minor of G major, inherits its one sharp: E F# G A B C D E. Minor keys sound darker or sadder than their major relatives — same notes, different home base, completely different mood.

The chromatic scale uses all twelve notes: C C# D D# E F F# G G# A A# B (sharps going up, flats coming down: B Bb A Ab G Gb F E Eb D Db C). You will rarely write melodies in it, but it is the complete palette everything else is drawn from.

Two mistakes to watch for. First, spelling scales with the wrong enharmonic: G major's seventh note is F#, never Gb — each scale position needs its own letter. Second, forgetting that a key signature applies to every octave: one sharp in the key signature means every F in the piece, high or low, is F#.

Practice: Spell these major scales using the W-W-H-W-W-W-H pattern and name their key signatures: D major, Bb major, A major, Eb major. (Check: D major — D E F# G A B C# D, two sharps; Bb major — Bb C D Eb F G A Bb, two flats; A major — A B C# D E F# G# A, three sharps; Eb major — Eb F G Ab Bb C D Eb, three flats.) Then spell the natural minor scales for F# minor, C minor, and D minor, and name each one's relative major.
