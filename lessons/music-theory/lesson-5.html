This lesson teaches you chords: three or more notes sounding together, built by stacking intervals. By the end you will build the four triad types in any key, understand inversions, add seventh chords, and label chords with Roman numerals. Chords are harmony — the vertical dimension of music — and they are what makes a melody feel supported, rich, or surprising.

A triad is a three-note chord built by stacking two thirds. The four types: a major triad is a root plus a major third plus a perfect fifth — C–E–G. It sounds bright, stable, happy. A minor triad is a root plus a minor third plus a perfect fifth — C–Eb–G (or A–C–E). It sounds darker, sadder, more serious. A diminished triad is a root plus a minor third plus a diminished fifth (tritone) — C–Eb–Gb, or the very common B–D–F. It sounds tense and unstable, begging to resolve. An augmented triad is a root plus a major third plus an augmented fifth — C–E–G#. It sounds dreamy and floating, used sparingly for color. The recipe never changes: pick a root, add the third of the right quality, add the fifth of the right quality.

Every major scale contains a fixed pattern of triads, and this is one of the most useful facts in music. In C major, building a triad on each scale degree using only scale notes gives: C major (I), D minor (ii), E minor (iii), F major (IV), G major (V), A minor (vi), B diminished (vii°). The pattern — major, minor, minor, major, major, minor, diminished — is the same in every major key. In G major: G, Am, Bm, C, D, Em, F#°. Roman numerals label scale-degree chords: uppercase for major (I, IV, V), lowercase for minor (ii, iii, vi), and vii° with a small circle for diminished. This numbering is the shared language of harmony — a "one-four-five progression" means the same thing in every key.

Chords can be rearranged through inversion. Root position has the root on the bottom (C–E–G). First inversion puts the third on the bottom (E–G–C). Second inversion puts the fifth on the bottom (G–C–E). Same notes, same chord name, different bass note and different color — inversions let bass lines move smoothly stepwise instead of leaping, which is why hymn and pop piano parts sound so flowing. In chord symbols, a slash shows the bass note: C/E means a C major chord with E in the bass (first inversion).

Add a fourth note — a seventh above the root — and you get seventh chords. The most important: the major seventh chord (root, major third, perfect fifth, major seventh) — Cmaj7 is C–E–G–B, warm and jazzy. The dominant seventh chord (root, major third, perfect fifth, minor seventh) — G7 is G–B–D–F — tense and bluesy, strongly pulling back to the tonic C. The minor seventh (A–C–E–G = Am7), soft and mellow. Seventh chords are the harmonic vocabulary of jazz, blues, soul, and much pop — when a chord symbol says "7", it means the dominant seventh unless marked otherwise (maj7, m7).

Two mistakes to avoid. First, building triads with the wrong third: C–E–G is major only because C to E is a major third (4 half steps). C–Eb–G is minor. One half step changes the chord's entire emotional character, so check your thirds. Second, confusing chord symbols: in popular notation, "C" alone means C major triad, "Cm" means C minor, "C7" means C dominant seventh (C–E–G–Bb), and "Cmaj7" means C major seventh (C–E–G–B). C7 and Cmaj7 are different chords — the "maj" matters.

Practice: Build triads on every degree of the F major scale (F G A Bb C D E F) using only scale notes, and label each with its Roman numeral and quality. (Check: F, Gm, Am, Bb, C, Dm, E°.) Then spell these chords from their symbols: Am, G7, Fmaj7, B°, Dm7. Finally, play I–IV–V–I in C major (C–F–G–C) on your keyboard, then in G major (G–C–D–G), listening to how the same Roman numerals produce the same journey in a new key.
