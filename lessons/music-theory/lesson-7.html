This lesson teaches you to read a melody note by note from the staff and to train your ear to recognize what you read. By the end you will decode a simple melody using a step-by-step method, sing it accurately, and run a daily ear-training routine of call-and-response exercises. Reading and hearing are two halves of one skill: the eye finds the notes, the ear confirms them, and together they make music literate in the true sense.

Here is the reading method. Work through this eight-note melody in C major, treble clef, 4/4 — one quarter note per beat, two measures:

Measure 1: C D E F | Measure 2: G F E D (then a half-note C to finish: C held two beats)

Step 1: check the clef and key signature. Treble clef, no sharps or flats — C major (or A minor; the melody will tell you). Step 2: find your landmarks. Middle C sits one ledger line below the staff; the G on the second line is wrapped by the treble clef's curl. Step 3: read the first note absolutely (C — ledger line below), then read the rest relatively: D is one step up from C, E one more step, F one more. Stepwise motion — moving to the neighboring note — is the easiest reading there is, and most simple melodies move mostly by step. Step 4: check the rhythm. All quarter notes except the final half note: count "1 2 3 4 | 1 2 3 4" with the last C held for beats 3 and 4. Step 5: sing it before you play it. Singing forces your ear to commit — if you can sing it, you have truly read it, not just decoded symbols. The full melody: C D E F | G F E (quarter notes) C (half note). It is the opening shape of countless folk tunes: up four steps, down to the tonic, rest.

Now ear training, the daily practice that makes theory audible. Exercise 1 — echo singing: play or sing a three-note pattern (C–D–E), then sing it back without looking. Extend to four and five notes as it gets easy. Exercise 2 — step or leap: have a practice partner (or a simple app that plays notes) play two notes; say whether the second is higher, lower, or the same, and whether it moved by step or leap. Exercise 3 — major or minor: play a triad and identify it as major (bright) or minor (dark). Start with root-position triads, then add inversions. Exercise 4 — scale-degree singing: in C major, sing up the scale with numbers — "1 2 3 4 5 6 7 8" — until each degree has a distinct feel; the pull of 7 up to 8 and the restfulness of 1 become physical sensations. Exercise 5 — dictation: listen to a four-note melody twice, then write the note names. Start with stepwise C-major melodies, then add leaps and new keys.

The secret of ear training is tiny daily doses, not marathon sessions. Ten focused minutes a day — echo singing, then one identification exercise — builds the ear steadily. Always sing: the voice is the instrument that connects ear to theory, and you carry it everywhere. A useful habit: when you hear any music during the day, try to identify the tonic (the "home" note everything returns to) and whether the song feels major or minor. You will be wrong often at first; being wrong attentively is how the ear learns.

Two mistakes to avoid. First, reading every note absolutely from scratch: after the first note, read intervals (step up, leap down a third) — relative reading is far faster and it is how experienced readers actually work. Second, skipping the singing because it feels silly: silent reading builds recognition, but singing builds audiation — hearing music in your head — which is the real goal. Hum if you must; just make sound.

Practice: Using the five-step method, read and sing this melody in G major (one sharp — F#), treble clef, 3/4: Measure 1: G A B | Measure 2: A G F# | Measure 3: G (dotted half note, 3 beats). Then run a ten-minute ear session: five echo-singing rounds, ten step-or-leap identifications, and five major/minor triad identifications. Log your scores — watching the numbers climb over weeks is deeply motivating.
