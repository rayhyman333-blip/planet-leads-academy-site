This lesson teaches you the raw material of all Western music: the twelve notes, how pitch works, and how notes are written on the staff. By the end you will name every note, read notes on the treble and bass clefs, and understand sharps, flats, and naturals. Everything in this course — scales, chords, melodies — is built from these twelve notes, so make sure you can name them cold before moving on.

Western music uses just seven letter names, repeating: A, B, C, D, E, F, G, then back to A. On a piano keyboard, the white keys play these seven letters in order, and the pattern of black keys helps you find them: the group of two black keys sits above C, and the group of three sits above F. Find middle C — near the center of the keyboard, just left of the two-black-key group — and say the white-key letters going up: C D E F G A B C. The distance from one C to the next C is called an octave, and notes an octave apart share the same name because they sound like higher and lower versions of each other. Between some white keys sit black keys, giving five more notes per octave: the sharps and flats. C-sharp (written C#) is the black key just above C; D-flat (written Db) is the black key just below D. Here is the key insight: C# and Db are the same key, the same pitch — one note with two names, depending on context. Notes that sound the same but are spelled differently are called enharmonic. The full twelve-note set, going up from C, is: C, C#, D, D#, E, F, F#, G, G#, A, A#, B. Notice there is no sharp between E and F or between B and C — those pairs are already a half-step apart, the smallest distance in Western music. Every other neighboring pair of white keys is a whole step apart.

Pitch is written on the staff: five horizontal lines with four spaces between them. Notes sit on lines or in spaces, and higher on the staff means higher in pitch. The treble clef (also called the G clef — its curl wraps around the G line) is used for higher notes. Its five lines, bottom to top, are E G B D F — remember with the phrase "Every Good Boy Deserves Fudge". Its four spaces, bottom to top, spell F A C E — the word "face". The bass clef (the F clef — its two dots surround the F line) is used for lower notes. Its lines, bottom to top, are G B D F A ("Good Boys Deserve Fudge Always"), and its spaces are A C E G ("All Cows Eat Grass"). Notes above or below the staff use short extra lines called ledger lines: the C one ledger line below the treble staff is middle C, and the C one ledger line above the bass staff is that same middle C — the two staffs meet there, which is why piano music shows both together.

Sharps (#), flats (b), and naturals (a natural sign cancels a previous sharp or flat) raise or lower a note by a half step. They can appear right before a single note (an accidental) or at the start of every staff line (a key signature, which you will master in Lesson 3). A sharp on F makes F#; a flat on B makes Bb; a natural on a previously sharpened F returns it to plain F. Accidentals apply only to the measure they appear in — a new measure resets everything unless the key signature says otherwise. This is a classic beginner reading mistake: carrying a sharp from one measure into the next.

Two more mistakes to avoid. First, mixing up the clefs: a note on the second line from the bottom is G in treble clef but B in bass clef. Always check which clef you are reading before naming a single note. Second, thinking enharmonic notes are different pitches — F# and Gb sound identical. The spelling matters for theory (which you will see in scales), never for the sound.

Practice: Draw a treble staff and label all nine positions (five lines, four spaces) with their note names from memory, then do the same for the bass staff. On a keyboard or keyboard app, play the twelve notes from C to B saying each name aloud, then play them again naming the enharmonic equivalent (C#, then Db, and so on). Finally, write out these notes and identify each as sharp, flat, or natural, and whether it is enharmonic with another name: F#, Bb, E#, Cb. (Answers: F# is a sharp, enharmonic with Gb; Bb is a flat, enharmonic with A#; E# is a sharp — enharmonic with F; Cb is a flat — enharmonic with B.)
