This lesson teaches you chord progressions: the sequences of chords that form the harmonic backbone of songs. By the end you will play the essential progressions — I–IV–V, I–vi–IV–V, ii–V–I, and the twelve-bar blues — in any key, and understand cadences, the punctuation marks of music. Melody gets the glory, but progression is the structure: learn a handful of progressions and you can accompany thousands of songs.

Start with the most important progression in Western music: I–IV–V. In C major that is C–F–G. The I chord (tonic) is home — stable, restful. The IV chord (subdominant, F) moves away gently. The V chord (dominant, G, often played as G7) creates tension that demands resolution back to I. Play C–F–G–C slowly and feel the journey: home, away, tension, home. This three-chord loop underpins rock and roll, country, folk, and countless pop songs. In G major it is G–C–D; in F major, F–Bb–C. The Roman numerals are the point — learn the pattern once, transpose it anywhere.

Add the vi chord for the famous I–vi–IV–V: in C, C–Am–F–G. This is the progression behind a vast number of ballads and doo-wop songs — the vi adds a touch of melancholy before the lift of IV–V. Variants you will hear everywhere: I–V–vi–IV (C–G–Am–F), the sensitive-pop progression of the last two decades, and vi–IV–I–V (Am–F–C–G), its close cousin. Notice these all use the same four chords in different orders — order is everything in progression, the way word order is everything in a sentence.

The ii–V–I (in C: Dm–G–C, often Dm7–G7–Cmaj7) is the core progression of jazz and the smoothest resolution in harmony. The ii adds a gentle pre-dominant color, the V7 brings the tension, the I (or Imaj7) releases it. Practice it as a loop — Dm7–G7–Cmaj7, Dm7–G7–Cmaj7 — and you will feel why jazz musicians can improvise for hours on it: the cycle never gets old because the resolution is so satisfying. In minor keys, the equivalent is ii°–V–i: in A minor, B°–E–Am (E is often E7).

The twelve-bar blues is a fixed 12-measure form using only I, IV, and V, and it is worth memorizing because it is a complete song structure in itself. In C, one chorus is:

| C | C | C | C | F | F | C | C | G | F | C | G |

Each cell is one measure (in 4/4, often with a shuffle rhythm). The form tells a story: four bars of home, two bars of lift (IV), two bars of home, then the turnaround — V, IV, I, V — that drives back to the top for another chorus. Blues, early rock, and thousands of jazz tunes are built on exactly this frame; soloists improvise over the cycling chords.

Cadences are the punctuation at the ends of phrases. An authentic cadence (V–I) is a period — final and conclusive. A plagal cadence (IV–I) is the "Amen" ending — gentle and conclusive. A half cadence (ending on V) is a comma or question mark — the music pauses but clearly continues. A deceptive cadence (V–vi instead of the expected V–I) is a plot twist — the resolution is dodged, usually once per song for effect. Listen for these in any song: the feeling of "ending" versus "continuing" is cadence at work.

Two mistakes to avoid. First, playing progressions as disconnected chords: connect them smoothly by keeping common tones (notes shared between chords) in the same position and moving the other notes as little as possible — C to F shares the C, C to G shares the G. Second, overusing V–I everywhere: constant full resolutions exhaust the ear. Let some phrases end on IV or ii and save the authentic cadence for the real endings.

Practice: Play these progressions in C major, then transpose each to F major: I–IV–V–I, I–vi–IV–V, ii–V–I. Then play one full chorus of the twelve-bar blues in C, counting the measures aloud. Finally, take any simple melody you know and try accompanying it with just I, IV, and V — change chords where the melody seems to want it, and notice how often three chords are enough.
