This lesson teaches you intervals: the measured distance between two pitches, named by number and quality. By the end you will identify every interval within an octave, understand why some sound smooth and others tense, and use intervals to build chords in the next lesson. If scales are the alphabet, intervals are the vocabulary — every chord and melody is described in interval terms.

An interval has two parts: the number and the quality. The number counts the letter names spanned, including both endpoints: C to E is a third (C-D-E = three letters), C to G is a fifth (C-D-E-F-G = five letters), C to C is a unison (also called a prime), C to the next C is an octave (eight). The quality — major, minor, or perfect — describes the exact size in half steps. Here is the complete table within one octave, measured up from C:

Unison (C–C): 0 half steps, perfect. Minor 2nd (C–Db): 1 half step. Major 2nd (C–D): 2 half steps. Minor 3rd (C–Eb): 3 half steps. Major 3rd (C–E): 4 half steps. Perfect 4th (C–F): 5 half steps. Tritone (C–F# or C–Gb): 6 half steps — the exact middle of the octave. Perfect 5th (C–G): 7 half steps. Minor 6th (C–Ab): 8 half steps. Major 6th (C–A): 9 half steps. Minor 7th (C–Bb): 10 half steps. Major 7th (C–B): 11 half steps. Octave (C–C): 12 half steps.

Unisons, fourths, fifths, and octaves are called perfect intervals — they have a hollow, stable, open sound, and they come in only one normal size. Seconds, thirds, sixths, and sevenths come in major and minor flavors: the major version is one half step larger than the minor. So C to E is a major third (4 half steps) and C to Eb is a minor third (3 half steps). Widen a perfect or major interval by a half step and it becomes augmented (C to F# is an augmented 4th as well as a tritone); shrink a perfect or minor interval by a half step and it becomes diminished (C to Gbb would be a diminished 5th). You will mostly need major, minor, and perfect — augmented and diminished appear in specific chords.

Intervals also have personalities. Consonant intervals (unison, octave, perfect fifth, major and minor thirds and sixths) sound smooth, stable, and restful — they are where phrases come to rest. Dissonant intervals (seconds, sevenths, and especially the tritone) sound tense and unstable — they create the urge to resolve to something consonant. This tension-and-release is the emotional engine of all harmony: composers set up dissonance and then resolve it, the way a story sets up a problem and resolves it. The tritone (C–F#) was historically called "the devil in music" for its extreme tension; in blues and jazz it is used constantly and resolved into smooth chords.

To identify an interval quickly: first count the letter-number (C up to A spans C-D-E-F-G-A = a sixth of some kind), then check the half steps against the major scale of the bottom note. The major scale gives you the major/perfect version of every interval for free: in the C major scale, C to D is a major 2nd, C to E a major 3rd, C to F a perfect 4th, C to G a perfect 5th, C to A a major 6th, C to B a major 7th. Lower the top note by a half step (without changing its letter) and major becomes minor: C to Bb is a minor 7th. Compound intervals go beyond the octave — a ninth is a second plus an octave (C to D above the next C), an eleventh is a fourth plus an octave — but they reduce to their simple forms for naming quality.

Two mistakes to avoid. First, counting the starting note as zero: C to E spans three letter names, so it is a third, not a "two". Always count both endpoints. Second, naming by half steps alone: C to D# is 3 half steps but it is an augmented 2nd, not a minor 3rd, because it spans only two letter names. The letter count decides the number; the half steps decide the quality.

Practice: Identify these intervals (number and quality): C–G, E–C (E up to the next C), F–Db, G–B, A–F, D–C (D up to the next C). (Check: C–G = perfect 5th; E–C = minor 6th; F–Db = minor 6th; G–B = major 3rd; A–F = minor 6th; D–C = minor 7th.) Then, on your keyboard, play each interval from the table above and say whether it sounds consonant or dissonant to you — train your ear alongside your eye.
