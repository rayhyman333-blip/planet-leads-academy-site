This final lesson puts the whole course together: you will compose an original eight-bar melody using everything you have learned, then get a map of where to go next. By the end you will have a finished, singable melody written in notation or note names, built with real compositional technique — not random notes, but shaped phrases with rhythm, contour, and a satisfying ending.

Set your constraints first — limits make composing easier, not harder. Key: C major (no sharps or flats). Meter: 4/4, eight measures, mostly quarter notes with a few half notes for breathing room. Range: keep every note between middle C and the G above it (C D E F G) so it is singable. These boundaries are your canvas; creativity lives inside them.

Step 1: rhythm first. Write eight measures of rhythm before choosing any pitches — for example: | quarter quarter quarter quarter | half quarter quarter | quarter quarter half | whole note (rest a full measure? no — keep it simple) |. Composers often sketch rhythm first because a strong rhythmic shape carries a melody even with plain notes. Clap your rhythm with a metronome until it feels natural.

Step 2: choose chord tones for the strong beats. Underlay a simple progression: | C | C | F | C | G | G | C | C |. (I I IV I V V I I.) Beats 1 and 3 are strong — put chord tones there (for the C measures: C, E, or G; for F: F, A, C; for G: G, B, D). This guarantees your melody always harmonizes, the way real songs do.

Step 3: shape the contour. Melodies breathe in phrases — aim for two four-bar phrases, the second answering the first. Let phrase 1 rise (C D E F G) and phrase 2 fall back home (G F E D C). Mix stepwise motion (which sounds smooth and singable) with one or two small leaps (a third or fourth) for interest. Avoid leaping twice in a row at first — stepwise recovery after a leap is the classic melodic habit.

Step 4: use repetition with variation. Repeat your first two bars exactly, then change the ending of the repeat — the ear loves recognition plus surprise. End phrase 1 on a non-tonic note (G or E) so it feels open, like a question; end phrase 2 on C, the tonic, held a little longer, so it feels answered.

Here is a complete example following every rule above (note names with beats; q = quarter, h = half):

| C(q) D(q) E(q) G(q) | A(h) G(q) E(q) | F(q) E(q) D(q) E(q) | D(h) C(q) D(q) | E(q) G(q) A(q) G(q) | F(h) E(q) D(q) | E(q) D(q) C(q) D(q) | C(h, held 2 beats) rest(q) rest(q) |

Check it: chord tones on strong beats, arch contour up then down, bars 1–2 echoed in bars 5–6 with variation, phrase 1 ending open on D, final C held for closure. Sing it — it sounds like a real folk melody, because it uses the same techniques folk melodies use.

Step 5: finish and reflect. Write your melody cleanly, sing it three times, and change anything that feels awkward — usually a leap that is hard to sing or a rhythm that trips you. There is no single right answer; there is only "does it sing well and end convincingly."

Where to go next depends on what excited you. If reading thrilled you, learn the alto and tenor clefs and start reading simple piano pieces hands together. If harmony appealed to you, study seventh-chord voicings and voice leading — how individual notes move between chords. If songwriting called to you, learn to fit lyrics to rhythm (prosody) and study the forms of the songs you love: verse, chorus, bridge. If jazz called to you, the ii–V–I is your doorway — learn it in all twelve keys. Whatever you choose, the method stays the same: small project, break into steps, sing everything, and trust your ear alongside your eye. You have the complete foundation; the rest is practice.

Practice: Compose your own eight-bar melody following Steps 1–5, in C major, 4/4, using the I–I–IV–I–V–V–I–I progression or one of your own from Lesson 6. Write it in note names with rhythm markings, sing it until it flows, and revise twice. Then transpose it to G major (remember the F#) — transposing is the final proof you understand keys. When it sings well in both keys, you have finished the course.
