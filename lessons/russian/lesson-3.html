This lesson tackles the engine of Russian sentences: present-tense verbs and the noun case system. Russian verbs change their endings for each person — I, you, he/she, we, you (plural/formal), they — and nouns change their endings depending on their job in the sentence. That sounds heavy, but the patterns are regular, and this lesson gives you the two verb groups plus the three cases you need first. Everything else in the course builds on this.

Russian verbs fall into two conjugation groups. Group 1 is the big one — most verbs ending in -ать, -ять, and -еть. Take работать (ra-BO-tat', to work): я работаю (ya ra-BO-ta-yu, I work), ты работаешь (ty ra-BO-ta-yesh, you work), он/она работает (on/a-NA ra-BO-ta-yet, he/she works), мы работаем (my ra-BO-ta-yem, we work), вы работаете (vy ra-BO-ta-ye-tye), они работают (a-NI ra-BO-ta-yut, they work). The pattern: drop -ть, add -ю, -ешь, -ет, -ем, -ете, -ют. It works the same for делать (DYE-lat', to do/make) — делаю, делаешь, делает, делаем, делаете, делают — and знать (znat', to know) — знаю, знаешь, знает, знаем, знаете, знают. Group 2 is mostly verbs ending in -ить. Take говорить (ga-va-RIT', to speak): я говорю (ya ga-va-RYU), ты говоришь (ty ga-va-RISH), он/она говорит (on/a-NA ga-va-RIT), мы говорим (my ga-va-RIM), вы говорите (vy ga-va-RI-tye), они говорят (a-NI ga-va-RYAT). The endings: -ю, -ишь, -ит, -им, -ите, -ят. Practice with жить (zhit', to live): живу, живёшь, живёт, живём, живёте, живут — Я живу в Чикаго (ya zhi-VU f chi-KA-ga, I live in Chicago).

A few very common verbs are irregular — learn them as vocabulary. Хотеть (kha-TYET', to want): хочу, хочешь, хочет, хотим, хотите, хотят — Я хочу кофе (ya kha-CHU KO-fye, I want coffee). Есть (yest', to eat): ем, ешь, ест, едим, едите, едят. Мочь (moch', to be able): могу, можешь, может, можем, можете, могут — Я могу помочь (ya ma-GU pa-MOCH', I can help). Дать (dat', to give) and идти (id-TI, to go) are also irregular; you will meet them in context later.

Here is a surprise that makes Russian easier than it looks: the present tense of "to be" is usually omitted. Я студент (ya stu-DYENT) means "I am a student" — no verb at all. Он врач (on vrach) — he is a doctor. Она дома (a-NA DO-ma) — she is at home. You only use есть (the "to be" verb) to mean "there is / there are": В комнате есть стол (f KOM-na-tye yest' STOL, there is a table in the room). Beginners often insert "is" where Russian needs nothing — drop it.

Now cases. Russian has six noun cases — different endings depending on the noun's role. You do not need all six today; you need the idea plus three of them. The nominative is the dictionary form, used for the subject: Студент читает (stu-DYENT chi-TA-yet, the student reads). The accusative marks the direct object — what is being acted on. For masculine and neuter inanimate nouns it looks exactly like the nominative (Я вижу дом — ya VI-zhu dom, I see a house), but feminine nouns ending in -а change -а to -у: книга becomes книгу — Я читаю книгу (ya chi-TA-yu KNI-gu, I read a book). Москва becomes Москву: Я люблю Москву (ya lyu-BLYU ma-SKVU, I love Moscow). The prepositional case is used after в (in) and на (on/at) for location: в Москве (f ma-SKVYE, in Moscow), в книге (f KNI-gye, in the book), на столе (na sta-LYE, on the table). The other three — genitive, dative, instrumental — you will recognize gradually; for now, just know they exist so the changing endings do not surprise you. Two more essentials: negation is simple — put не before the verb (Я не понимаю — I don't understand; Он не работает — he doesn't work). And word order is flexible because the endings carry the meaning: Я люблю тебя and Тебя люблю я both mean "I love you", though the first is neutral.

Two beginner mistakes to avoid. First, translating "do" word-for-word in questions: English "Do you speak Russian?" is just Вы говорите по-русски? — Russian needs no helper verb, so never say "Вы делаете говорите". Second, forgetting that он/она/оно and the plural pronouns are usually dropped when the verb ending already shows the person — говорю already means "I speak", so я говорю is used mainly for emphasis or clarity.

Dialogue — two coworkers:

— Где ты работаешь? (gdye ty ra-BO-ta-yesh) — Where do you work?
— Я работаю в банке. А ты? (ya ra-BO-ta-yu f BAN-kye) — I work at a bank. And you?
— Я работаю в школе. Я учитель. (ya u-chi-TYEL') — I work at a school. I am a teacher.
— Ты говоришь по-английски? (ty ga-va-RISH pa-an-GLIY-ski) — Do you speak English?
— Да, немного. Я хочу говорить лучше. (nye-MNO-ga) — Yes, a little. I want to speak better.
— Мы можем говорить по-русски! (my MO-zhem) — We can speak Russian!
— Хорошо! Ты живёшь в Москве? (ty zhi-VYOSH f ma-SKVYE) — Good! Do you live in Moscow?
— Да, я живу в Москве. (da, ya zhi-VU) — Yes, I live in Moscow.

Practice: Conjugate in writing — работать, делать, говорить, жить, хотеть — all six persons each. Then write six sentences about yourself: two with Group 1 verbs, two with Group 2 verbs, two using the omitted "to be" (Я студент / Я дома style). Say each aloud. Finally, convert these to the accusative: книга → книгу, Москва → Москву, and write three "Я вижу..." sentences and three "в/на + prepositional" location sentences.
