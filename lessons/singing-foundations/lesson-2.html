This lesson teaches you to find your vocal range: the span of notes you can sing comfortably, from your lowest easy note to your highest easy note. Knowing your range is practical, not a label - it tells you which songs will sit comfortably for your voice, which notes to avoid forcing, and where your voice naturally feels at home. By the end of this lesson you will know your comfortable range and you will have a simple map of it to use when choosing songs.

First, find your lowest comfortable note. Sit or stand in your singing posture, breathe in low, and hum gently on "mmm" starting on a note that feels easy and relaxed - for most people this is somewhere in the middle of their speaking voice. Now slide the hum slowly downward, one small step at a time, like walking down a staircase. Keep going until the sound starts to feel gravelly, weak, or strained, then step back up one note - that note is the bottom of your comfortable range. Write it down. Do not push below it: the lowest notes are not a contest, and forcing them only adds tension. Repeat the slide three times to check that you land on the same bottom note each time.

Now find your highest comfortable note. Start again on an easy middle note and slide the hum slowly upward, one small step at a time. The voice will feel lighter as you climb, and at some point the sound will start to feel thin, tight, or hard to control - step back down one note, and that is the top of your comfortable range for now. Write it down beside the low note. Your range today is simply the distance between those two notes, and it is a starting point, not a fixed limit: ranges grow slowly over months of gentle practice, and they grow by expanding a little at a time, never by forcing.

Between the bottom and the top, your voice has different feels, often called registers. The lower part of your range usually feels warm and speech-like - this is your chest voice, the sound you use when you talk. The upper part usually feels lighter and floatier - this is your head voice, the sound that feels as if it resonates higher up. In the middle there is a passage area, sometimes called the break, where the voice wants to shift from one feel to the other and the sound can wobble or crack. That wobble is normal and every singer has it. The goal of this lesson is not to erase the break but to know where it is: slide slowly through it on a hum, notice where the shift happens, and write that spot down too.

Time for drills. First, the downward slide: hum from an easy middle note down to your lowest comfortable note, three times, checking that you stop at the same note. Second, the upward slide: hum from the same middle note up to your highest comfortable note, three times, stopping at the same top note. Third, the siren: slide your voice slowly up and down across your whole range like a siren, on a gentle "oo" sound, three times, moving smoothly through the passage area without stopping or jumping. The siren teaches your voice to glide through the break instead of cracking at it.

Choosing songs by range is the practical payoff. Before you sing any song, check its lowest and highest notes against your range map. If the song's highest note sits above your comfortable top, the song will feel like a strain every time you reach it - choose a lower key, or choose a different song. If the song sits entirely in the middle of your range, it will feel easy and sound relaxed. As a rule for this course, pick songs whose notes stay inside your comfortable range with at least one note of room at the top and the bottom, so you never have to sing at the very edge of what you can do.

Two common mistakes. The first is confusing your range with your speaking voice: the notes you speak on are usually in the lower-middle of your range, and many beginners assume their singing range ends where their speaking voice ends. It does not - the hum slides in this lesson will show you notes above and below your speaking pitch. The second is forcing the top: pushing hard to reach a high note, which makes the throat tight and the note sharp and thin. High notes come from steady breath and a relaxed throat, not from effort - if a note feels like work, you are above your comfortable range for now.

Practice: Find your lowest and highest comfortable notes using the hum slides, and write both down on the range worksheet, with the passage area marked. Then sing the siren exercise three times on "oo," sliding smoothly across your whole range. Finish by singing a simple, familiar tune and checking whether its notes sit inside your range - write down the highest and lowest notes of the tune and compare them with your map.
