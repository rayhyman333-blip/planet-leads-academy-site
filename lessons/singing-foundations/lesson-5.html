This lesson teaches you to shape vowels and pronounce words clearly while singing. In speech, vowels carry the sound and consonants carry the meaning; in singing, the same is true, but the balance shifts - vowels need to stay open and ringing so the note can sustain, while consonants need to be quick and crisp so the words stay understandable. By the end of this lesson you will sing the five main vowels with an open, relaxed mouth, and pronounce consonants without tightening your jaw.

Start with the five vowels: ah, eh, ee, oh, oo. Sing each one on a single comfortable note, holding it for four slow counts, and notice the shape of your mouth. For "ah," the mouth opens wide and tall, the tongue lies flat, and the jaw drops loosely. For "eh," the mouth opens halfway, the tongue lifts slightly. For "ee," the mouth is narrower, the lips spread gently, and the tongue rises toward the roof of the mouth. For "oh," the lips round into a soft circle and the mouth opens tall. For "oo," the lips round more tightly and the mouth opens smaller. Practice singing all five vowels in a row on the same note, slowly, feeling the mouth reshape for each one. The sound should stay steady and free - if a vowel sounds pinched or thin, the mouth is probably too closed or the jaw is tight.

Now the vowel ladder. Sing the five vowels in order - ah, eh, ee, oh, oo - on a single note, one vowel per beat, four times through. Then sing the ladder on a five-note scale, one vowel per note, climbing and descending. The ladder teaches your mouth to move cleanly between vowel shapes without disturbing the tone. A common trouble spot is the shift from "ee" to "oh": the mouth has to open and round quickly, and beginners often keep the "ee" shape too long, which makes the "oh" sound muddy. Slow the ladder down at that transition until the shift is clean, then speed it back up.

Consonants are the other half of diction. Unlike vowels, which you sustain, consonants are quick events: they start or end a sound and then get out of the way. Practice the consonant drill: sing "mah, nah, lah, dah, bah" on a single note, one syllable per beat, feeling each consonant land crisply at the front of the mouth and then release immediately into the vowel. The tongue and lips do the consonant work - the jaw stays relaxed and does not clamp down. Try the same drill with "tah, kah, sah, zah," noticing how each consonant uses a different part of the mouth. Then sing a line of a familiar song slowly, exaggerating the consonants slightly - not shouting them, just making them clear - and listen for whether every word is understandable.

The jaw deserves special attention because it is where most diction tension lives. Place your fingertips lightly on your jaw joints, just in front of your ears, and sing a line of a song: if you feel the jaw clenching or pushing forward, stop and let it hang loose. A useful check is the two-finger test: you should be able to fit two fingers stacked vertically between your upper and lower teeth while singing "ah" - if you cannot, your jaw is too closed. Practice singing "ah" with the jaw dropped and loose, and notice how much bigger and freer the sound becomes. Relaxed jaw, open mouth, clear consonants: those three habits are the whole of diction.

Two common mistakes. The first is mumbling: vowels that are too closed and consonants that are too soft, so the words blur together and the listener cannot follow the song. If you cannot understand your own words on a recording, the diction needs work - open the vowels and sharpen the consonants. The second is over-enunciating: biting off every consonant so hard that the singing sounds like spelling, with the flow broken by constant interruptions. Consonants should be clear but quick, like a tap on the shoulder, not a shove.

Time for drills. First, the vowel ladder: all five vowels on one note, then on a five-note scale, four times through. Second, the consonant drill: "mah, nah, lah, dah, bah" and "tah, kah, sah, zah" on a single note, four times each. Third, the jaw check: sing a line of a song with fingertips on your jaw joints, stopping and resetting whenever you feel tension.

Practice: Sing the vowel ladder four times, then the consonant drills four times each, then sing one verse of a familiar song slowly, focusing only on diction - open vowels, quick consonants, relaxed jaw. Record it and listen back, writing down one word that was unclear and one vowel that sounded pinched, and repeat that line five times with the fixes in place.
