This lesson teaches you to match pitch accurately and to hear musical distances - intervals - by ear. Pitch is the foundation of singing in tune: if you can hear a note clearly and reproduce it with your voice, you can sing any melody. Ear training is simply the habit of listening carefully before you sing, and it improves faster than most beginners expect. By the end of this lesson you will match single notes reliably and recognize the most common intervals by ear.

Start with single-note matching. Play a note on a piano, keyboard, or tuning app - choose a note in the comfortable middle of your range. Listen to it for a few seconds, then hum the same note on "mmm." Listen to yourself: does your hum sit exactly on the played note, or does it drift above or below it? If you are unsure, play the note again while you hum, and listen for the beating or wobbling sound that happens when two notes are close but not identical - when the wobble disappears, the notes match. Practice this with ten different notes, one at a time, taking your time on each. The goal is not speed but accuracy: one matched note is worth more than ten guessed ones.

Now learn to hear intervals, the distance between two notes. The smallest step is a half step, the move from any note to its nearest neighbor. Two half steps make a whole step. The most useful intervals to learn first are the ones that appear constantly in melodies: the major second (a whole step up), the minor third (three half steps), the major third (four half steps), the perfect fourth (five half steps), the perfect fifth (seven half steps), and the octave (twelve half steps, the same note higher). Play each interval on the keyboard - lower note first, then upper note - and sing both notes back. Give each interval a mental picture: the perfect fifth sounds open and strong, like the start of a fanfare; the major third sounds bright and cheerful; the minor third sounds softer and sadder.

The echo exercise is the fastest way to build your ear. Play a short pattern of three notes on the keyboard, listen carefully, then sing the pattern back without the keyboard. Start with simple patterns that move by step - up one, down one, up one - and gradually add small jumps. Do twenty echo patterns per session, and keep a tally of how many you get exactly right. When you miss one, play it again slowly, sing each note separately, and then sing the whole pattern again. The echo exercise works because it forces you to hold a sound in your mind and reproduce it - exactly what singing a melody requires.

Add the slide-and-match drill for notes that feel hard to reach. If a target note is above your comfortable starting point, do not jump straight to it: slide your voice slowly upward from a note you can match, and stop when you hear the slide meet the target note. The slide gives your ear time to compare the moving pitch with the target, and it teaches your voice to find notes by listening rather than by guessing. Practice five slide-and-match exercises per session, choosing target notes a little above and a little below your starting note each time.

Two common mistakes. The first is singing before listening: beginners hear a note and immediately blurt out a guess, and the guess is usually wrong. Build the habit of a silent beat - hear the note, hold it in your mind for a second, then sing. That one-second pause doubles your accuracy. The second is drifting flat on long notes: the pitch sags as the breath runs out. Keep the breath support engaged through the whole note, and imagine the note staying lifted, like a balloon you are holding up, rather than letting it sink.

Time for drills. First, single-note matching: ten notes, played then hummed, checking for the wobble that signals a mismatch. Second, interval singing: play and sing each of the six intervals above, five times each, naming the interval as you sing it. Third, the echo exercise: twenty three-note patterns, sung back from memory, with a tally of exact matches.

Practice: Do ten single-note matches, then the six intervals five times each, then twenty echo patterns, writing down your score. Finish by singing a simple, familiar tune slowly, stopping on each note to check whether it matches the melody you hear in your head - if a note feels wrong, play it on the keyboard and match it before moving on.
