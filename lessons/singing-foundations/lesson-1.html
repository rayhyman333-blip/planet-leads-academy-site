This lesson teaches you how to breathe and stand for singing. Everything later in this course - holding long notes, singing in tune, shaping clear words - builds directly on the physical habits you set up today. Good breathing and posture prevent the two problems that stop most beginners: running out of air halfway through a phrase, and tension in the neck and shoulders that makes the voice sound tight and tired. Give this lesson a full practice session on its own. It may feel like you are not really singing yet, but this foundation work pays off every single time you open your mouth to sing.

Start with posture. Stand with your feet about shoulder-width apart, your weight balanced evenly between both feet, and your knees soft - not locked straight and not bent deeply. Let your arms hang relaxed at your sides. Imagine a gentle thread pulling the top of your head upward, lengthening your spine, while your shoulders stay down and loose. Your chin should be level, neither tucked down toward your chest nor lifted high toward the ceiling. Your chest stays open and lifted, but not puffed out or stiff. Sit tall in the same way if you prefer to practice sitting: both feet flat on the floor, back straight but not rigid, arms relaxed. Good posture is not a military pose - it is a balanced, easy stance that lets your breath move freely.

Now the breath. Place one hand on your belly, just below your ribs, and one hand on your chest. Breathe in slowly through your nose and notice which hand moves: for singing, the lower hand should move outward while the upper hand stays almost still. This is low breathing, sometimes called belly breathing, and it gives you the steady air supply singing needs. Your belly expands as you breathe in, and it draws gently back in as you breathe out. Practice this for two minutes: breathe in for four slow counts, feeling the belly expand, then breathe out for four slow counts on a soft "sss" sound, feeling the belly draw in steadily. The hiss should be even and unbroken - if it sputters or fades, you are letting the air out in bursts instead of a steady stream.

The next skill is managing the air as you sing. Singing uses far less air than most beginners expect; the goal is a slow, controlled release, not a big blast. Try this: breathe in low for four counts, then sing a single comfortable note on "ah" for as long as you can, keeping the sound steady and even. Count how many seconds you last, and repeat the exercise five times, trying to keep the tone from wobbling or fading at the end. Most beginners find the note gets shaky near the end - that is the moment the breath support runs out, and it improves with practice. The trick is to start the note gently, with no push or burst, and to keep the belly engaged as the note goes on, as if you were slowly squeezing air out of a tube.

Time for drills. First, the belly-breath check: five slow breaths with hands on belly and chest, watching the lower hand move and the upper hand stay still. Second, the hiss exercise: breathe in for four counts, hiss out on "sss" for eight counts, keeping the sound perfectly even - repeat six times, then try hissing for twelve counts. Third, the long-tone exercise: breathe in low, sing one comfortable note on "ah" for as long as you can, five times, writing down how many seconds each attempt lasts so you can watch the number grow over the coming weeks.

Two common mistakes to watch for. The first is chest breathing: the shoulders rise and the chest heaves on every breath, which gives you a shallow, shaky air supply and a tight throat. If your shoulders move when you breathe in, stop and reset with your hands on your belly until the lower hand is doing the work. The second is pushing: forcing a big burst of air at the start of a note, which makes the sound blast and then fade. Singing is a steady stream, not a sneeze - start each note gently and let the breath flow out evenly.

Practice: Stand in your singing posture and do the belly-breath check five times. Then do the hiss exercise six times, aiming for eight even counts each time. Finish with five long tones on "ah," one comfortable note each, writing down the length of each one. Before every exercise, run the checklist: feet balanced, knees soft, shoulders down, chin level, belly breathing. End the session by singing a simple, familiar tune once through, standing tall and breathing low, just to feel the difference good posture makes.
