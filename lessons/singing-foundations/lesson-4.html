This lesson teaches you to warm up safely: gentle exercises that prepare your voice for singing without strain. A warm-up is not a performance - it is a slow, easy stretch for the voice, like walking before running. Singers who warm up regularly find their voices respond faster, stay in tune more easily, and feel less tired after practice. By the end of this lesson you will have a ten-minute warm-up routine you can use before every practice session and every song.

Start with the body, not the voice. Roll your shoulders slowly backward five times, then forward five times, letting them drop down and away from your ears. Turn your head gently from side to side, then tilt it slowly toward each shoulder, holding each stretch for a slow count of five - never force the stretch, and never roll your head in a full circle. Shake your hands out, then do five slow belly breaths from Lesson 1, feeling the lower hand move while the shoulders stay still. This two-minute body warm-up removes the tension that makes the voice sound tight, and it signals to your brain that singing time has started.

Now the voice, starting with the gentlest sound of all: the hum. Hum on "mmm" at a comfortable middle pitch for ten seconds, feeling a light buzzing in your lips and face. Slide the hum slowly up a few notes and back down, like a small wave, five times. Humming is gentle because the lips are closed and the sound is soft - it wakes the voice up without asking it to work hard. Next, lip trills: blow air through loosely closed lips so they flutter, like a horse, while sliding the pitch slowly up and down. Lip trills are one of the safest warm-ups because the fluttering lips keep the airflow steady and the throat relaxed. Do lip trills for two minutes, sliding through the middle of your range.

Add sirens on "oo" and "ee." A siren is a slow slide from the bottom of your comfortable range to the top and back down again, taking about ten seconds each way. Sing the siren on "oo" three times, then on "ee" three times, keeping the volume soft and the slide smooth - no jumps, no pushing, no holding your breath. The siren stretches the voice gently across its whole range and smooths the passage area where the voice shifts between its lower and upper feels. If the siren cracks or wobbles at the passage area, slow it down and make it softer - the goal is a smooth glide, not a perfect sound.

Finish the warm-up with gentle scales. Sing a five-note pattern - up five notes and back down - on "ah," starting on a comfortable middle note, then move the whole pattern up one step and repeat, climbing slowly through the middle of your range. Keep the volume moderate and the sound easy; if a note feels tight, stop climbing and come back down. Do five of these patterns, then sing the same five-note pattern on "ee" and on "oo," noticing how each vowel feels different in your mouth. End the warm-up with one minute of silence - no talking, no singing - letting the voice settle before you begin your practice.

Two common mistakes. The first is warming up too hard: beginners treat the warm-up like a performance, singing loudly and pushing for high notes, which tires the voice before the real practice begins. A warm-up should feel easy - if you are working hard, you are doing it wrong. The second is skipping the warm-up entirely on days when time is short. Even three minutes of humming and lip trills is better than none, and it protects your voice from the strain of starting cold. If your voice feels tired, hoarse, or sore, stop singing and rest it - rest is part of good practice, not a failure of it.

Time for drills. First, the body warm-up: shoulder rolls, gentle head turns, and five belly breaths, two minutes. Second, hums and lip trills: five hum slides and two minutes of lip trills, all soft. Third, sirens and scales: three sirens on "oo," three on "ee," and five five-note scale patterns on "ah," all at a comfortable volume.

Practice: Do the full ten-minute warm-up exactly as written - body, hums, lip trills, sirens, scales - before your next three practice sessions. Write down how your voice feels after the warm-up each time, using one honest sentence, and notice whether the feeling improves across the three days.
