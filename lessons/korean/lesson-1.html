This lesson teaches you to read and write hangul, the Korean alphabet — one of the most logical writing systems in the world. Hangul was designed so that each letter's shape hints at how your mouth makes the sound, and the whole alphabet can be learned in a day of focused study. Once you can read hangul, every sign, menu, and message in Korea opens up to you. Your goal today: read every character below and pronounce syllable blocks correctly.

Hangul has 14 consonants and 10 basic vowels. The consonants: ㄱ (g/k), ㄴ (n), ㄷ (d/t), ㄹ (r/l), ㅁ (m), ㅂ (b/p), ㅅ (s), ㅇ (silent at the start of a syllable, "ng" at the end), ㅈ (j), ㅊ (ch), ㅋ (k), ㅌ (t), ㅍ (p), ㅎ (h). The vowels: ㅏ (a), ㅑ (ya), ㅓ (eo — like the "u" in "cut"), ㅕ (yeo), ㅗ (o), ㅛ (yo), ㅜ (u — "oo"), ㅠ (yu), ㅡ (eu — say "oo" with lips spread flat), ㅣ (i — "ee"). The ㅓ/ㅗ and ㅡ distinctions are the hardest for English speakers: ㅓ (eo) is open and relaxed, ㅗ (o) is rounded. Practice the pair: 거 (geo) vs 고 (go), and 서 (seo) vs 소 (so).

Letters stack into syllable blocks, read left-to-right, top-to-bottom. A block always starts with a consonant: 가 = ㄱ + ㅏ (ga), 나 = ㄴ + ㅏ (na), 다 = ㄷ + ㅏ (da). With a final consonant (called batchim) underneath: 한 = ㅎ + ㅏ + ㄴ (han), 국 = ㄱ + ㅜ + ㄱ (guk). The silent ㅇ fills the first slot when a syllable starts with a vowel: 아 (a), 어 (eo), 오 (o). Read these real words now: 한국 (han-guk, Korea), 한국어 (han-gu-geo, Korean language), 서울 (seo-ul, Seoul), 김치 (gim-chi, kimchi), 사랑 (sa-rang, love). Notice each block is one syllable — say them block by block.

Five pronunciation rules make your Korean sound natural. Rule 1: consonants soften between vowels — the ㄱ in 아기 (a-gi, baby) sounds like "g," but at the start of a word like 가방 (ga-bang, bag) it is a light "k." Rule 2: double consonants ㄲ, ㄸ, ㅃ, ㅆ, ㅉ are tense and unaspirated — say 아까 (a-kka, a while ago) with tight, held consonants, no puff of air. Rule 3: final consonants (batchim) neutralize — a syllable-final ㄱ, ㅋ, or ㄲ all sound like a soft unreleased "k," so 부엌 (bu-eok, kitchen) ends with a stopped "k," never a released one. Rule 4: ㅅ before ㅣ, ㅑ, ㅕ, ㅛ, ㅠ sounds like "sh" — 시 (shi, as in 시간, time). Rule 5: ㄹ is a light flap between vowels (like the quick "tt" in American "butter") and an "l" at the end of a syllable: 사랑 (sa-rang) flaps, 서울 (seo-ul) ends with "l."

Combined vowels (diphthongs) complete the set: ㅐ (ae, as in "cat"), ㅔ (e, as in "bed"), ㅒ (yae), ㅖ (ye), ㅘ (wa), ㅙ (wae), ㅚ (oe — round your lips for "o" but say "eh"), ㅝ (wo), ㅞ (we), ㅟ (wi), ㅢ (ui). The ㅐ/ㅔ pair sounds nearly identical in modern speech — don't stress the difference. Practice words: 왜 (wae, why), 뭐 (mwo, what — colloquial), 의사 (ui-sa, doctor), 교회 (gyo-hoe, church).

Common beginner mistakes to fix now. First, pronouncing every consonant with English force — Korean consonants are lighter and less aspirated than English ones; say 다 (da) softly, not with a burst. Second, reading blocks letter-by-letter instead of syllable-by-syllable — train yourself to see 한 as "han," one unit. Third, ignoring batchim: 한국 is "han-guk," two syllables — beginners who say "ha-n-gu-k" as four beats sound choppy.

Practice: Write all 14 consonants and 10 vowels from memory, then check. Read these words aloud block by block: 가방 (bag), 나비 (butterfly), 다리 (leg/bridge), 라면 (noodles), 머리 (head), 바다 (sea), 사과 (apple), 우유 (milk), 전화 (phone), 학교 (school). Then write your name in hangul as best you can using today's letters, and read this sentence: 저는 학생입니다 (jeo-neun hak-saeng-im-ni-da — I am a student). It is your first real Korean sentence.
