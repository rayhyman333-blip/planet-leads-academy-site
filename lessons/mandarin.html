Lesson 1: Tones, Pinyin, and Greetings

Mandarin pronunciation has two parts you must learn together: sounds and tones. Pinyin spells the sounds with Latin letters, but pinyin is not English spelling. For example, x, q, and zh are not pronounced as in English, and you must learn them as Mandarin sounds. Tones are changes in pitch that distinguish words. Mandarin has four main tones plus a neutral tone. First tone is high and level, marked with a flat line, as in mā. Second tone rises, marked with a rising line, as in má. Third tone falls and then rises, marked with a v shape, as in mǎ. Fourth tone falls sharply, marked with a falling line, as in mà. The neutral tone is light and short, with no mark, as in ma in some particles. Changing the tone can change the meaning: mā can mean mother, má can mean hemp, mǎ can mean horse, and mà can mean to scold. That famous example shows why tones are not decoration; they are part of the word.

Start with greetings. Nǐ hǎo means hello, with nǐ meaning you and hǎo meaning good. Note that nǐ is third tone and hǎo is third tone, but when two third tones meet, the first one is pronounced like a second tone in speech, so nǐ hǎo sounds like ní hǎo. That is a regular tone change, and you will hear it often. Nín hǎo is the polite form for someone older or in a formal setting. Zǎoshang hǎo means good morning, wǎnshang hǎo means good evening. Zàijiàn means goodbye. Xièxie means thank you. Bù kèqi means you are welcome, literally not polite, in the sense of no need to be polite. Duìbuqǐ means sorry. Méi guānxi means it does not matter or no problem. Qǐng means please, and it is often used before a request.

To introduce yourself, say Wǒ jiào, followed by your name, meaning my name is called. Wǒ means I, jiào means to be called. Wǒ shì means I am, with shì meaning to be. Wǒ shì xuésheng means I am a student, with xuésheng meaning student. Hěn gāoxìng rènshi nǐ means very pleased to meet you, with hěn meaning very, gāoxìng meaning happy, and rènshi meaning to know or meet. A full short introduction is: Nǐ hǎo, wǒ jiào Anna. Wǒ shì xuésheng. Hěn gāoxìng rènshi nǐ. That is a complete, polite exchange you can use from day one.

A common mistake is to speak Mandarin with English stress, making one syllable loud and the others weak. Mandarin syllables are more even in length, with tone doing the work that stress does in English. Another mistake is to ignore tones because you think context will save you. Context helps, but tones are part of learning the word, so learn each new word with its tone from the start. A third mistake is to read pinyin as English: c in pinyin is like ts, q is like ch with the tongue forward, x is like sh with the tongue forward, and zh is like j with the tongue curled back. Practise these sounds slowly before you try to speak fast.

Practice exercise: Say each tone on mā, má, mǎ, mà in order, then in mixed order, until you can hear the difference. Then practise five greetings aloud: Nǐ hǎo, Nín hǎo, Zǎoshang hǎo, Xièxie, Zàijiàn. Finally, write and say your own introduction in three sentences, using Wǒ jiào, Wǒ shì, and Hěn gāoxìng rènshi nǐ. Record yourself if you can, and check: is each syllable clear, and does the tone rise or fall in the right place?

Practise slowly.

Lesson 2: Characters, Numbers 1–10, and Counting

Chinese characters are not pictures of whole ideas in the simple sense often claimed. Many characters combine a meaning part and a sound part, and some began as pictures, but the system is a writing system with its own rules. This lesson introduces a small set of characters, numbers, and the idea of measure words, which are essential in Mandarin counting.

Simplified characters for numbers are: 一 1, 二 2, 三 3, 四 4, 五 5, 六 6, 七 7, 八 8, 九 9, 十 10. In pinyin they are: yī, èr, sān, sì, wǔ, liù, qī, bā, jiǔ, shí. Notice that yī changes tone in speech before other tones: before a fourth tone it sounds like second tone, as in yí gè, and before other tones it often sounds like fourth tone. Do not worry about every change yet; learn the dictionary tone first, then notice the changes in phrases. Stroke order matters for handwriting: generally write top to bottom, left to right, horizontal before vertical, and outside before inside. Following stroke order makes characters easier to recognise and write neatly, even if you mainly type.

Counting in Mandarin needs a measure word between the number and the noun, similar to saying a cup of water in English. The general measure word is gè: yí gè rén means one person, with rén meaning person; liǎng gè píngguǒ means two apples, with píngguǒ meaning apple. Note that liǎng, not èr, is used before a measure word for two in most cases. That is a regular rule: say liǎng gè, not èr gè. For books, the measure word is běn: yì běn shū means one book, with shū meaning book. For cups, it is bēi: yì bēi shuǐ means a cup of water, with shuǐ meaning water. For animals, it is zhī: yì zhī māo means a cat, with māo meaning cat, and yì zhī gǒu means a dog, with gǒu meaning dog.

Worked examples: Wǒ yǒu sān gè péngyou means I have three friends, with yǒu meaning to have and péngyou meaning friend. Tā yǒu yì běn shū means he or she has a book, with tā meaning he, she, or it, spelled the same in pinyin but written with different characters for male, female, and non-human. Zhè shì yí gè píngguǒ means this is an apple, with zhè meaning this and shì meaning to be. Nà shì liǎng gè píngguǒ means those are two apples, with nà meaning that. Zhè and nà are a pair, like this and that, and they combine with gè to mean this one and that one: zhè gè means this one, nà gè means that one.

A common mistake is to leave out the measure word and say yī rén or sān shū, which sounds incomplete. Another mistake is to use èr before a measure word instead of liǎng. A third mistake is to confuse zhè and nà, pointing to something near you and saying nà. Practise with objects around you: point near and say zhè gè, point far and say nà gè, until the pair feels automatic. A fourth mistake is to write characters in any order and then find them hard to read later; slow, ordered strokes pay off.

Practice exercise: Count from 1 to 10 aloud, then backwards, using both pinyin and characters. Then write ten counting phrases: five with gè, three with běn, and two with bēi, using nouns from this lesson. For example: yì běn shū, liǎng bēi shuǐ. Then make five sentences with yǒu, such as Wǒ yǒu yì zhī māo, I have a cat. Check each phrase: number, measure word, noun, in that order, and liǎng for two before a measure word.

Lesson 3: Shì, Yǒu, De, and Simple Sentences

Mandarin word order is generally subject, verb, object, like English, which makes basic sentences easier at first than in some other languages. The challenge is not word order but small words that do specific jobs: shì for identity, yǒu for having and existence, de for possession and description, and question words that stay in place.

Shì links a subject to a noun description: Wǒ shì lǎoshī means I am a teacher, with lǎoshī meaning teacher. Tā shì yīshēng means he or she is a doctor, with yīshēng meaning doctor. Zhè shì wǒ de shū means this is my book, with de marking possession. The negative of shì is bú shì: Wǒ bú shì xuésheng means I am not a student. Note that bù changes tone before a fourth tone, sounding like bú, which is a regular tone change. Shì is not used before adjectives in the same way as English is: to say something is good, you say Hěn hǎo, not Shì hǎo. That is a key difference, and it is explained fully in Lesson 6.

Yǒu means to have, and it also introduces existence: Wǒ yǒu yí gè dìdi means I have a younger brother, with dìdi meaning younger brother. Méi yǒu means not to have: Wǒ méi yǒu māo means I do not have a cat. In a place sentence, yǒu comes after the place: Zhèr yǒu yí gè shāngdiàn means there is a shop here, with zhèr meaning here and shāngdiàn meaning shop. Nàr yǒu yí gè xuéxiào means there is a school there, with nàr meaning there and xuéxiào meaning school. Do not use shì for there is; use yǒu after the place.

De has several jobs, and the main ones here are possession and linking a description to a noun. Wǒ de means my, nǐ de means your, tā de means his, her, or its. Wǒ de péngyou means my friend. Hóngsè de shū means a red book, with hóngsè meaning red colour. The description comes before de and the noun comes after. To ask whose something is, say Shéi de, meaning whose, with shéi meaning who. Shéi de shū means whose book. To ask what something is, say Shénme, meaning what: Zhè shì shénme means what is this. To ask who someone is, say Shéi: Tā shì shéi means who is he or she.

Questions in Mandarin often keep the same word order as statements, with a question word in place or a final particle. Yes-no questions can add ma at the end: Nǐ shì xuésheng ma means are you a student. The answer is often a repeat of the verb, not just yes: Shì means yes, I am, and Bú shì means no, I am not. Another question pattern uses the verb twice, positive and negative: Nǐ shì bú shì xuésheng means are you a student or not. That pattern is very common: Nǐ yǒu méi yǒu shū means do you have a book or not. Both patterns are correct; ma questions are simpler at first, and verb-not-verb questions sound natural in conversation.

A common mistake is to add shì before every adjective, saying Wǒ shì hǎo, which is wrong. Another is to use yǒu for identity, saying Wǒ yǒu xuésheng for I am a student, which is wrong; yǒu is for having, shì is for being a noun. A third mistake is to move question words to the front as in English. In Mandarin, the question word stays where the answer would go: Nǐ jiào shénme míngzi means what is your name, literally you are called what name, with míngzi meaning name. Do not move shénme to the front.

Practice exercise: Write twelve sentences: four with shì for identity, four with yǒu or méi yǒu for having, and four with de for possession. Then turn four of them into questions, two with ma and two with verb-not-verb. Answer each question with a full repeat, not a bare yes or no. For example: Q: Nǐ yǒu shū ma. A: Wǒ yǒu shū. Or: Wǒ méi yǒu shū. Read each pair aloud and check tones, especially bù before a fourth tone.

Lesson 4: Verbs, Time Words, and Daily Routine

Mandarin verbs do not change for tense or for person. The same verb form is used for I, you, he, she, and they, and time words or context show when something happens. That makes verb learning simpler in one way, but it means you must use time words clearly and learn aspect particles that show completion or experience.

Common verbs in this lesson are: chī to eat, hē to drink, kàn to see, read, or watch, xué to learn or study, shuìjiào to sleep, qǐchuáng to get up, gōngzuò to work, mǎi to buy, qù to go, lái to come, ài to love or like very much, xǐhuan to like. Examples: Wǒ chī mǐfàn means I eat rice or I eat a meal, with mǐfàn meaning cooked rice or a meal. Wǒ hē shuǐ means I drink water. Wǒ kàn shū means I read a book. Wǒ xué Hànyǔ means I learn Chinese, with Hànyǔ meaning the Chinese language. Do not add a tense ending to these verbs; add a time word instead.

Time words usually come before the verb, after the subject. Jīntiān means today, míngtiān tomorrow, zuótiān yesterday. Měitiān means every day. Zǎoshang means morning, zhōngwǔ midday, wǎnshang evening or night. Examples: Wǒ jīntiān xué Hànyǔ means I study Chinese today. Wǒ měitiān chī zǎofàn means I eat breakfast every day, with zǎofàn meaning breakfast. Wǒ zuótiān kàn diànyǐng means I watched a film yesterday, with diànyǐng meaning film. Wǒ míngtiān qù xuéxiào means I will go to school tomorrow. The verb does not change; jīntiān, zuótiān, and míngtiān do the time work.

To show a completed action, Mandarin often uses le after the verb: Wǒ chī le fàn means I ate, with le marking completion. To show experience, use guo: Wǒ qù guo Zhōngguó means I have been to China, with Zhōngguó meaning China and guo marking experience. To show an action in progress, use zài before the verb: Wǒ zài kàn shū means I am reading a book. To show a continuing state, use zhe after the verb in some patterns, but zài plus verb is enough for this lesson. Do not use le for every past sentence; a time word like zuótiān is often enough, and le is for completion or a new situation, not a simple past tense marker.

Daily routine vocabulary includes: zǎofàn breakfast, wǔfàn lunch, wǎnfàn dinner or supper, shuìjiào to sleep, qǐchuáng to get up, shàngkè to attend class, xiàkè to finish class, huí jiā to return home. A short routine might be: Wǒ měitiān zǎoshang qǐchuáng, chī zǎofàn, qù xuéxiào, shàngkè, huí jiā, chī wǎnfàn, kàn shū, shuìjiào. That is a list of verbs in order, which is natural in Mandarin because verbs do not need connecting words in a list. To say with someone, add gēn before the person: Wǒ gēn péngyou qù diànyǐngyuàn means I go to the cinema with a friend, with diànyǐngyuàn meaning cinema.

A common mistake is to add English tense endings to Mandarin verbs, or to change the verb for he and she. There is no such change. Another mistake is to put time words after the verb as in English in some sentences; in Mandarin, time words for when usually come before the verb. A third mistake is to use le as if it meant past in every sentence, producing le even when the action is habitual. Use měitiān for habit, zuótiān for yesterday, and le only when you mean completed or changed.

Practice exercise: Write your daily routine in eight sentences, using at least six different verbs and three time words. Include one sentence with le for a completed action, one with zài for an action in progress, and one with gēn for with someone. Then read the routine aloud, checking that no verb has an English tense ending and that each time word comes before the verb. As a second task, translate five English sentences into Mandarin, choosing the right time word instead of changing the verb: I eat breakfast every day, I watched a film yesterday, I will go to school tomorrow, I am reading a book now, I have been to China.

Lesson 5: Numbers to 100, Time, Days, and Places

Larger numbers in Mandarin are regular, which is good news after the measure-word work in Lesson 2. Once you know 1 to 10 and the words for ten, hundred, and thousand, you can build most numbers by combining them in order. Time and days follow similar regular patterns, and place words use zài and qù in ways that are worth learning as a set.

Numbers: shí is 10, èrshí is 20, sānshí is 30, sìshí is 40, wǔshí is 50, liùshí is 60, qīshí is 70, bāshí is 80, jiǔshí is 90, yìbǎi is 100. To make 11 to 19, say ten plus the number: shíyī is 11, shí'èr is 12, shísān is 13. To make 21 to 99, say the tens plus the ones: èrshíyī is 21, sānshíwǔ is 35, jiǔshíjiǔ is 99. For 101, say yìbǎi líng yī, with líng meaning zero as a placeholder. For money, kuài is the colloquial word for yuan: sān kuài means three yuan. For age, suì is used: Wǒ èrshí suì means I am twenty years old. Do not use nián, year, for age in this pattern; suì is the age word.

Days of the week use xīngqī plus a number: xīngqīyī Monday, xīngqī'èr Tuesday, xīngqīsān Wednesday, xīngqīsì Thursday, xīngqīwǔ Friday, xīngqīliù Saturday, xīngqītiān or xīngqīrì Sunday, with tiān and rì both used for Sunday in this pattern. Months use number plus yuè: yí yuè January, èr yuè February, sān yuè March, and so on to shí'èr yuè December. Dates use number plus hào or rì: yí hào or yí rì for the first day of the month. A full date is year, month, day, in that order: èr líng èr liù nián, yí yuè, yí hào would be 1 January 2026 in that pattern, with nián meaning year. Notice the order: largest unit first, which is opposite to the common English order.

Clock time uses diǎn for hour and fēn for minute: yì diǎn means one o'clock, liǎng diǎn means two o'clock, using liǎng for two before a measure-like word here, sān diǎn bàn means half past three, with bàn meaning half. For minutes, say the hour, then the minutes: sān diǎn shí fēn means 3:10. To ask the time, say Xiànzài jǐ diǎn means what time is it now, with xiànzài meaning now and jǐ meaning how many for small numbers. Jǐ is used for numbers under ten in questions; duōshao is used for larger numbers and prices: Zhè gè duōshao qián means how much money is this, with qián meaning money.

Place words and prepositions: zài means at or in, and it comes before the place for being somewhere: Wǒ zài jiā means I am at home, with jiā meaning home. Wǒ zài xuéxiào means I am at school. For going somewhere, use qù plus place, without zài: Wǒ qù xuéxiào means I go to school. Wǒ qù shāngdiàn mǎi dōngxi means I go to the shop to buy things, with dōngxi meaning things. Cóng means from, dào means to or arrive: Wǒ cóng jiā dào xuéxiào means I go from home to school. Other places include: yīyuàn hospital, yínháng bank, túshūguǎn library, gōngyuán park, fàndiàn restaurant or hotel depending on context, chēzhàn station, fēijīchǎng airport.

A common mistake is to say Wǒ zài qù xuéxiào for I go to school; zài is for being somewhere, qù is for going, and they are not combined in that way for a simple going sentence. Another mistake is to put the date in English order, month before day, when Mandarin puts year, month, day. A third mistake is to use èr for two o'clock instead of liǎng diǎn; in time, liǎng diǎn is the natural form. A fourth mistake is to confuse jǐ and duōshao: use jǐ diǎn for time and jǐ gè for a small number of things, and duōshao qián for prices.

Practice exercise: Write numbers 11, 15, 20, 25, 30, 47, 99, and 100 in pinyin and characters. Then write a timetable with six times, using diǎn, fēn, and bàn. Then write six place sentences: two with zài for being somewhere, two with qù for going, and two with cóng and dào. Ask and answer: Xiànzài jǐ diǎn, Jīntiān xīngqījǐ, meaning what day of the week is today, and Nǐ qù nǎr, meaning where are you going, with nǎr meaning where. Check each sentence for zài versus qù.

Lesson 6: Adjectives, Comparisons, and Describing People and Things

Mandarin adjectives do not change for gender or number, and they do not need a verb to be in a sentence in the way English adjectives do. The pattern is subject plus adjective, often with hěn before the adjective, not because it always means very, but because hěn makes the sentence sound complete. That use of hěn is one of the first things to learn, because it surprises English speakers.

Examples: Zhè gè píngguǒ hěn hǎo means this apple is good, with hǎo meaning good. Tā hěn gāo means he or she is tall, with gāo meaning tall. Zhè běn shū hěn yǒuyìsi means this book is interesting, with yǒuyìsi meaning interesting. To make a negative, put bù before the adjective: Zhè gè píngguǒ bù hǎo means this apple is not good. Tā bù gāo means he or she is not tall. To say very, you can still use hěn, fēicháng, or hěn hěn, but in a simple description hěn is often just a link, not a strong very. To say really very, use fēicháng: Tā fēicháng gāo means he or she is very tall. To say a little, use yìdiǎnr: Yìdiǎnr xiǎo means a little small, with xiǎo meaning small.

Common adjectives in this lesson are: dà big, xiǎo small, xīn new, jiù old for things, hǎo good, huài bad, guì expensive, piányi cheap, kuài fast, màn slow, yuǎn far, jìn near, máng busy, xián free or not busy, kāixīn happy, lèi tired, è hungry, kě thirsty. Examples: Zhè jiàn yīfu hěn piányi means this item of clothing is cheap, with yīfu meaning clothes and jiàn as the measure word for clothing. Nà jiā fàndiàn hěn guì means that restaurant is expensive. Wǒ jīntiān hěn máng means I am very busy today. Wǒ hěn lèi means I am tired. To ask how someone is, say Nǐ hǎo ma, which is a greeting, or Nǐ zěnmeyàng, meaning how are you, with zěnmeyàng meaning how.

Comparisons use bǐ for than: Wǒ bǐ tā gāo means I am taller than he or she is. Zhè běn shū bǐ nà běn shū yǒuyìsi means this book is more interesting than that book. To say the most, use zuì: Tā shì zuì gāo de means he or she is the tallest, with de linking the description. To say as much as, use gēn and yíyàng: Wǒ gēn tā yíyàng gāo means I am as tall as he or she is, with yíyàng meaning the same. To say more and more, use yuè lái yuè: Yuè lái yuè máng means busier and busier. These patterns are regular, and they avoid needing a comparative adjective form as in English.

Describing people uses the same patterns: Tā yǒu hēi tóufa means he or she has black hair, with hēi meaning black and tóufa meaning hair. Tā de yǎnjing hěn dà means his or her eyes are big, with yǎnjing meaning eyes. Tā chuān hóng yīfu means he or she is wearing red clothes, with chuān meaning to wear. For age and height questions: Tā duō dà means how old is he or she, literally how big, and Tā duō gāo means how tall is he or she. Answers use suì for age and the adjective for height: Tā èrshí suì, Tā hěn gāo.

A common mistake is to say Tā shì gāo for he is tall; shì is for nouns, not for adjectives in this pattern. Use Tā hěn gāo. Another mistake is to put bù in the wrong place in a comparison or to translate more as gèng in every sentence; gèng means even more and is used in a comparison, as in Wǒ bǐ tā gèng gāo, but bǐ alone already makes the comparison. A third mistake is to use jiù for old people; jiù is for old things, and for old people the word is lǎo, as in lǎo rén, old person. Keep jiù for objects and lǎo for people in this lesson.

Practice exercise: Write ten descriptions: five with hěn plus adjective, three negatives with bù, and two comparisons with bǐ. Then describe a friend or family member in five sentences, using at least three adjectives, one yǒu sentence for hair or eyes, and one comparison. Read them aloud and check: no shì before a bare adjective, bù directly before the adjective, and bǐ between the two things compared, with the adjective at the end.

Lesson 7: Liking, Wanting, Ability, and Polite Requests

Preference and requests in Mandarin use a small set of verbs and patterns that are easy to confuse if you translate word for word from English. This lesson separates liking a thing, wanting a thing, wanting to do something, being able to do something, and asking someone to do something, because each has its own pattern.

Xǐhuan means to like: Wǒ xǐhuan píngguǒ means I like apples. Wǒ xǐhuan kàn shū means I like reading books, with the verb kàn shū directly after xǐhuan. Ài means to love or like very much: Wǒ ài wǒ de jiā means I love my family, with jiā meaning family or home depending on context. To ask what someone likes, say Nǐ xǐhuan shénme means what do you like. To say you do not like something, say Bù xǐhuan: Wǒ bù xǐhuan yú means I do not like fish, with yú meaning fish. Do not use shì with xǐhuan; xǐhuan is itself the verb.

Wanting a thing uses xiǎng yào: Wǒ xiǎng yào yì bēi shuǐ means I want a cup of water. Wanting to do something uses xiǎng plus verb: Wǒ xiǎng chī fàn means I want to eat. Wǒ xiǎng qù Zhōngguó means I want to go to China. Wǒ xiǎng xué Hànyǔ means I want to learn Chinese. Xiǎng is softer than yào alone, which can sound like a demand. In a shop, a polite want is Wǒ xiǎng mǎi zhè gè, I want to buy this one. To ask what someone wants, say Nǐ xiǎng chī shénme means what do you want to eat.

Ability uses huì for learned skills and néng or kěyǐ for permission and possibility. Wǒ huì shuō Hànyǔ means I can speak Chinese, with shuō meaning to speak. Wǒ huì yóuyǒng means I can swim, with yóuyǒng meaning to swim. Wǒ bú huì means I cannot, for a skill. For permission, use kěyǐ: Wǒ kěyǐ jìnlai ma means may I come in, with jìnlai meaning to come in and ma making a question. Nǐ kěyǐ bāng wǒ ma means can you help me, with bāng meaning to help. Néng is used for ability in a general sense and for permission in some contexts, but kěyǐ is safer for permission at this level. Do not use huì for permission to enter a room; huì is for a skill you have learned.

Polite requests often use qǐng plus verb: Qǐng zuò means please sit, with zuò meaning to sit. Qǐng shuō màn yìdiǎnr means please speak a little more slowly, with màn meaning slow. Qǐng gěi wǒ yì bēi shuǐ means please give me a cup of water, with gěi meaning to give. To ask someone to show you something, say Qǐng gěi wǒ kànkan means please let me have a look, with kànkan as a repeated verb meaning to have a look. Repeating a verb in this way softens a request in Mandarin, and it is common in shops and classrooms. Shopping language includes: Duōshao qián means how much money, Tài guì le means too expensive, with tài meaning too and le marking a new situation, Piányi yìdiǎnr means a little cheaper, and Wǒ mǎi le means I will buy it or I bought it depending on context.

A common mistake is to use ài for every like; ài is stronger than xǐhuan, so use xǐhuan for ordinary likes and ài for love or a strong like. Another mistake is to use yào for a polite want in a shop when xiǎng is softer. A third mistake is to use huì for may I, when kěyǐ is the permission word. A fourth mistake is to answer a xǐhuan question with only shì; the natural answer repeats xǐhuan: Xǐhuan means yes, I like it, and Bù xǐhuan means no, I do not.

Practice exercise: Write a shop dialogue with ten lines, including one xǐhuan question and answer, one xiǎng yào sentence, two xiǎng plus verb sentences, one huì sentence, one kěyǐ question, and two qǐng requests. Then write five sentences about your own likes, wants, and abilities, checking each pattern: xǐhuan for like, xiǎng for want, huì for a learned skill, kěyǐ for permission. Read the dialogue aloud, taking both parts, and practise a polite, unhurried tone.

Lesson 8: Directions, Travel, and Putting It All Together

Travel language brings together numbers, places, directions, and polite requests, and it is a good final test of the course because you must use several patterns in one conversation. This lesson teaches directions, transport, and a review task that uses the whole course.

Direction words are: qián front, hòu back, zuǒ left, yòu right, shàng up or on, xià down or under, lǐ inside, wài outside, zhōngjiān middle, pángbiān beside. To give a location, say place plus direction word: Xuéxiào qiánmiàn means in front of the school, with qiánmiàn meaning front side. Shū zài zhuōzi shàng means the book is on the table, with zhuōzi meaning table and shàng meaning on. Māo zài zhuōzi xià means the cat is under the table. To ask where something is, say Zài nǎr, meaning where is it: Diànyǐngyuàn zài nǎr means where is the cinema. To ask how to get somewhere, say Qù diànyǐngyuàn zěnme zǒu means how do I walk to the cinema, with zěnme meaning how and zǒu meaning to walk.

Transport words include: chē car or vehicle in general, gōnggòng qìchē bus, dìtiě subway, huǒchē train, fēijī aeroplane, chuán boat, zìxíngchē bicycle. To say by a means of transport, use zuò plus the vehicle: Wǒ zuò dìtiě qù xuéxiào means I go to school by subway. Wǒ zuò fēijī qù Běijīng means I go to Beijing by plane, with Běijīng meaning Beijing. To say on foot, use zǒu lù: Wǒ zǒu lù qù shāngdiàn means I walk to the shop. To ask how long something takes, say Yào duō jiǔ means how long will it take, with yào meaning to need and duō jiǔ meaning how long. To ask how far, say Duō yuǎn means how far. Answers use fēnzhōng for minutes and xiǎoshí for hours: Yào sānshí fēnzhōng means it takes thirty minutes.

Travel sentences often combine several lessons: Wǒ míngtiān zuò huǒchē qù Shànghǎi means tomorrow I will go to Shanghai by train, with Shànghǎi meaning Shanghai. Wǒ xiǎng mǎi yì zhāng piào means I want to buy a ticket, with zhāng as the measure word for tickets and piào meaning ticket. Jǐ diǎn kāichē means what time does it leave, with kāichē meaning to depart for a vehicle. Diànyǐngyuàn zài nǎr, Wǒ zěnme qù means where is the cinema, how do I get there. Those sentences are not lists of words; they are full, usable questions, and they show why measure words, time words, and qù must be learned together.

Now put the course together. A good short self-introduction and travel paragraph at this level might be: Nǐ hǎo, wǒ jiào Anna. Wǒ shì xuésheng. Wǒ xué Hànyǔ. Wǒ xǐhuan kàn shū, wǒ xiǎng qù Zhōngguó. Wǒ míngtiān zuò huǒchē qù Běijīng. Diànyǐngyuàn zài nǎr, Wǒ zěnme qù. Xièxie, zàijiàn. That paragraph uses greetings, shì, xué, xǐhuan, xiǎng, time words, transport, and a direction question. It is not a test of every point; it is a model of how the pieces fit in real speech.

A common mistake is to use zài for going somewhere, as in Lesson 5, and the same error returns in travel: Wǒ zài qù Běijīng is wrong for I go to Beijing; use Wǒ qù Běijīng. Another mistake is to put the direction word before the place, as in English left of the school; in Mandarin the place comes first, then the direction, as in xuéxiào zuǒbiān, to the left of the school. A third mistake is to forget the measure word for a ticket, saying yí piào instead of yì zhāng piào. A fourth mistake is to ask for a place with only nǎr and no zài, when Zài nǎr is the fuller, clearer question.

Practice exercise, final review: Write a 150-word piece in Mandarin, in pinyin with characters where you know them, that includes: a greeting and introduction, a daily routine with three time words, a past or completed action with le or guo, a liking sentence, a want sentence, a comparison with bǐ, and a travel question with directions or transport. Then read it aloud, check every measure word, and correct any missing de, zài, qù, or le. As a second task, list ten verbs you now know, with pinyin, characters where you know them, and one example sentence each. That table is your revision card for the next stage of study. Keep studying in short, regular sessions, practise tones aloud every day, and add new vocabulary only when you can use it in a full sentence, not as an isolated word list.
