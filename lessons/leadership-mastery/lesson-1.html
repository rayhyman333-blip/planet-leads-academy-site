This lesson teaches the practical distinction between management and leadership, so you know which hat you are wearing in any given moment and spend your time on the right things. Management is about the system: plans, schedules, budgets, quality checks, and making sure the machinery of work runs. Leadership is about the people inside the system: direction, motivation, trust, and how they feel about the work they are doing. A schedule tells people what to do; a sense of purpose helps them do it well. Most new managers get promoted because they were excellent at the work itself, and then discover that their job has changed from doing tasks to creating the conditions in which other people do tasks. That transition is the whole subject of this course, and it starts here.

Consider a concrete scenario. Maya has just been promoted to lead a support team of six. Her first instinct is to jump into the ticket queue herself whenever it gets long — she is fast, and it feels productive. But every hour she spends closing tickets is an hour she does not spend on the things only she can do now: noticing that one agent is burning out, that another has never been shown the new escalation process, that the team has no shared sense of which tickets matter most. After a month, the queue is no shorter, and two agents are frustrated. Maya was managing the work but not leading the people. When she shifts — setting a weekly priority list, holding short one-to-ones, coaching the slower agents — the team's output improves without her touching a single ticket. The lesson: your leverage as a leader comes from multiplying other people's effectiveness, not from adding your own output.

Here is a useful mental model. Imagine your week has two kinds of tasks. System tasks are checklists, reports, approvals, schedules — the machinery. People tasks are conversations: setting expectations, giving feedback, asking what is blocked, resolving friction, recognizing good work. New leaders usually spend almost all their time on system tasks because those feel concrete and controllable. But the people tasks are where leadership actually happens, and they are the ones that get skipped when you are busy. A simple habit helps: at the start of each week, write down your three most important people tasks — the conversations you owe — and schedule them like meetings. If your calendar has no people tasks in it, you are managing a process, not leading a team.

A common mistake is the hero trap. The hero leader proves their worth by solving every crisis personally, which trains the team to wait for rescue instead of acting. The team becomes dependent, the leader becomes exhausted, and nothing improves structurally because the same crises keep recurring. If you notice people routing problems to you that they could handle themselves, ask: 'What would you do if I were on holiday this week?' Then let them do it, and review the result afterward. Your job is to build the team's judgment, not to be the team's judgment.

A second common mistake is confusing authority with leadership. A title gives you the right to make decisions, but it does not give you anyone's trust. Trust is earned through consistency: doing what you said you would do, admitting mistakes openly, and treating people's time as seriously as your own. People follow leaders they believe are competent, fair, and honest about bad news. If you inherited a team that does not trust you yet, do not demand respect — build a track record. Make one promise and keep it, every week, and the relationship changes faster than any speech.

Leadership also looks different from leading without a title. You can practice every skill in this course before anyone promotes you: clarify a goal the group was fuzzy about, ask the quiet person for their view, summarize a decision in writing. These small acts of leadership are visible, and they are often what earns someone the formal role later. Keep that in mind as you work through the lessons.

Reflection: Think about your current role. List five things you did last week at work. Mark each one as 'system task' or 'people task.' If fewer than two are people tasks, write down three conversations you owe your team or colleagues this week — a piece of feedback, a check-in with someone quiet, a clarification of priorities — and put the first one on your calendar today. In two sentences, describe what changed in your job the moment you started leading instead of just doing, and what you are still tempted to do yourself that the team should own.
