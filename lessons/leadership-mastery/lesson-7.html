This lesson teaches two of the hardest parts of leadership: handling difficult conversations — underperformance, behavioral problems, delivering bad news — and leading a team through change, when uncertainty is high and trust is tested. Both situations tempt leaders toward the same two failures: avoiding the conversation until the problem is severe, or handling it so bluntly that it damages the relationship. There is a middle path, and it is learnable.

Start with the structure of a difficult conversation. Preparation matters more than eloquence. Before the meeting, write down: the specific behavior or situation (with examples and dates), the impact it has on the team or the work, and the change you need to see with a clear timeframe. Then the conversation follows four moves. First, state the purpose plainly: 'I want to talk about your recent deadlines, because I want us to fix this together.' Second, describe what you have observed, using the Situation-Behavior-Impact pattern from Lesson 5 — facts, not character judgments. Third, listen. There may be context you do not have: a personal crisis, a blocked dependency, a misunderstanding of priorities. Ask, 'What's your view of what's happening?' and actually hear the answer. Fourth, agree on a specific plan with a follow-up date: 'Let's check in every Friday for the next month, and the goal is zero missed handoffs.' End by reaffirming your confidence that they can improve — people work harder for leaders who believe in them.

If the problem is behavioral — disrespect, undermining colleagues — the same structure applies, but the standard is non-negotiable and the timeline shorter. Be explicit that the behavior must stop, not merely improve. Document the conversation afterward in a brief factual note to yourself; if the pattern continues, you will need that record for formal steps, and memory is unreliable. And remember the lesson from Lesson 3: protecting a high performer with toxic behavior costs the whole team. Kindness to one person at the expense of five is not kindness.

Delivering bad news — a missed promotion, a budget cut, a reorganization — has its own rules. Tell people early, tell them directly, and tell them what you know and what you do not know. The worst approach is the slow leak: rumors fill every silence, and they are always worse than the truth. Say it plainly — 'The project is being cancelled' — then pause and let people react before you move to logistics. Do not over-explain or defend the decision in the first conversation; people need to absorb before they can discuss. And never deliver bad news by surrogate or by message when a conversation is possible.

Leading through change builds on all of this. Change — a reorg, a new system, a strategy shift — creates uncertainty, and uncertainty creates anxiety, speculation, and resistance. Your job is not to eliminate the uncertainty (you cannot) but to manage it. Three practices help. First, over-communicate the why: people accept difficult changes far more readily when they understand the reason, and leaders consistently underestimate how many times the why needs repeating. Second, name what is not changing. In a sea of uncertainty, stable anchors — 'your role, your manager, and your current projects are unchanged' — reduce panic. Third, create short feedback loops: weekly check-ins during a transition, where people can ask questions and you answer honestly, including 'I don't know yet, but I'll find out by Friday.'

A common mistake is confusing announcing with leading. Sending one email about a change and considering it handled is announcing. Leading means repeating the message, answering the same questions patiently for the fifth time, and watching for who is struggling quietly.

A second common mistake is promising certainty you do not have. 'Nobody's job is at risk' feels reassuring in the moment and destroys your credibility if it turns out wrong. Honest uncertainty — 'Here's what I know, here's what I don't, here's when I'll update you' — builds more trust than false comfort.

Practice: Think of one difficult conversation you have been postponing. Write your preparation: the specific observations (with examples), the impact, and the change you need with a timeframe. Draft your opening two sentences. Schedule the conversation within the next seven days. Then write a short paragraph: if your team faced a significant change next month, what would you tell them in the first meeting — the why, what is not changing, and how you will keep them updated?
