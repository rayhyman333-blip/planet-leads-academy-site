This lesson teaches the most leveraged leadership skill of all: feedback. Giving clear, timely, useful feedback — and receiving it gracefully — is what turns a group of individuals into a team that improves. Most leaders give too little feedback, too late, and too vaguely; and most receive it badly, which teaches everyone around them to stop offering it. This lesson fixes both sides with concrete scripts you can use this week.

Start with giving feedback. Good feedback has three properties: it is specific, it is timely, and it points to an action. Compare: 'Your presentation wasn't great' is none of those. 'In yesterday's client call, you skipped the budget slide and the client asked about pricing twice — next time, keep the budget slide even if we're running long, because pricing questions stall the deal' is all three. A simple structure helps, especially for critical feedback: Situation, Behavior, Impact. Situation anchors it in time ('In the Thursday review...'), Behavior describes what you observed without interpretation ('...you interrupted Lena three times...'), Impact explains the consequence ('...and she stopped contributing, so we lost her input on the timeline'). Then add the request: 'Next time, let people finish before responding.' Notice there is no label — no 'you're disrespectful' — only observable behavior and its effect. Labels trigger defensiveness; observations invite correction.

Positive feedback deserves the same specificity. 'Great job' feels nice and teaches nothing. 'The way you summarized the disagreement in the meeting got us unstuck in five minutes — that skill is valuable, keep using it' tells the person exactly what to repeat. A good rule: for every piece of critical feedback, aim to give several pieces of specific positive feedback. Not because criticism should be sugar-coated, but because a leader who only speaks up about problems becomes someone people avoid.

Timing matters enormously. Feedback decays fast: a comment about last Tuesday's meeting given three weeks later feels like an ambush, not help. Give critical feedback as soon as practical — ideally within a day or two — and always privately, never in front of the person's peers. Positive feedback, by contrast, can and should be public; recognizing someone in front of the team multiplies the effect. The one exception: if you are too angry to be fair, wait until you are calm. Feedback delivered in frustration becomes a venting session, and the message gets lost.

Now the harder side: receiving feedback without defensiveness. When someone criticizes your work, your brain hears a threat, and the reflex is to explain, justify, or counterattack. That reflex is exactly what trains people to stop giving you feedback — and a leader who gets no feedback flies blind. The discipline is simple: listen fully, thank them, and ask questions before you respond. 'Thank you for telling me. Can you give me an example?' buys you time and usually reveals that the feedback is more specific and more fixable than it first sounded. You do not have to agree with all of it; you do have to consider it. Later, decide what to act on. The person who gave you feedback does not need you to agree — they need to see that speaking up was safe.

A common mistake is the feedback sandwich: praise, criticism, praise. People see through it immediately, and it teaches them to dread praise because it signals incoming criticism. Give positive and critical feedback separately, each on its own merits.

A second common mistake is saving feedback for the annual review. By then it is a historical document, not a tool for improvement. Nothing in a performance review should surprise the person receiving it — if it does, the leader failed at feedback all year.

Practice: Identify one piece of specific positive feedback and one piece of constructive feedback you owe someone this week. Write each using the Situation-Behavior-Impact structure plus a clear request (for the constructive one) or a 'keep doing this' note (for the positive one). Deliver the positive one publicly if appropriate, and the constructive one privately within the next three days. Afterward, write two sentences: what did you say, and how did they react? Then ask one person you trust for feedback on your own leadership this month — listen, thank them, ask for an example — and note what you will do differently.
