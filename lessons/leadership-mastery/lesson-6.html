This lesson teaches delegation as a skill: how to hand work to someone else with clear expectations, real ownership, and just enough oversight. Poor delegation is one of the two great failure modes of new leaders (the other is poor feedback). Most people either dump tasks with no guidance and get angry at the result, or hover so closely that the other person learns nothing and the leader saves no time. Good delegation sits between those extremes, and it is a repeatable method you can learn.

Start with what to delegate and what not to. Delegate tasks that develop people, tasks you are not uniquely good at, and recurring work that someone else could own. Do not delegate the things that are genuinely yours: setting direction, hiring and firing decisions, performance conversations, and accountability for the team's results. A useful test: if doing the task yourself is the only way it gets done well, that is a training problem to fix, not a reason to keep the task forever. Many leaders cling to work they enjoy or work that made them successful before promotion. That work is now someone else's growth opportunity — and your calendar's biggest leak.

The delegation method has five steps. First, define the outcome, not the method. 'Produce a monthly client report that answers these three questions by the fifth of each month' beats 'spend two hours on the first Friday formatting the report.' People do better work when they own the how. Second, set the boundaries: budget, deadlines, what they can decide alone versus what needs your sign-off, and who else is involved. Third, agree on checkpoints: not surveillance, but scheduled moments to review progress — 'send me a draft outline by Wednesday so we can align early.' Early checkpoints catch misunderstandings when they are cheap to fix. Fourth, hand over the authority that matches the responsibility. Nothing kills delegation faster than making someone responsible for a result while withholding the tools, access, or decision rights to achieve it. Fifth, let go. Once you have delegated, the person's approach will differ from yours. Judge the result against the outcome you agreed on, not against the method you would have used.

Consider a scenario. Omar leads a small operations team and has always written the weekly status update himself. He delegates it to Priya: outcome ('a half-page update every Friday by 3pm covering wins, blockers, and next week's priorities'), boundaries ('keep it under 300 words, pull numbers from the shared dashboard'), checkpoint ('send me the first two drafts before you publish, then you're on your own'), authority ('you can ask anyone on the team for their numbers directly'). The first draft needs edits; the third is fine; by week six Priya writes it better than Omar did, and Omar has ninety minutes back every Friday. That is what good delegation compounds into.

A common mistake is reverse delegation: the person brings the problem back to you and you take it. 'This is stuck, can you handle it?' — and suddenly you are doing their task at 9pm. When someone brings you a problem, coach instead of taking: 'What options have you considered? What would you recommend?' If they are genuinely stuck, help them think, but the task stays theirs. Every time you accept a reversed delegation, you train the team that bringing you problems is more effective than solving them.

A second common mistake is delegating and disappearing. Handing off a complex task with no checkpoints and checking back at the deadline is not trust, it is abandonment — and the failure at the deadline is yours, because you set up no early warning. Match your checkpoint density to the person's experience and the task's risk: new person plus high-stakes task means frequent early check-ins; experienced person plus routine task means a light touch.

Practice: Pick one real task you currently do that someone else could own. Write a one-paragraph delegation brief: the outcome (what 'done' looks like), the boundaries (deadline, budget or limits, what needs your sign-off), two checkpoints with dates, and the authority you are handing over. Then have the conversation this week. After the first checkpoint, write down what surprised you — about their approach, and about your own urge to intervene. Keep the brief; you will reuse the format.
