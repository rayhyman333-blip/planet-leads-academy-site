This lesson teaches you to communicate as a leader: how to set expectations so clearly that people rarely have to guess, how to be direct without being harsh, and how to use written communication to prevent most misunderstandings. Almost every team problem — missed deadlines, duplicated work, resentment — traces back to something that was assumed but never said. Leadership communication is not about being eloquent; it is about removing ambiguity. When people know exactly what you expect, by when, and what 'done' looks like, they can focus on the work instead of decoding you.

Start with expectations, because vague expectations are the most common source of team friction. Compare these two requests. Vague: 'Can you look into the onboarding numbers?' Clear: 'Please pull the last six weeks of onboarding completion rates by Thursday at noon, and send me three bullet points on where the biggest drop-offs are. Here is the dashboard link.' The clear version names the deliverable, the deadline, the format, and the purpose. A reliable formula is: who does what, by when, what good looks like, and why it matters. Use it in meetings, emails, and one-to-ones. It feels overly formal at first, but teams find it a relief — nobody enjoys guessing what their manager really wanted.

Directness is the second pillar, and it is where many leaders fail by softening messages until the meaning disappears. 'This is fine, but maybe we could tweak a few things when you get a chance' is not feedback; it is a puzzle. Direct sounds like this: 'The report needs the error-rate chart added before Friday, because the client asked for it.' Notice what direct is not: it is not blunt for its own sake, it is not personal, and it is not delivered with an edge. The test of a direct message is that the other person can repeat back exactly what you asked for. If they cannot, you were not direct enough. Kindness enters through tone and respect for the person: you can be direct about the work while being warm toward the human. 'I know this week has been heavy — the deadline is still Friday, so let's figure out what we can move' is both clear and kind.

Written communication deserves special attention because teams run on it: emails, chat messages, project updates. Two rules prevent most damage. First, put decisions in writing. After any meeting where something was decided, send a short summary: what was decided, who owns what, and the deadlines. This takes five minutes and eliminates the 'I thought we agreed...' disputes that poison teams. Second, match the medium to the message: facts and decisions go in writing, sensitive or complicated topics go in conversation. Never give serious critical feedback or deliver bad news in a chat message; those need a voice or a face, where tone is visible.

A common mistake is over-communicating urgency and under-communicating purpose. Teams drowning in 'urgent' messages stop treating anything as urgent, and people who are told what to do but never why do the minimum. Reserve words like 'urgent' and 'as soon as possible' for genuine emergencies, and make a habit of adding one sentence of context: 'We're prioritizing this because the client renews next month.' Purpose is a motivator you get for free.

A second common mistake is the hallway decision: two people agree on something verbally and everyone else hears about it third-hand or not at all. If a decision affects the team, it belongs in the team's shared space within a day. You do not need bureaucracy — one message in the team channel will do — but invisible decisions breed suspicion even when the intent was innocent.

Practice: Take a real message you need to send this week — a request, a deadline reminder, or a decision announcement. Rewrite it using the formula: who does what, by when, what good looks like, and why it matters. Read it back and check: could the recipient repeat your exact expectation? Is the tone direct about the work and respectful toward the person? Then, after your next team meeting, write a three-bullet decision summary and send it to the group. Note how long it took (aim for under ten minutes) and whether it prevented any follow-up confusion that week.
