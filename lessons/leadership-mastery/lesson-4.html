This lesson teaches three decision frameworks that leaders use to make better calls faster: reversibility thinking, the 10-10-10 lens, and a disciplined pros-and-cons analysis. Leaders are judged by their decisions, and the two classic failures are opposite: agonizing for weeks over something trivial, or snapping off a hasty call on something consequential. The frameworks below fix both problems, because they start with the same question every time: what kind of decision is this?

The first framework is reversibility. Divide decisions into two types. Reversible decisions are one-way-door choices you can undo cheaply: trying a new meeting format, testing a different report layout, letting someone attempt a task a new way. These should be made quickly, ideally by the person closest to the work, because the cost of delay exceeds the cost of being wrong — you can just reverse it. Irreversible decisions are two-way-door choices that are expensive or impossible to undo: hiring, firing, signing a contract, changing a pricing model. These deserve deliberation, data, and often a second opinion. The common failure is treating reversible decisions as irreversible — a team that needs a week of meetings to change the agenda format — and irreversible decisions as reversible, rushing a hire because the calendar is full. When a decision lands on your desk, ask first: 'Can we undo this cheaply?' If yes, decide fast and move on.

The second framework is 10-10-10, a lens for decisions clouded by short-term emotion. Ask: how will I feel about this choice in ten minutes, in ten months, and in ten years? The ten-minute view captures the immediate pressure — the awkward conversation, the discomfort. The ten-month view captures the practical consequences. The ten-year view captures your values: what kind of leader do you want to have been? Consider a leader deciding whether to give honest critical feedback to a struggling team member. Ten minutes: uncomfortable, they might get upset. Ten months: the person has either improved or been moved to a role that fits, and the team is stronger. Ten years: you are someone who tells people the truth. The framework does not make the choice for you, but it stops the ten-minute discomfort from overruling the ten-year judgment — which is exactly what happens when leaders avoid hard calls.

The third framework is pros and cons done right. Most pros-and-cons lists fail because they are just two unlabeled columns of unequal items, compared by gut feel. A disciplined version has four steps. First, write the decision as a clear question: 'Should we move the weekly review from Friday to Monday?' Second, list pros and cons as specific consequences, not adjectives — 'Fridays, the data is final' beats 'better.' Third, weight the items: mark the two or three that matter most, because most lists are decided by one heavyweight item and a pile of feathers. Fourth, check for the hidden option: is there a third choice that keeps the main pro and drops the main con — for example, a short Monday standup plus the Friday review? Finally, set a decision deadline before you start analyzing. Analysis without a deadline is procrastination in a suit.

A common mistake is deciding alone what should be decided together, and vice versa. Not every decision needs consensus — asking the whole team about the report layout wastes everyone's time. But decisions that people must execute with commitment need their input, or you get compliance without energy. The rule: consult on decisions people will live with; decide solo on reversible operational details, and tell people clearly which kind it is. 'I've decided X, here's why' is fine for the second kind. 'Here's the problem, what am I missing?' is right for the first.

A second common mistake is never reviewing decisions. Teams that never look back repeat the same errors with confidence. Once a quarter, pick two significant decisions and ask: what did we expect, what actually happened, what would we do differently? This is not about blame; it is how a team's judgment compounds over time.

Practice: Take one real decision you are currently avoiding or overthinking. First, classify it: reversible or irreversible? If reversible, make the call today and note what you will watch to know whether to reverse it. If irreversible, run it through 10-10-10 and write your answers in three short paragraphs. Then do a disciplined pros-and-cons: clear question, specific consequences, weights, hidden third option, and a decision deadline you commit to in writing. Bring the result to your notebook — you will use a similar review in Lesson 8.
