This final lesson turns everything you have learned into a personal leadership plan: the habits you will practice, the goals you will pursue over the next ninety days, and how you will review your own progress. Courses end; leadership does not. Without a plan, the insights from Lessons 1 through 7 fade within weeks, crowded out by the urgency of daily work. With a plan — written down, reviewed regularly — they compound into a genuinely different way of leading.

Start by auditing where you are. Go back through your notebook from the earlier lessons and pull out your answers: your system-versus-people task split, your team dynamics map, your decision review, your feedback attempts, your delegation brief, your difficult-conversation preparation. For each of the seven skill areas, rate yourself honestly on a simple scale: solid, developing, or weak. Nobody is solid at all seven; the point is to see your pattern. Most new leaders find feedback and delegation at the bottom. Most experienced leaders find they have drifted — they used to give feedback regularly and stopped. Write one sentence for your two weakest areas describing what 'good' would look like for you specifically.

Next, convert weaknesses into habits, because goals without habits are wishes. A habit is small, specific, and scheduled: not 'communicate better' but 'send a three-bullet decision summary after every team meeting'; not 'delegate more' but 'delegate one task per week using the five-step brief.' Pick three habits, one for each of your weakest areas or the areas most relevant to your current role. Attach each habit to an existing trigger so it survives busy weeks: the decision summary goes out right after the meeting ends; Friday afternoon is your weekly people-task review; the first Monday of the month is your decision review. Leaders who tie habits to calendar triggers keep them; leaders who rely on remembering do not.

Now set your ninety-day goals: two or three outcomes you want to be true three months from now. Make them concrete and checkable. 'Improve team communication' is not checkable. 'Every team member has had a one-to-one with me at least twice a month, and meeting decisions are summarized in writing within a day' is. 'Get better at feedback' is not checkable. 'I have given specific positive feedback to each team member and addressed the two performance issues I've been avoiding' is. Write each goal with its measure: how will you know, on day ninety, whether it happened? Then work backward: what must be true at day thirty and day sixty? Those midpoints are your early warnings.

Build the review rhythm into the plan now, while your motivation is high. Schedule three appointments with yourself: a thirty-minute review at day thirty, day sixty, and day ninety. In each review, ask four questions. What did I actually do — which habits stuck, which slipped? What changed in the team — quieter signals, faster decisions, fewer surprises? What did I learn about my own patterns — where do I still avoid, rush, or over-control? What will I adjust for the next thirty days? Write the answers down. This review is the difference between a plan you wrote once and a practice you actually develop.

A common mistake is planning in isolation and never telling anyone. A plan nobody knows about is easy to abandon. Tell at least one person — a peer, a mentor, your own manager — what you are working on. Better yet, tell your team something appropriate: 'I'm working on giving clearer feedback this quarter — tell me if I'm not being specific enough.' That single sentence makes you accountable and, not incidentally, models exactly the openness you want from them.

A second common mistake is trying to fix everything at once. Three habits and three goals is the maximum; one habit done consistently beats five attempted sporadically. Leadership development is a years-long practice, and this ninety-day plan is just the first cycle. When it ends, run the review, keep what worked, and write the next one.

Practice: Draft your full plan now, in your notebook. Section one: your seven-area self-audit with ratings. Section two: your three habits, each with its calendar trigger. Section three: your two or three ninety-day goals, each with a day-ninety measure and day-thirty and day-sixty midpoints. Section four: your three review dates, scheduled in your actual calendar. Finally, write the name of one person you will tell about this plan this week, and the one sentence you will say. Keep this document — revisit it at each review, and start your next ninety-day cycle when this one ends.
