This lesson teaches you single-note playing through the minor pentatonic scale - the five-note scale behind countless melodies and solos in rock, blues, and folk music. Until now every note you played was part of a chord; now your fingers learn to play one note at a time with precision and even timing. This is also your introduction to playing up the neck, away from the open chords you know.

The shape. We will use the A minor pentatonic in fifth position, which means your index finger lives around the fifth fret. On each string you play two notes; the pattern across the six strings (low to high) is: 5th-8th frets, 5th-7th, 5th-7th, 5th-7th, 5th-8th, 5th-8th. The fingering rule for single-note playing: one finger per fret - index covers the 5th fret, middle the 6th, ring the 7th, little finger the 8th. So on the low E string you play 5 (index) then 8 (little finger); on the A string, 5 (index) then 7 (ring); and so on up across all six strings, then back down. Play it ascending and descending slowly, saying each fret number out loud at first.

Picking. Use alternate picking: downstroke, upstroke, downstroke, upstroke, one pick stroke per note, strictly alternating. It feels awkward at first and then becomes automatic - and it is the technique that makes fast, even single-note lines possible later. Steady your picking hand lightly: rest the heel of your palm near the bridge or let your pinky brush the pickguard, whichever feels stable, but keep the wrist loose and the motion small. Each note should sound for its full value with no accidental string noise - if neighboring strings ring, mute them by resting unused fretting fingers lightly against them.

Making music with it. A scale is a tool, not a song - so here is your first improvisation exercise. Set a slow metronome pulse, or simply count a steady beat, and play any notes from the scale in any order, four notes per beat-group, for two minutes. There are no wrong notes inside this scale over an A minor or C major backing - everything you play will sound intentional. Try repeating small groups of notes (motifs), leaving gaps of silence, and ending phrases on the 5th fret of the high E string (the note A, home base). This is how solos are born: not from speed, but from simple ideas played with feeling.

Two common mistakes. The first is uneven timing: the stretch from the 5th to the 8th fret takes longer than the step from the 5th to the 7th, so the rhythm lurches. Slow down until every note lands exactly on its beat, and give the wide stretches extra isolated repetitions. The second is collapsing finger joints: pressing with flat, buckled fingers instead of curved fingertips kills both speed and tone. Watch your fretting hand in a mirror - the fingers should arch over the strings like a small bridge.

Time for drills. First, the slow climb: play the full scale ascending and descending, alternate picking, at a slow metronome tempo, six times through. Second, the stretch isolation: on the low E string only, play 5-8-5-8 twenty times, then the high E string the same, training the wide index-to-little-finger stretch. Third, the two-minute improvisation: play freely within the scale over a steady pulse, focusing on simple repeating motifs rather than speed.

Practice: Play the scale ascending and descending six times at a slow tempo with strict alternate picking. Then improvise within the scale for three minutes over a steady beat, using at least three repeating motifs and ending each phrase on the A note (5th fret, high E string). Finish with the stretch isolation drill, twenty repetitions per string. As a final test, play the scale ascending only, then immediately improvise for one minute without stopping. Switching between the disciplined scale and free playing without pausing is exactly what real soloing feels like.
