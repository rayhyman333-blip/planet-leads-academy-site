This lesson teaches you strumming: how to move your right hand across the strings in steady rhythmic patterns. Chords give you the notes; strumming gives you the groove. A great strum pattern can make two simple chords sound like a real song, while stiff, arrhythmic strumming makes even fancy chords sound amateur. By the end of this lesson you will play three core strumming patterns in time.

The mechanics. Hold the pick between your thumb and the side of your index finger, with only a small tip of the pick sticking out - gripping too much pick makes the strum clumsy and loud. Keep your wrist loose: the strumming motion comes from the wrist (with a little forearm), not from the elbow swinging like a pendulum and not from stiff fingers. Strum downstrokes with a relaxed downward brush across the strings, and upstrokes with a light upward flick - upstrokes should be quieter and touch fewer strings, mostly the top three or four. Rest your forearm lightly on the guitar body and let the wrist do the work; if your shoulder gets involved, you are using too much arm.

The patterns, counted in 4/4 time. Pattern one, the foundation: four down-strums, one per beat - counted "1, 2, 3, 4." Play it on E minor until it is metronome-steady. Pattern two: down, down-up, down, down-up - counted "1, 2-and, 3, 4-and." The "and" is the upstroke falling between beats. Pattern three: continuous down-up strumming on every eighth note - down-up-down-up-down-up-down-up, counted "1-and-2-and-3-and-4-and." Learn the patterns in order, and do not move to the next until the current one stays steady for two full minutes.

Timing is everything. Practice with a metronome at a slow tempo - slow enough that you never rush. Play pattern one on E minor for two full minutes without stopping, then switch to A major for two minutes. Then pattern two, same deal. The goal is not speed but unbroken steadiness: if you stumble, do not stop and restart - keep the right hand moving and rejoin on the next beat, because real playing never gets a restart button. Count out loud at first ("1-and-2-and..."), then let the counting go internal once it is solid.

Once a pattern is steady, add accents - slightly emphasizing certain beats. In pattern one, accent beat one: play the first down-strum of each measure a touch louder and the other three lighter, counted "ONE, 2, 3, 4." This single change makes the rhythm feel alive instead of mechanical. Try the same with pattern two, accenting beats one and three. Then practice dynamic control: play pattern three very softly for four measures, then loudly for four, then softly again - keeping the tempo identical throughout. Beginners often speed up when playing loud and drag when playing soft; holding the tempo steady across volume changes is an advanced skill disguised as a simple exercise.

Two common mistakes. The first is a stiff wrist: beginners lock the wrist and strum from the elbow, producing a harsh, uneven sound and a tired arm. Shake your hand loose, slow down, and strum as if brushing dust off the strings. The second is strumming too hard: digging the pick deep between the strings catches and jerks the rhythm. The pick should skim the surface - you want a light, even brush, with the upstrokes noticeably lighter than the downstrokes.

Time for drills. First, the two-minute steadiness drill: pattern one on E minor, metronome slow, two minutes unbroken. Second, the pattern ladder: play pattern one for four measures, pattern two for four, pattern three for four, then back down, all on one chord, three times through. Third, the chord-change strum: play pattern two, changing from E minor to A major every four measures, for five minutes - this merges your Lesson 2 chord changes with your new strumming.

Practice: Set the metronome slow. Play pattern two on E minor for two minutes, then A major for two minutes, counting aloud. Then play the pattern ladder (patterns 1-2-3-2-1, four measures each) twice through on E minor. Finish with five minutes of pattern two while switching chords every four measures.
