This lesson teaches you the five essential open chords: G, C, D, E minor, and A minor. With these five shapes you can play thousands of songs in the keys of G, C, D, and A minor - this is the working vocabulary of the rhythm guitarist. You already know E minor; now you add the other four and start changing between all of them.

The shapes, one at a time, with the clarity check after each. G major: low E 3rd fret with finger 2, A string 2nd fret with finger 1, high E 3rd fret with finger 3, and D, G, B open - strum all six strings. C major: low E is not played (X), A string 3rd fret finger 3, D string 2nd fret finger 2, B string 1st fret finger 1, and G and high E open - strum five strings, skipping the low E. D major: low E and A are not played (X), D open, G string 2nd fret finger 1, B string 3rd fret finger 3, high E 2nd fret finger 2 - strum the four thinnest strings. A minor: low E is not played (X), A open, D string 2nd fret finger 2, G string 2nd fret finger 3, B string 1st fret finger 1, high E open - strum five strings.

Learn them in this order: Em (already known), Am, C, G, D. For each new chord: study the diagram, place the fingers one at a time, run the clarity check by plucking each string individually, adjust anything that buzzes or mutes, and then strum. Spend at least five minutes per chord before moving on. Say the chord name and its finger numbers out loud as you place them - naming wires the shape into memory faster than silent repetition.

Now the changes. The one-minute change drill is the fastest known way to build switching speed: pick two chords, set a timer for one minute, and count how many clean changes you can make, strumming once after each change to verify. Only clean changes count. Do Em to Am, then Am to C, then C to G, then G to D, then D to Em. Write down each score. Repeat the whole circuit daily and watch the numbers climb - when every pair is above thirty clean changes per minute, your hands know the shapes.

Speed up your changes with the hover technique. When you lift your fingers to change chords, do not pull them far off the strings - instead, let them hover just above the strings in roughly the shape of the next chord, as if the shape were a shadow waiting to land. Practice this in slow motion: form G, then lift all fingers only a centimeter, reshape them mid-air into the C shape, and set them down together. Do this shape-in-the-air move ten times between each chord pair before you start the one-minute drill. Changes built this way stay fast even under pressure, because the fingers never travel farther than they have to.

Two common mistakes. The first is the flying-fingers habit: when changing chords, beginners lift all fingers high off the neck and rebuild the shape from scratch. Keep your fingers close to the strings during changes - the less they travel, the faster the change. Look for pivot fingers: positions that barely move between two chords should glide, not lift. The second is the death grip: squeezing harder during changes because the new shape feels insecure. Tension slows you down - if your forearm burns, loosen up and slow the drill.

Time for drills. First, the circuit: one-minute changes for each of the five pairs above, recording your scores. Second, the progression loop: play G-C-D-Em, one strum per chord, slowly, ten times through - this is a real four-chord song progression. Third, the blind change: form each chord, close your eyes, lift your fingers one inch, and reform the shape by feel, five times per chord.

Practice: Run the full one-minute-change circuit (all five pairs) and record your scores. Then play the G-C-D-Em loop with pattern two strumming from Lesson 3, four beats per chord, for five minutes. Finish with the blind-change drill on your two weakest chords.
