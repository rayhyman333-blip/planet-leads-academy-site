Full lesson text, Academy-built. This is the complete 8-lesson course text for this course. Prices and course links are on the course page.

Validate an idea, build the minimum viable product, and win your first paying customers.

Lesson 1: Idea Validation — Finding a Painful Problem

This lesson teaches you how to find out whether your business idea solves a problem painful enough that strangers will pay to make it go away — before you spend money building anything. Most new businesses fail quietly for one reason: the founder built something nobody wanted badly enough. Validation is the discipline of testing demand with conversations instead of assumptions, and it is the highest-leverage skill in this entire course.

Start by writing your idea as a problem statement, not a product description. Bad: 'An app for dog owners.' Good: 'Busy dog owners feel guilty leaving dogs alone all day and will pay for reliable midday check-ins.' The formula is: a specific person has a specific painful problem and currently copes with it in a specific, observable way. The coping behavior matters — if people have no workaround at all, the pain may not be real; if they pay for an awkward workaround today, that is strong evidence. Write three problem statements for your idea now, each naming a different specific person.

Next, run customer interviews — real conversations with 10 to 15 people who match your specific person. These are not surveys and not pitches; you are a detective, not a salesperson. The five core questions: 1) 'Tell me about the last time you dealt with this problem — what happened?' 2) 'What have you tried to fix it?' 3) 'What did you like or dislike about those attempts?' 4) 'How much time or money does this problem cost you?' 5) 'If this problem disappeared tomorrow, what would change for you?' Then stop talking and listen. Take notes on their exact words — customers' phrasing becomes your future marketing copy. A full interview question worksheet is in the materials section; print it and use it verbatim for your first five interviews.

Where do you find interviewees? Start with your own network and ask for introductions: 'Do you know anyone who struggles with this problem? I'd love a 20-minute chat — I'm not selling anything.' Then go where the problem lives: relevant online communities, local groups, comment sections, and in-person spots. Offer nothing but gratitude and a summary of what you learn. Aim for 10 to 15 conversations; patterns usually emerge by interview 7 or 8. You are looking for consistent, specific pain described in emotional language ('it drives me crazy', 'I dread it every week'), plus evidence of money or serious effort already spent on workarounds.

Two beginner mistakes will wreck your validation. First, pitching instead of listening: the moment you describe your solution, the interviewee starts being polite instead of honest. Rule: do not mention your idea until the last five minutes, if at all. Second, asking leading questions like 'Would you pay for something that fixed this?' — everyone says yes to hypotheticals and it means nothing. Instead ask about past behavior: 'Have you ever paid for anything to solve this? What did you pay?' Past spending predicts future spending; opinions do not.

How do you know you are validated? Look for three signals together: at least half your interviewees describe the problem unprompted in strong language; several have already paid for an imperfect solution; and when you finally describe your idea in one sentence, people ask 'when can I try it?' or 'how much?' instead of 'oh, interesting.' If you do not see these signals, that is a successful validation

too — it just saved you months of building the wrong thing. Adjust the specific person, the problem, or both, and interview again. Pivoting now is cheap; pivoting after launch is expensive.

Practice: Write three problem statements using the formula above. List 20 specific people you could interview (names, not 'dog owners' — actual humans). Send five outreach messages today using the script in this lesson. Conduct your first three interviews this week with the printed worksheet, and write a one-page summary: the three most common pains in their exact words, what they have paid for before, and your honest verdict — strong signal, weak signal, or pivot.

Lesson 2: Business Models — How the Money Actually Flows

This lesson teaches you to choose a business model — the mechanism by which your business actually gets paid — and to map the money flow before you build anything. Beginners obsess over the product and treat the business model as an afterthought; experienced founders know the model often matters more than the product. A brilliant product with a broken money flow still fails, while an ordinary product with a clean, repeatable money flow can thrive.

Here are the core models, each with how money flows. 1) Services (done-for-you): a customer pays you per project or per hour — e.g., a bookkeeper charges a monthly fee per client. Money flow: you sell time and skill directly; cash arrives fast but stops when you stop. 2) Product sales: you sell a physical or digital item per unit — e.g., a printable planner sold per download. Money flow: you build once, sell many times; each sale needs a buyer. 3) Subscription/recurring: customers pay monthly for ongoing value — e.g., a meal-planning membership. Money flow: slower start, compounding revenue as members stack. 4) Marketplace or commission: you connect buyers and sellers and take a cut — hardest for beginners, skip it for now. 5) Education/coaching: you sell knowledge as courses, workshops, or 1-on-1 help — e.g., an 8-week course. Money flow: high margin, but you must prove the outcome first.

Worked example — compare two models for the same skill. Maya is good at organizing. Option A (services): home-organization sessions at a per-session fee. Ten sessions a month at her price = her monthly revenue; to grow she raises prices or works more hours. Option B (digital product): a room-by-room decluttering guide sold per download. She builds it once (40 hours), then each sale is nearly pure margin, but she must find every buyer through marketing. Neither is 'better' — services give fast cash and direct customer learning (great for Lesson 1 validation), products give leverage but need an audience. Many founders start with services to learn what customers pay for, then productize the most repeatable part.

Choose using three filters. Filter 1 — cash speed: how fast does the first dollar arrive? Services and simple products win; subscriptions and marketplaces are slow. Filter 2 — your unfair edge: which model uses what you already have (skills, audience, access)? Filter 3 — repeatability: can one unit of work serve many customers, or does every dollar cost you an hour? Write each candidate model in one line naming your offer, your customer, your price, your unit (per hour, per month, per download), and your delivery channel. If you cannot name all five concretely, the model is not ready — go back to interviews.

Two common mistakes. First, the 'free now, figure out money later' trap: attention without a payment mechanism is a hobby, not a business. Decide how money flows before you build an audience. Second, copying a model that needs scale: a commission marketplace or ad-supported model needs thousands of users to work — a beginner with zero audience should pick a model where ten customers is a real business (services, coaching, simple products). Ten paying customers at a fair price is a business; ten thousand free users is a to-do list.

Practice: Write your idea in the one-line model format for three different models (e.g., service version, product version, subscription version). Score each 1–5 on cash speed, your unfair edge, and repeatability. Pick a winner and write one paragraph defending the choice against the other two. Then pressure-test it: list exactly how customer #1 finds you, pays you, and receives value — if any step is

vague, that vagueness is your next task.

Lesson 3: Building the Minimum Viable Product — Lean and Low-Cost

This lesson teaches you to build a minimum viable product (MVP): the smallest version of your offer that a real customer can pay for. The MVP is not a rough draft of your dream product — it is a deliberate test: what is the least I can build that still delivers the core value and proves someone will pay? Every feature beyond that is a guess you have not earned yet. Founders who skip this step spend months polishing features no customer asked for; founders who embrace it get paying feedback in weeks.

Start by isolating the core value. Ask: if my product could do only ONE thing, what would make a customer say 'that was worth paying for'? A meal-planning service's core value is 'I know what to cook this week' — not the app, not the branding, not the recipe photos. Write your core value in one sentence, then list every feature you imagined and sort them into 'core' vs. 'later'. Be ruthless: most lists end up 10% core, 90% later. Your MVP contains only the core, delivered in the simplest possible form.

Choose the cheapest delivery method that still feels real. The MVP ladder, cheapest first: 1) Done manually (concierge MVP): deliver the service by hand behind the scenes — e.g., personal meal plans written in a document and emailed weekly. 2) No-code assembly: combine free or cheap tools — a simple website builder, a payment link, a scheduling tool, a shared document. 3) Single-feature build: one simple tool or template that does the core thing, nothing else. Worked example: Lena validated that busy parents want weeknight dinner plans. Her MVP: a $15/month email with five recipes and one grocery list, written in a document, paid via a payment link, delivered every Sunday. Total build cost: zero beyond her time. Ten subscribers = proof; then she earns the right to build more.

Set a hard budget and deadline: the MVP should take days or weeks, not months, and cost little or nothing beyond your time. Write a one-page MVP spec before building: the core value sentence, exactly what the customer gets (format, frequency, delivery method), what is explicitly NOT included, the price (from Lesson 4's method, or a starting guess you will test), and your launch date no more than 14 days out. If the spec does not fit one page, it is not minimal — cut again. A useful test: could you explain the MVP to a customer in 30 seconds and have them understand what they get? If not, simplify.

Two beginner mistakes. First, building in secret: hiding the MVP until it is 'ready' guarantees you build the wrong thing confidently. Show rough versions to interviewees from Lesson 1 within days and watch them use it — their confusion is your specification. Second, gold-plating: adding a logo, a fancy website, or extra features before one stranger has paid. Polish does not create demand; it decorates demand that already exists. Get the first paying customer with the ugly version, then polish what they actually use.

Consider smoke tests when even a manual MVP feels heavy. A smoke test measures demand before you build anything deliverable: a simple one-page description of the offer with a payment link or a 'join the waitlist' button, shown to fifty real prospects. If nobody clicks, you saved weeks; if ten people try to pay, you build with confidence. Another lean option is the single-result MVP: instead of a full service, sell one narrow outcome — not 'a fitness program' but 'a two-week kickstart plan delivered by email.' Narrow offers are faster to build, easier to price, and simpler to explain, and you can widen them after the first customers are happy. Whatever form you choose, write down the one question your MVP must answer ('will strangers pay for this core value?') and judge the MVP only on that question — everything

else is noise until the answer is yes.

Practice: Write your one-page MVP spec (core value, deliverable, format, frequency, delivery method, exclusions, price guess, 14-day launch date). List every tool you need and its cost — keep the total at or near zero. Then build the smallest testable piece TODAY: the payment link, the one-page description, or the first manual delivery. Message three interviewees from Lesson 1: 'I built the smallest version — can I show you this week?' Their reaction is your first real market data.

Lesson 4: Pricing — How to Set Prices with Confidence

This lesson teaches you to set prices using math and testing instead of fear and guessing. Pricing is where beginners leave the most money on the table: they price too low from insecurity, or copy a competitor without understanding the costs behind the number. A good price covers your costs, reflects the value delivered, and can be tested and raised — pricing is a decision you revisit, not a tattoo.

Start with the cost floor: the lowest price that does not lose money. List every cost per unit or per month: materials, tool subscriptions, payment processing fees, packaging, shipping, and your time valued honestly (hours × a reasonable hourly figure). Worked example: Sam sells a digital home-workout guide. Monthly tool costs: $12. Payment fees: about 3% per sale. His time: 20 hours to create it, which he values at $25/hour = $500 one-time. If he expects 50 sales in year one, his per-unit cost is roughly ($500 + $144 annual tools) ÷ 50 = about $13 per sale, plus 3% fees. His absolute floor is ~$14 — any price below that loses money at that volume. Your turn: compute your floor on paper before reading further. Most beginners discover their 'cheap' price was actually below cost.

Next, find the value ceiling: what is the problem worth to the customer? Return to your interview notes — what did people say the problem costs them in money, time, or stress? A bookkeeping service that saves a freelancer 5 hours a month is worth a fraction of those 5 hours' earnings to that freelancer. Price between your floor and their perceived value, and anchor with three tiers when it fits: a basic option, a standard option (your target — most buyers choose the middle), and a premium option. Tiers let customers self-select and raise your average sale without pressure tactics.

Then test, because no formula replaces market contact. Method 1 — the price ladder: quote your starting price to the next 5 prospects; if all 5 buy instantly with no hesitation, raise it 20% for the next 5. Repeat until you hear occasional 'that's more than I expected' — that resistance marks your current ceiling. Method 2 — A/B the offer page: run one price for two weeks, another for two weeks, compare conversion (visitors who buy). Change only the price between tests. Method 3 — ask lost prospects: 'Was price the reason? What would have felt fair?' One honest answer beats ten guesses.

Two pricing mistakes to avoid. First, racing to the bottom: competing on 'cheapest' attracts the most demanding customers with the least loyalty, and there is always someone willing to earn less than you. Compete on a specific outcome or experience instead. Second, apologetic pricing: discounting before anyone objects, or justifying the price unprompted. State the price plainly, then be quiet — the silence is where the customer decides, and filling it with discounts trains people to wait for them.

A few practical pricing principles that compound over time. First, never compete on being the cheapest unless your costs truly are the lowest — instead, be the clearest about the outcome: 'dinner handled five nights a week' beats 'cheap meal plans' because it names the relief. Second, raise prices for new customers before raising them for existing ones; early buyers who took a chance on you deserve grandfathered pricing, and new buyers only ever know the current price. Third, review prices quarterly using your Lesson 7 numbers: if your waitlist is growing and your time is full, the price is too low — that is the market telling you to charge more. Finally, keep a simple discount policy in writing (for example: no discounts except an annual-plan prepay option) so you never negotiate against yourself in the moment. Consistent pricing builds trust; random discounts train customers to haggle.

Practice: Complete the pricing worksheet in the materials section for your offer: cost floor calculation, value ceiling from interview notes, and a three-tier structure. Write your one-sentence price statement

in this shape: my offer costs a stated price because it delivers a stated outcome. Then test it this week: quote it to five real prospects using the price ladder method and record each reaction verbatim — buy, hesitate, or object — plus the objection in their words.

Lesson 5: Finding Your First Ten Customers

This lesson teaches you to win your first ten customers by hand — through direct outreach, not advertising. Your first ten customers are special: they teach you what to build, give you honest feedback you can learn from, and prove the business works before you spend a dollar on marketing. Founders who chase scale before ten hand-won customers usually discover — expensively — that their message does not convert.

Start with a list of 100 potential customers. Not 'small businesses' — 100 named humans or companies with a contact. Sources: your interviewees from Lesson 1 (warmest), their referrals ('who else has this problem?'), online communities where the problem is discussed, local businesses you can walk into, and directories or groups in your niche. A spreadsheet with name, contact, source, date contacted, and status is your CRM for now — the template habit starts here and scales later.

Outreach that gets replies follows a simple structure. The message: one line showing you understand their specific situation, one line on what you offer, one low-pressure ask. Example: 'Hi Dana — saw your post about spending Sundays meal planning for your family of five. I write a weekly plan (5 dinners + one grocery list) for busy parents; it's $15/month. Would you like a free first week to see if it fits?' Key principles: personalize the first line (never mass-blast), name the price (hiding it wastes everyone's time), and make the ask small (a trial, a 15-minute call, a sample — not 'buy now'). Send 10 per day; expect roughly 2–3 replies and aim to convert 1 in 10 contacts into a customer at first. Those ratios improve as your message sharpens.

Follow-up is where most sales happen — and where beginners quit. Most buyers need 3–5 touches: the first message, a value-add follow-up ('thought of you when I wrote this week's plan — here's a free sample recipe'), a direct check-in, and a final polite close ('Should I close your file on this?'). Space touches 3–4 days apart, always add something useful, and never guilt-trip. Track every touch in your spreadsheet; 'follow up Thursday' is a task, 'they seemed interested' is a feeling. When someone says no, ask one question: 'Is it the timing, the price, or the fit?' — the answer improves your offer.

Two mistakes that kill early sales. First, waiting to 'feel ready' or to finish the website: your first customers buy the outcome and your personal attention, not your branding. Sell the concierge version this week. Second, taking silence personally and stopping outreach: silence is data about your message or targeting, not your worth. If 30 personalized messages get zero replies, change the first line and the offer framing — then send 30 more. Volume plus iteration beats inspiration.

Worked example — Ana's first ten: Ana sells resume reviews for $49. Day 1–2: lists 100 recent graduates from her college's alumni group. Day 3–7: sends 10 personalized messages daily offering a free 10-minute review of one resume section. Results: 68 messages sent, 19 replies, 11 free reviews delivered, 7 paid customers in week one. Each free review taught her the three most common resume mistakes — which became her sales page. Week two: referrals from the first 7 brought 3 more. Ten customers, zero ad spend, and a message proven to convert before she spent anything on marketing.

Practice: Build your list of 100 (names and contacts, in a spreadsheet with the five columns). Write your outreach message using the three-part structure and personalize it for five specific people. Send 10 messages today, schedule follow-ups for each, and log every reply and objection. Your target: first paying customer within 7 days of starting outreach.

Lesson 6: A Simple Social Media System for a New Business

This lesson teaches you a social media routine you can actually sustain: 30–45 minutes a day, built on content that teaches and proves rather than content that begs for attention. Social media is a slow compounder, not a lottery ticket — this course makes no promises about followers or virality, because those depend on factors outside any system. What the system does guarantee is consistency: a year of steady, useful posts builds an audience asset that outreach alone never will.

Pick ONE platform where your customers already spend time — not the trendy one, the one your interviewees mentioned. A resume coach belongs where job seekers gather; a meal-planning service belongs where busy parents scroll. One platform done well beats three done badly; you can expand after 90 days. Set up a simple profile: a clear photo or logo, one line saying who you help and how ('Weekly dinner plans for busy parents'), and one link to your offer or trial.

Run four content pillars in rotation so you never stare at a blank screen. 1) Teach: one useful tip from your expertise ('The 3-ingredient rule for 20-minute dinners'). 2) Prove: show your work and results — before/after, a customer story (with permission), your process on camera or in screenshots. 3) Relate: your story and opinions — why you started, a mistake you made, what you believe about your niche. 4) Invite: the offer — trials, customer stories (shared with permission), limited spots, direct calls to message you. The ratio: roughly 4 teach/prove/relate posts for every 1 invite post. Nobody follows an account that only sells; everybody respects one that teaches generously and sells occasionally.

The weekly routine (about 4 hours total). Monday: write 5 posts in one 60-minute batch (one per pillar plus one extra teach). Tuesday–Saturday: publish one daily and spend 20 minutes engaging — answer every comment, comment thoughtfully on 5 accounts in your niche, reply to every direct message. Sunday: 30-minute review — which post got the most saves, shares, or replies? Do more of that next week; drop what flopped twice. Batching beats daily inspiration, and engagement beats posting into the void — platforms reward conversations, so be conversational.

Two mistakes to avoid. First, perfectionism: spending two hours on one post's design while posting twice a month. Frequency with honest, useful content beats rare polished content — your 50th post will be better than your 5th because you made the first 49. Second, vanity metrics: chasing follower counts while ignoring the only metrics that matter at this stage — profile visits, direct messages, and trial signups. A hundred followers with five customers beats ten thousand followers with zero; track business outcomes weekly.

Make engagement do the heavy lifting with a simple daily outreach habit alongside posting. Each day, send five genuine direct messages to people who engaged with your niche: answer a question they asked publicly, share a tip relevant to their post, or invite them to your free trial when it truly fits. This is not spam — it is being helpful in public and continuing the conversation in private, and it is how small accounts grow without ad spend. Also repurpose everything: one teaching post becomes a short video script, three tips become one carousel, and your five best posts become a pinned 'start here' collection. Repurposing multiplies each hour of writing into a week of presence. And protect the routine: schedule your content batch like an appointment, because 'post when inspired' quietly becomes 'never post.' Thirty focused minutes a day for ninety days will teach you more about your market than any course about social media ever could.

Practice: Choose your one platform and rewrite your profile using the formula. Write next week's five posts now (one per pillar + one teach), each under 150 words with one clear takeaway. Publish the first today, then do 20 minutes of genuine engagement. Set up a simple tracker: date, post topic, pillar, saves/shares/replies, profile visits, DMs, trial signups — review it every Sunday for four weeks and write one line on what to change.

Lesson 7: Money Basics — Costs, Break-Even, and Tracking

This lesson teaches you the money basics every founder needs: knowing your costs, calculating your break-even point, and tracking your numbers on one page. You do not need accounting software or a finance background — you need a clear-eyed list of what goes out, what comes in, and the exact number of sales that makes the business sustain itself. Founders who track money monthly make calm decisions; founders who avoid the numbers make anxious ones.

Start by listing all costs in two buckets. Fixed costs happen every month whether you sell or not: tool subscriptions, website hosting, insurance, a phone plan used for business. Variable costs happen per sale: materials, packaging, shipping, payment processing fees (roughly 3% per transaction), and any per-customer labor. Worked example: Priya sells handmade soap gift sets online. Fixed: $29/month website + $15/month email tool = $44. Variable per set: $6 materials + $3 packaging + $5 shipping + 3% fees on a $32 price (~$1) = $15. Her contribution per sale (price minus variable cost) is $32 − $15 = $17. That $17 is the number that matters — it is what each sale contributes toward covering fixed costs and then toward earnings.

Now the break-even point: fixed costs ÷ contribution per sale. Priya: $44 ÷ $17 = 2.6, so 3 sets per month covers all costs; sale #4 onward contributes to earnings. This single calculation answers the most important question in early business: 'How many do I need to sell to not lose money?' Compute yours now with real numbers — not hopeful ones. Then set two targets above it: your 'sustain' target (covers costs + pays you a modest amount for your time) and your 'grow' target (sustain + reinvestment). Write all three numbers on one line and pin it where you work.

Track with a one-page monthly sheet — no fancy tools needed. Columns: date, description, money in, money out, running balance. Once a month (pick a fixed date, like the 1st), fill in five summary numbers: total revenue, total costs, net (revenue − costs), units sold, and cash in the business account. Compare against your three targets. The discipline is the product: 20 minutes a month of honest numbers beats any dashboard you never open. Keep business money in a separate account or at least a separate ledger from personal spending — mixing them is the #1 source of 'where did the money go?' panic.

Two money mistakes beginners make. First, forgetting their own time: pricing as if your labor is free makes the business look profitable while you earn nothing per hour. Always include a time cost in your math, even roughly. Second, confusing revenue with profit: 'I made $1,000 this month!' while costs were $900 is a $100 month, not a $1,000 month. Celebrate revenue, but decide based on net. And a standing rule from this course: never spend money you cannot afford to lose on unproven ideas — validate with conversations and manual MVPs first, spend on tools and ads only after paying customers exist.

Build a small buffer before you need it. Once revenue covers costs, set aside a fixed slice of each month's net — even ten percent — into a separate business savings spot for slow months, refunds, or surprise expenses. Beginners often spend every dollar of a good month and then panic in a quiet one; a buffer turns volatility from a crisis into an inconvenience. Also decide your pay rule in advance: for example, you pay yourself a fixed modest amount monthly once the business sustains it, rather than draining whatever is left. A business that cannot pay you and still grow is sending you a message about its pricing or costs — listen to it via the numbers, not via stress. Revisit this lesson quarterly: update

your fixed costs, recompute break-even, and confirm your three targets still match reality. Fifteen minutes with honest numbers beats hours of worrying without them.

Practice: Build your one-page monthly tracker (on paper or a spreadsheet) with the five columns. Fill in last month's real numbers, even if they are zeros. Calculate your fixed costs, variable cost per unit, contribution per sale, and break-even units. Write your three targets (break-even / sustain / grow) and schedule a recurring monthly 20-minute 'money date' with yourself. Bring this sheet to every future pricing and spending decision.

Lesson 8: Launch Checklist + Your 90-Day Action Plan

This final lesson gets you launched: a concrete pre-launch checklist that catches the things beginners forget, and a 90-day action plan that turns this course into a working business. Launch does not mean a big announcement — it means real customers can find you, pay you, and receive value, starting this week. Everything before this lesson was preparation; this lesson is the starting gun.

The launch checklist — verify each item honestly before you announce. Offer: one clear offer with a price, written in one sentence a stranger understands. Payment: a working way to accept money, tested with a small transaction. Delivery: the MVP from Lesson 3 is built and you have delivered it at least once (even free). Proof: three interview notes or early customer reactions that confirm the pain is real. Presence: your one social platform set up per Lesson 6, with five posts scheduled. Pipeline: your list of 100 prospects started and first 10 outreach messages ready. Numbers: your break-even and targets from Lesson 7 written down. Legal basics: check your local rules for business registration, taxes, and any permits your offer needs — this course cannot give legal advice, so verify locally. If any item is missing, that missing item is your task for this week, not a reason to delay everything else.

Your 90-day action plan. Days 1–30 (Prove): finish validation interviews, build the MVP, set the price, win customers 1–10 by hand. Weekly scoreboard: interviews done, outreach sent, customers won, revenue. Days 31–60 (Stabilize): deliver excellently to the first ten, collect their feedback and referrals, start the social media routine, raise the price once using the ladder method if demand is strong. Weekly scoreboard adds: referrals received, posts published, repeat buyers. Days 61–90 (Systematize): document your delivery process so it takes less of your time, productize the most repeatable part (template, guide, or package), set the next 90-day targets. End-of-quarter review: revenue vs. targets, what worked, what to stop, one big bet for next quarter. The full template is in the materials section — print it and fill it in today.

Two launch mistakes to avoid. First, the endless polish loop: 'I'll launch when the website is perfect / the logo is done / I feel ready.' Launch is a date on the calendar, not a feeling — pick a date within 14 days and announce it to someone who will hold you to it. Second, launching to everyone instead of to your list: a launch post to strangers converts poorly; personal messages to your 100 prospects convert well. Launch quietly to warm contacts first, gather proof, then go wider.

A final honest note, because this course teaches process, not promises: doing everything here does not guarantee success — markets are uncertain and some good ideas still fail. What it does guarantee is that you will fail fast and cheap if the idea is wrong (Lessons 1–3), price sanely (Lesson 4), learn directly from customers (Lesson 5), build an audience asset (Lesson 6), know your numbers (Lesson 7), and execute in focused 90-day cycles (this lesson). That is the entire game: run the cycle, keep what works, cut what does not, repeat. Most people never finish one cycle. Finish yours.

Practice: Run the launch checklist now and mark each item done or missing with a date to fix it. Pick your launch date (within 14 days) and tell one person. Fill in the 90-day action plan template from the materials with your actual numbers and dates. Send your first 10 outreach messages this week. Your business starts the day a stranger pays you — make that day soon.
