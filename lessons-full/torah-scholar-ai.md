## Lesson 1: What Is the Torah, and How Do Jewish Scholars Study It?

In Jewish tradition, the Torah is the first five books of the Hebrew Bible: Genesis, Exodus, Leviticus, Numbers, and Deuteronomy. In Hebrew, these books are named by their opening words: Bereshit, Shemot, Vayikra, Bamidbar, and Devarim. Jewish tradition holds that the Torah is central to Jewish life, law, and study, and that it is read in synagogue in a weekly cycle over the course of a year. That weekly reading is called the parashah, and each portion has a name taken from its opening words. Study of Torah in Jewish tradition is not only reading for information; it is a practice of interpretation, discussion, and application, carried out over generations.

Jewish study distinguishes several layers of text. The written Torah is the five books themselves. The Mishnah and Talmud are later rabbinic works that record discussion, debate, and legal reasoning about the Torah and Jewish practice. Commentaries, such as those by Rashi, a medieval French scholar, and Maimonides, a medieval philosopher and legal scholar, interpret the text and explain how earlier scholars understood it. Jewish scholars often study a verse together with its commentaries, asking what the words mean, what other verses say on the same theme, and how the passage has been applied in practice. That method means a single verse can support a long discussion, and disagreement between commentators is normal and expected, not a sign that study has failed.

A useful way to begin study is to ask four questions about any passage. First, what does the text say in plain language, in its own context? Second, what do earlier commentators say about it, and where do they disagree? Third, what laws or practices, if any, does Jewish tradition connect to it? Fourth, what ethical or human question does it raise for the reader? Those four questions do not replace careful reading, but they give a beginner a structure that matches how Jewish study often proceeds. They also help you avoid a common error: taking one verse in isolation and treating it as if it settled a large question by itself, without its context or its history of interpretation.

This course uses the term AI in its title to mean a study helper, not a religious authority. An AI tool can help you find a passage, compare translations, summarise a commentary, and organise notes, but Jewish tradition holds that legal decisions and religious guidance belong to qualified human scholars and communal practice, not to a machine. That distinction is important throughout this course: use AI to support study, check its summaries against a reliable text, and do not treat an AI summary as a ruling or as Scripture itself.

A well-known opening verse, given verbatim here because its wording is certain in standard English translations, is Genesis 1:1: "In the beginning God created the heavens and the earth." Jewish commentators have discussed that verse at length, including what beginning means, what creation means, and how the rest of Genesis relates to it. This course does not settle those discussions for you; it introduces them so you can study them further with reliable sources. Most other passages in this course are given as labelled paraphrases, precisely so that no paraphrase is mistaken for a verbatim quotation.

A common mistake for beginners is to read the Torah as if it were a single modern book with one authorial voice and one simple message per passage. Jewish scholarship, as tradition presents it, reads it as a sacred text with a long history of interpretation, and it expects the reader to notice different genres: narrative, law, poetry, genealogy, and instruction. Another mistake is to assume that one movement or one scholar speaks for all Jews. Orthodox, Conservative, Reform, Reconstructionist, and other Jewish movements differ on authority, practice, and interpretation, and this course notes those differences rather than hiding them.

## Lesson 2: Genesis, Creation, and the First Human Stories

Genesis, called Bereshit in Hebrew, opens with creation and then follows early human stories and the ancestors of Israel: Abraham, Isaac, Jacob, and Joseph, with Sarah, Rebekah, Rachel, Leah, and other figures central to the narratives. Jewish tradition reads these stories as foundational, not only as history in a modern sense, but as texts that establish themes that return throughout the Torah: promise, covenant, family conflict, exile, and return. This lesson introduces those themes without claiming that every detail can be settled by one reading.

The verbatim opening, Genesis 1:1, "In the beginning God created the heavens and the earth," is followed in Genesis by an ordered account of creation over six days, with a seventh day of rest. Jewish tradition connects that seventh day to Shabbat, the weekly day of rest, which is studied in Lesson 4. Commentators have discussed whether the creation account should be read literally, allegorically, or in a way that holds both narrative meaning and other kinds of truth, and different Jewish scholars have taken different positions. This course does not choose one position for you; it notes that the discussion exists and that it has continued for centuries. What Jewish tradition widely holds is that the text presents God as creator and human beings as made in a special relationship to creation, with responsibility for it.

Paraphrase, labelled as paraphrase: the Genesis narrative presents Adam and Eve in a garden, with a command not to eat from one tree, and it presents their eating as a turning point that brings consequences for human life, including work, pain, and mortality. That is a paraphrase, not a quotation, and it is given here to summarise the story so you can find the full text and read it yourself. Later stories include Cain and Abel, with themes of jealousy and responsibility for a brother; Noah and the flood, with themes of corruption, preservation, and a covenant after the flood; and the Tower of Babel, with themes of human ambition and the diversity of languages. Each story is short in the text but long in commentary, and each has been read in many ways.

The ancestor stories begin with Abraham, whom Jewish tradition holds as a founding figure who is called to leave his home and to enter a covenant. Paraphrase, labelled as paraphrase: the covenant narrative presents promises of land, descendants, and blessing, and it presents Abraham's family as the carriers of that covenant, through Isaac and then Jacob, whose other name, Israel, becomes the name of the people. The Joseph story, which closes Genesis, presents family rivalry, slavery in Egypt, rise to power, and reconciliation, and it sets up the Exodus story that follows. Jewish study often asks how these family stories illuminate later national stories, and how personal choices in Genesis echo in communal choices later.

A common mistake is to read Genesis only for moral lessons that fit a modern view, and to ignore the text's own context, including its ancient setting and its later interpretation. Another mistake is to treat a paraphrase as if it were the text itself, or to quote a summary as Scripture. A third mistake is to assume that a story's meaning is settled because one commentary says so; Jewish study expects you to compare commentaries and to notice where they differ. A fourth mistake is to read the ancestor stories as if they were simple biographies, when Jewish tradition also reads them as texts about covenant, promise, and the formation of a people.

## Lesson 3: Exodus, Covenant, and the Ten Commandments in Tradition

Exodus, called Shemot in Hebrew, tells the story of Israelite slavery in Egypt, liberation, journey through the wilderness, and covenant at Mount Sinai. Jewish tradition holds that Exodus is central to Jewish identity, because it presents liberation from slavery as a founding experience, and it is retold every year at Passover, the festival that commemorates the Exodus. The Passover retelling is not only a history lesson; in Jewish tradition it is a communal act of memory, in which each generation is taught to see itself as if it had come out of Egypt. That teaching is presented here as what tradition holds, not as a claim about every individual reader's experience.

Paraphrase, labelled as paraphrase: the Exodus narrative presents Moses as a leader called to confront Pharaoh, plagues as signs that lead to release, crossing of the Sea of Reeds as escape, and a journey in which the people receive food, water, and guidance, and also complain, doubt, and learn. The covenant at Sinai is presented as a moment in which the people receive laws and enter a binding relationship, with obligations that shape communal life. Jewish scholars have discussed the historical, literary, and theological dimensions of Exodus at length, and different scholars emphasise different dimensions. This course introduces the narrative and its traditional importance, and it leaves detailed historical debate to specialist study.

The Ten Commandments, also called the Ten Words or Decalogue, are presented in Jewish tradition as a core summary of covenant obligations, given in Exodus 20 and repeated in Deuteronomy 5, with small differences between the two versions that commentators have discussed. Because translations vary in wording, this lesson does not present a full verbatim list from memory. Instead, it summarises the themes as tradition presents them, labelled as paraphrase: acknowledgement of God who brought the people out of Egypt, prohibition of other gods and idols, respect for the divine name, observance of Shabbat, honour for parents, and prohibitions of murder, adultery, theft, false witness, and coveting. That is a paraphrase of themes, not a quotation, and you should read the full text in a reliable translation if you want the exact wording, with book, chapter, and verse.

Jewish tradition distinguishes between commandments that are understood as between a person and God and those between a person and other people, and it holds that both kinds matter. The Ten Commandments include both kinds, which is one reason they are often taught as a summary. Later Jewish law, called halakhah, develops these commandments into detailed practice, and the Talmud and later codes discuss how they apply in specific cases. For example, the Shabbat commandment is developed into detailed laws about work, rest, and observance, which are studied in Lesson 4, and the prohibition of false witness is developed into laws about testimony, honesty, and reputation. That development is a key idea in Jewish study: a short commandment can generate a long tradition of interpretation.

A well-known verse that is certain in standard English translations, and given verbatim here, is Deuteronomy 6:4: "Hear, O Israel: The LORD our God, the LORD is one." That verse, called the Shema, is central in Jewish prayer and tradition, and it is recited daily in traditional prayer. Jewish scholars have discussed what oneness means, how it relates to the prohibition of other gods, and how the Shema functions as a declaration of faith and loyalty. This course presents the Shema as tradition presents it: a central declaration, not a full theological system by itself.

A common mistake is to treat the Ten Commandments as if they were the whole of Jewish law, when Jewish tradition holds that there are 613 commandments counted in rabbinic tradition, with the Ten as a summary, not a complete code. Another mistake is to quote a paraphrase of the commandments as if it were the exact text, when translations differ. A third mistake is to read Exodus only as a past event, when Jewish tradition also reads it as a living memory retold at Passover and in prayer. A fourth mistake is to assume that all Jewish movements observe the commandments in the same way; observance varies, and this course notes that variation rather than presenting one practice as universal.

If you quote, copy the wording from your translation and give book, chapter, and verse. ## Lesson 4: Shabbat, Festivals, and the Rhythm of Jewish Time

Jewish time, as tradition presents it, is shaped by Shabbat and by festivals that mark seasons, history, and agricultural cycles. Shabbat is the weekly day of rest, from Friday evening to Saturday evening, and Jewish tradition holds that it commemorates both creation, with rest on the seventh day, and liberation from Egypt, with rest from slavery. That double connection is important: Shabbat is not only a pause from work, but a weekly practice of freedom and of remembering creation. Observance varies between movements, and this lesson describes traditional practice while noting that variation.

Paraphrase, labelled as paraphrase: traditional Shabbat observance includes lighting candles on Friday evening, sharing festive meals, prayer in synagogue and at home, study, and refraining from categories of work defined in rabbinic tradition, including activities such as writing, building, cooking from scratch, and using electrical devices in ways that tradition defines as work. That is a paraphrase of traditional practice, not a ruling, and it is given as a summary so you can study the details in a reliable halakhic source if you wish. Different movements interpret the categories of work differently, and some Jews observe Shabbat in a more liberal way, focusing on rest, family, and community rather than on the full set of traditional restrictions. This course does not present one level of observance as the only authentic one; it describes traditional practice and notes variation.

Festivals in Jewish tradition include Passover, which commemorates Exodus; Shavuot, which tradition connects to the giving of the Torah at Sinai; Sukkot, the festival of booths, which tradition connects to the wilderness journey and to harvest; Rosh Hashanah, the New Year, and Yom Kippur, the Day of Atonement, which together form the High Holy Days, a period of reflection, repentance, and prayer; Hanukkah, which commemorates the rededication of the Temple in the Maccabean period; and Purim, which commemorates the story of Esther. Each festival has its own texts, foods, prayers, and practices, and each is studied in Jewish tradition as both a historical memory and a present communal practice. For example, Passover includes the Seder, a structured meal with retelling, questions, and symbolic foods, and Sukkot includes building and eating in a sukkah, a temporary booth.

Jewish study often connects festivals to the Torah readings that accompany them. Passover readings include Exodus, Shavuot readings include the Ten Commandments and the Book of Ruth, Sukkot readings include Ecclesiastes, and Purim readings include Esther. That connection between calendar and text is a useful study habit: when you study a festival, also study its readings, and when you study a Torah portion, note which festival, if any, it is connected to. AI tools can help you find those connections, but they should be checked against a reliable calendar and text, because festival dates and readings follow the Jewish calendar, which is lunisolar and does not align simply with the Gregorian calendar.

A common mistake is to treat Shabbat as only a set of prohibitions, when Jewish tradition also presents it as a positive practice of rest, joy, study, and family, with the Hebrew word oneg Shabbat meaning delight in Shabbat. Another mistake is to assume that all festivals are equally observed by all Jews, when observance varies by movement, family, and community. A third mistake is to read a festival only as history, without its present practice, or only as present practice, without its history. A fourth mistake is to use an AI summary of a festival as if it were a ruling on how to observe it; for practice, consult a qualified scholar or a reliable communal source, not a machine summary.

Practice exercise: Choose one festival, such as Passover or Sukkot, and write four paragraphs: one on its historical memory as tradition presents it, labelled as paraphrase where you summarise, one on its main practices, one on its Torah or related readings, and one on how observance varies between movements or families as you understand it from a reliable source. Then write a Shabbat paragraph describing, in general terms, how a traditional Shabbat evening begins, without presenting your description as a ruling. ## Lesson 5: Leviticus, Holiness, and Ethics Between People

Leviticus, called Vayikra in Hebrew, is often the hardest book for beginners, because much of it is law about sacrifices, purity, and priestly practice in the ancient Temple, rather than narrative. Jewish tradition holds that Leviticus is central to ideas of holiness, which in Hebrew is kedushah, meaning set apart for a sacred purpose. That idea is not only about ritual; in Leviticus, holiness is connected to ethics, to how people treat each other, and to how a community lives. This lesson introduces that connection, and it does so without presenting ancient Temple practice as if it were current practice in the same form, because the Temple no longer stands and Jewish practice has developed since then.

Paraphrase, labelled as paraphrase: Leviticus presents laws about offerings, about purity and impurity in relation to the sanctuary, about the Day of Atonement, and about a long list of ethical and communal laws, including honesty in business, care for the poor, respect for parents and elders, and prohibitions of wrongdoing. That is a paraphrase, not a quotation, and it is given to orient you before you read the text itself. A central chapter for ethics is Leviticus 19, which Jewish tradition often calls the Holiness Code, because it connects holiness to everyday behaviour. Paraphrase, labelled as paraphrase: that chapter presents the idea that a holy people should act justly, leave part of the harvest for the poor and the stranger, not steal or lie, pay workers promptly, not curse the deaf or put obstacles before the blind, and love one's neighbour as oneself. The last phrase is widely known, but because translations vary, it is given here as paraphrase, not as a verbatim quotation, and you should read it in a reliable translation if you want the exact wording.

Jewish scholars have discussed how Leviticus 19 relates to the rest of the Torah, and how its ethical laws relate to ritual laws. One view, presented in many commentaries, is that holiness in Leviticus is not only separation from impurity, but also a positive way of living that includes justice and compassion. Another view emphasises the Temple context and asks how laws that depended on the Temple are studied today, when there is no Temple. Jewish study today often reads Leviticus historically, ethically, and communally, rather than as a direct instruction to rebuild Temple practice. Different movements differ on how much of Leviticus is understood as binding law today, and how much is studied as history and ethics. This course notes that difference rather than settling it.

The Day of Atonement, Yom Kippur, is connected in Leviticus to a yearly ritual of atonement, and in later Jewish tradition it becomes a day of fasting, prayer, and repentance for the whole community. That development shows how Jewish tradition works: a biblical ritual is reinterpreted and reshaped over time, while keeping its core themes of accountability, forgiveness, and return. The Hebrew word teshuvah, often translated as repentance or return, is central here: in Jewish tradition, teshuvah means turning back, making amends, and changing behaviour, not only feeling sorry. That idea is developed in rabbinic literature and in later Jewish thought, and it is a good example of a concept that begins in the Torah and grows through commentary.

A common mistake is to skip Leviticus because it seems only ritual, and to miss its ethical chapters, especially Leviticus 19. Another mistake is to read purity laws as if they were modern hygiene rules, when Jewish tradition presents them as ritual categories related to the sanctuary, not as medical advice. A third mistake is to treat a paraphrase of an ethical law as if it were the exact text, or to apply an ancient law directly to a modern case without studying how tradition has interpreted it. A fourth mistake is to assume that all Jews today observe Leviticus in the same way; observance and interpretation vary, and this course presents that variation as normal.

Then write a paragraph on how teshuvah, as tradition presents it, goes beyond feeling sorry, including making amends and changing behaviour. ## Lesson 6: Numbers, Deuteronomy, and the Second Telling

Numbers, called Bamidbar in Hebrew, meaning in the wilderness, follows the Israelites through the wilderness, with censuses, journeys, complaints, and laws. Deuteronomy, called Devarim in Hebrew, meaning words, presents itself as Moses speaking again, retelling laws and history before the people enter the land. Jewish tradition holds that Deuteronomy is a second telling, not a contradiction of earlier books, but a restatement with emphasis, explanation, and application for a new generation. That idea of retelling for a new generation is useful for study in general: a text can be retold without being replaced, and a later telling can highlight what matters for a new situation.

Paraphrase, labelled as paraphrase: Numbers presents the organisation of the camp, the journeys from place to place, stories of rebellion and doubt, including the report of scouts who fear entering the land, and laws about offerings, vows, and inheritance, including the case of the daughters of Zelophehad, who raise a question about inheritance when there is no son. That case is important in Jewish study because it shows law developing through a question: a new situation arises, a question is brought to Moses, and a ruling follows that becomes part of the law. Jewish scholars often point to that pattern as an example of how law in the Torah is not only given once, but also developed through cases and questions.

Paraphrase, labelled as paraphrase: Deuteronomy retells the journey, restates the covenant, and emphasises loyalty to God, justice, care for the vulnerable, including the stranger, the orphan, and the widow, and the importance of teaching the law to children and of remembering the Exodus. It also presents the Shema, given verbatim in Lesson 3 as Deuteronomy 6:4, "Hear, O Israel: The LORD our God, the LORD is one," and it connects that declaration to teaching, to daily practice, and to communal memory. Jewish tradition connects Deuteronomy to the practice of writing the Shema in mezuzot, small cases on doorposts, and in tefillin, small boxes worn during weekday morning prayer in traditional practice, but observance of those practices varies between movements, and this course describes them as traditional practice, not as a universal rule for all readers.

Deuteronomy also presents blessings and curses, a long section that Jewish tradition reads as a warning about the consequences of loyalty and disloyalty, and as a text that has been read in many ways over time, including as history, as theology, and as a call to responsibility. Commentators have discussed whether those passages should be read literally, as a prediction, or as a rhetorical form that emphasises choice. This course does not settle that discussion; it notes that it exists and that it matters for how you read Deuteronomy. What Jewish tradition widely holds is that Deuteronomy presents choice as central: the people are presented with a path and are called to choose life, in a paraphrase of a theme that you should read in full in a reliable translation if you want the exact wording.

A common mistake is to read Numbers only as a list of numbers and journeys, and to miss its stories of doubt, leadership, and law developing through cases. Another mistake is to read Deuteronomy as if it were a completely new law that replaces earlier books, when Jewish tradition presents it as a retelling and application. A third mistake is to quote a paraphrase of a Deuteronomy theme as if it were a verbatim verse. A fourth mistake is to assume that a practice connected to Deuteronomy, such as mezuzah or tefillin, is observed in the same way by all Jews; observance varies, and this course notes that variation.

## Lesson 7: Prophets, Writings, and How the Torah Is Read in the Wider Bible

The Torah is the first section of the Hebrew Bible, called Tanakh in Jewish tradition, from the initials of Torah, Nevi'im meaning Prophets, and Ketuvim meaning Writings. Jewish tradition holds that the Torah has a special status, and that the Prophets and Writings are also sacred, but they are read in relation to the Torah, not as replacements for it. This lesson introduces the Prophets and Writings so you can see how the Torah is read in its wider context, and how later texts interpret, challenge, and apply earlier ones.

Paraphrase, labelled as paraphrase: the Prophets include historical books such as Joshua, Judges, Samuel, and Kings, which continue the story after the Torah, and prophetic books such as Isaiah, Jeremiah, Ezekiel, and the Twelve Minor Prophets, which present messages of warning, comfort, justice, and hope. Jewish tradition presents prophets not primarily as predictors of the future, but as messengers who call the people back to covenant, justice, and loyalty, often criticising kings, priests, and the people themselves when they fail. That emphasis on justice is a key theme: many prophetic passages, in paraphrase, present the idea that ritual without justice is empty, and that care for the vulnerable is a test of loyalty. That is a paraphrase of a prophetic theme, not a quotation, and you should read specific prophetic passages in a reliable translation to see how they make that point in their own words.

Paraphrase, labelled as paraphrase: the Writings include Psalms, which are prayers and songs used in worship and private prayer; Proverbs, which presents wisdom about everyday life; Job, which presents questions about suffering and justice; Ecclesiastes, which presents reflections on the limits of human understanding; Ruth, Esther, Song of Songs, Lamentations, and Daniel, each with its own genre and history. Jewish tradition reads Psalms as prayer, Proverbs as wisdom, and the other Writings as texts that raise human questions that the Torah's laws do not settle by themselves. That diversity is part of why Jewish study values discussion: different books ask different questions, and they do not always give the same answer in the same form.

How the Torah is read in synagogue connects all three sections. The weekly Torah portion is read from a handwritten scroll, called a Sefer Torah, and it is followed by a haftarah, a passage from the Prophets chosen to relate to the Torah portion or to the festival. That pairing is a study practice in itself: reading a Torah portion together with its haftarah shows how later texts were understood to illuminate earlier ones. Jewish tradition also includes the practice of studying a portion with commentaries during the week, so that the synagogue reading is not the only encounter with the text. Different movements vary in how much Hebrew is used, how the haftarah is chosen, and how commentaries are presented, but the weekly cycle itself is widely shared.

AI study tools can help you find a haftarah, compare a Torah portion with its prophetic parallel, or summarise a Psalm, but they must be checked against a reliable text. A common error with AI tools is to accept a summary that mixes two passages, or that presents a paraphrase as a quotation, or that invents a verse reference. The habit from Lesson 1 applies here with extra force: always check book, chapter, and verse against a reliable source, and label paraphrases as paraphrases. If an AI tool gives you a quotation you cannot verify, treat it as unverified, not as Scripture.

A common mistake is to read the Prophets only as predictions, and to miss their call to justice and covenant. Another mistake is to read the Writings only as poetry or wisdom, and to miss their connection to Torah themes. A third mistake is to assume that the Christian Old Testament and the Jewish Tanakh are ordered in the same way; they are not, and the Jewish order, Torah, Prophets, Writings, reflects a Jewish understanding of how the books relate. A fourth mistake is to present a prophetic paraphrase as if it were a Torah verse, or vice versa; always give the correct book and section.

Write three paragraphs: one summarising the Torah portion, labelled as paraphrase, one summarising the haftarah, labelled as paraphrase, and one explaining a connection between them that you can support from the texts themselves. If you quote, copy the wording from your translation and give book, chapter, and verse. Check that you have not mixed the two passages, and that you have not presented a summary as a quotation.

## Lesson 8: Studying with AI Responsibly, and Putting It All Together

This final lesson brings the course together by teaching a responsible method for using AI as a study helper for Torah, and by giving you a review task that uses all the skills from Lessons 1 to 7. The central principle, as presented throughout this course, is that AI can support study, but it cannot replace a reliable text, a qualified scholar, or communal practice. That principle protects both the text and the learner: it prevents a machine summary from becoming a false Scripture, and it prevents a learner from mistaking a plausible summary for a ruling.

A responsible AI study workflow has five steps. First, start with a reliable text: a printed or digital edition of the Torah in Hebrew and in a translation you trust, with book, chapter, and verse. Second, ask the AI for help with a specific task, such as summarising a commentary, finding related passages, or organising notes, and ask it to give citations for any quotation. Third, check every quotation against the reliable text, and check every verse reference by opening the passage yourself. Fourth, label your own notes clearly: verbatim quotation with citation, paraphrase labelled as paraphrase, commentary summary with the commentator's name, and your own reflection labelled as your own. Fifth, for any question about practice or law, consult a qualified human scholar or a reliable communal source, not the AI alone. That workflow is not a religious ruling; it is a study method that matches the labelling habit taught in this course.

Worked example of a good AI question: "Summarise, in your own words labelled as paraphrase, how Rashi and Maimonides differ on Genesis 1:1, and give me the book, chapter, and verse for the verse itself, without quoting from memory if you are unsure." That question asks for paraphrase, not for a new quotation, and it asks for a citation you can check. A poor AI question would be: "Give me a verse that proves my point, and do not worry about the exact wording." That question invites invention, and it should not be used. Another poor habit is to ask an AI for a ruling, such as "Is this allowed on Shabbat for me?" and to treat the answer as a decision. For practice questions, Jewish tradition holds that guidance belongs to qualified human scholars and to communal practice, not to a machine, and this course follows that distinction.

That note does not claim to be a complete summary of the Torah, and it does not present any new words as Scripture. It is a model of how to organise study notes honestly.

Differences between movements and scholars should be noted in your final note, not hidden. For example, if you write about Shabbat observance, note that traditional practice and liberal practice differ, and describe each as what its adherents hold, rather than presenting one as the only Jewish position. If you write about a commentary, give the commentator's name and note where another commentator disagrees, if you know of a disagreement from a reliable source. If you do not know, say that you do not know, and leave the question open for further study. A common mistake in final reviews is to pad a note with repeated summaries and to claim more than you have studied. Do not do that. A short, accurate note with correct citations is better than a long note with invented verses. Another mistake is to present a personal reflection as if it were tradition, or to present tradition as if it were your own opinion, without distinguishing the two. Label your reflections as your own. A third mistake is to use AI to generate a final note and to submit it without checking quotations and references; always check them yourself. A fourth mistake is to treat this course as a source of religious authority; it is a study introduction, not a ruling, and it does not replace a scholar, a teacher, or a community.
