## Lesson 1: Pronunciation, Greetings, and Being Polite

French pronunciation is regular once you learn its rules, but those rules are not English rules. Final consonants are often silent, vowels combine in fixed ways, and linking between words, called liaison, joins a normally silent final consonant to a following vowel. This lesson gives you the sounds you need for greetings and introductions, and it warns you about the most common English habits to drop.

Key sounds: the letter e at the end of a word is often silent, as in table, pronounced tabl. The combination ou sounds like oo in boot, as in bonjour. The combination ai and ei sound like eh, as in maison, house, pronounced meh-zon. The letter r in French is pronounced at the back of the throat, not like the English r, and it takes practice; do not worry about perfecting it on day one, but do not replace it with an English r in every word and expect to be understood easily. Nasal vowels are vowels pronounced through the nose, as in bon, good, and vin, wine, where the n is not fully pronounced as a consonant but nasalises the vowel. Bonjour is pronounced bon-zhoor, with a nasal on and a soft j like the zh in measure.

Greetings: Bonjour means good morning and good afternoon, and it is the standard polite hello during the day. Bonsoir means good evening. Bonne nuit means good night when someone is going to sleep. Salut is a casual hello, used with friends, not with a teacher or in a formal shop on first meeting. Au revoir means goodbye. À bientôt means see you soon. Merci means thank you, and merci beaucoup means thank you very much. S'il vous plaît means please in polite speech, and s'il te plaît is the casual form for friends and family. Excusez-moi means excuse me in polite speech, and pardon means sorry or excuse me in a lighter way. Oui means yes, non means no.

To introduce yourself, say Je m'appelle, followed by your name, meaning I call myself. Je suis means I am: Je suis étudiant means I am a student, with the masculine form étudiant, and Je suis étudiante is the feminine form. Enchanté means pleased to meet you, with enchantée as the feminine form. A full short introduction is: Bonjour, je m'appelle Anna. Je suis étudiante. Enchantée. That is polite, complete, and usable from day one. To ask someone's name politely, say Comment vous appelez-vous, meaning what do you call yourself, and to ask how someone is politely, say Comment allez-vous, meaning how are you going, with the casual form Comment vas-tu for friends.

Politeness in French uses vous for strangers, older people, and formal settings, and tu for friends, family, and children. Using tu too soon can sound rude, so vous is the safe default in this course until tu is introduced and labelled as casual. Titles help: Monsieur means Mr. or sir, Madame means Mrs. or madam, Mademoiselle was traditionally used for a young unmarried woman but is now often avoided in formal French administration; Madame is the safe default when you are unsure. Do not attach Monsieur or Madame to your own name when introducing yourself; they are for the other person.

A common mistake is to pronounce every final letter, saying bonjour with a hard r and a hard n, or merci with a hard i and a final sound that is not there. Another mistake is to use salut with a shopkeeper or teacher on first meeting; bonjour is safer. A third mistake is to say je suis bien for I am well in answer to Comment allez-vous, when the natural answer is Je vais bien, I am going well, or Très bien, merci, very well, thank you. Je vais bien follows the same verb aller, to go, as the question.

Practice exercise: Say each greeting aloud three times, slowly, then at normal speed, paying attention to nasal vowels and silent final letters. Write and say your own introduction in three sentences, using Je m'appelle, Je suis, and Enchanté or Enchantée as appropriate. Then practise a short exchange aloud: A: Bonjour, comment vous appelez-vous? B: Je m'appelle Anna. Comment allez-vous? A: Je vais bien, merci. Et vous? B: Très bien, merci. Record yourself if you can, and check that you are not adding English stress to every word.

## Lesson 2: Alphabet, Numbers 1–20, Gender, and Articles

French nouns have gender: every noun is masculine or feminine, and that gender affects articles, adjectives, and pronouns. Gender in French is grammatical, not a statement about the thing itself, and it must be learned with each noun. This lesson teaches numbers, articles, and a first set of nouns, so you can name things and count them correctly.

The French alphabet is the same Latin alphabet as English, but letter names and some sounds differ. For spelling aloud, learn: a ah, b beh, c seh, d deh, e uh, f eff, g zheh, h ash, with h usually silent in words, i ee, j zhee, k kah, l ell, m emm, n enn, o oh, p peh, q koo, r air, s ess, t teh, u oo, v veh, w doobluh-veh, x eeks, y ee-grek, z zed. Spelling your name aloud is a practical skill, and practising the alphabet helps you understand French spelling in general.

Numbers 1 to 20 are: 1 un, 2 deux, 3 trois, 4 quatre, 5 cinq, 6 six, 7 sept, 8 huit, 9 neuf, 10 dix, 11 onze, 12 douze, 13 treize, 14 quatorze, 15 quinze, 16 seize, 17 dix-sept, 18 dix-huit, 19 dix-neuf, 20 vingt. Note that 17, 18, and 19 are built as ten plus seven, eight, and nine. Pronunciation needs care: six is pronounced sees, huit is pronounced wheat without the w sound in the English sense, and dix is pronounced dees. Do not read these numbers as if they were English words.

Articles: le is the masculine singular the, la is the feminine singular the, les is the plural the for both genders, un is a masculine, une is a feminine, and des is some or plural a. Examples: le livre means the book, with livre masculine; la table means the table, with table feminine; les livres means the books; un livre means a book; une table means a table; des livres means some books. When a noun starts with a vowel, le and la become l', as in l'école, the school, with école feminine, and l'ami, the friend, with ami masculine. That elision, dropping the vowel before a vowel, is regular and must be used; saying le école sounds wrong.

Vocabulary in this lesson includes: le livre book, la table table, la maison house, le chien dog, le chat cat, l'école school, le café coffee or café, le pain bread, la pomme apple, l'ami friend. To say there is, use il y a: Il y a un livre sur la table means there is a book on the table, with sur meaning on. Il y a deux pommes means there are two apples. To say I have, use j'ai: J'ai un chien means I have a dog. Je n'ai pas de chien means I do not have a dog, with pas de replacing un or une in a negative. That change from un to de in negatives is a regular French pattern and a common test point.

A common mistake is to guess gender from English or from the thing itself. Le livre is masculine even though a book has no biological gender, and la table is feminine. Learn each noun with its article: not livre, but le livre; not table, but la table. Another mistake is to keep le or la before a vowel instead of using l'. A third mistake is to say je n'ai pas un chien instead of je n'ai pas de chien. A fourth mistake is to pronounce numbers as English, especially six, huit, and dix.

Practice exercise: Count from 1 to 20 aloud, then backwards. Then write ten phrases with articles, five masculine and five feminine, using nouns from this lesson, and pluralise five of them with les. Write five sentences with il y a and five with j'ai or je n'ai pas de, checking each article and each negative. For example: Il y a une pomme sur la table. Je n'ai pas de chat. Read them aloud and check that every noun has an article and that l' is used before a vowel.

## Lesson 3: Être, Avoir, and Simple Sentences

Être, to be, and avoir, to have, are the two most important verbs in French, and they are irregular, so they must be learned by heart. They appear in introductions, descriptions, age, feelings, and later in compound tenses, so learning them now saves time later. This lesson gives their full present forms and shows how to use them in simple sentences and questions.

Être in the present: je suis I am, tu es you are casual, il est he is, elle est she is, nous sommes we are, vous êtes you are polite or plural, ils sont they are masculine, elles sont they are feminine. Examples: Je suis professeur means I am a teacher, with professeur used for both masculine and feminine in modern usage, though professeure is also seen. Elle est française means she is French, with française as the feminine form of français. Nous sommes étudiants means we are students, masculine or mixed group, and Nous sommes étudiantes is the feminine group form. Il est dix heures means it is ten o'clock, with heures meaning hours. C'est means it is or this is, and it is used for introductions and descriptions: C'est un livre means this is a book, and C'est Marie means this is Marie.

Avoir in the present: j'ai I have, tu as you have casual, il a he has, elle a she has, nous avons we have, vous avez you have polite or plural, ils ont they have masculine, elles ont they have feminine. Examples: J'ai un frère means I have a brother, with frère meaning brother. Elle a une sœur means she has a sister, with sœur meaning sister. Nous avons deux enfants means we have two children, with enfants meaning children. Vous avez un chien means you have a dog. Note the elision in j'ai, where je plus ai becomes j'ai, and the liaison in ils ont, where the s in ils is pronounced as z before ont.

Age in French uses avoir, not être: J'ai vingt ans means I am twenty years old, literally I have twenty years, with ans meaning years. Do not say Je suis vingt ans; that is an English transfer error. Feelings also often use avoir: J'ai faim means I am hungry, literally I have hunger, with faim meaning hunger. J'ai soif means I am thirsty, with soif meaning thirst. J'ai froid means I am cold, and J'ai chaud means I am hot. To ask age, say Quel âge avez-vous, meaning what age do you have, politely, and to ask if someone is hungry, say Avez-vous faim, meaning do you have hunger.

Questions in French can be formed in three ways: by rising intonation, Vous êtes étudiant, with a rising voice; by inversion, Êtes-vous étudiant, with verb and pronoun swapped; or by est-ce que, Est-ce que vous êtes étudiant, meaning is it that you are a student. All three are correct, and intonation questions are the simplest for speaking. The negative is formed with ne and pas around the verb: Je ne suis pas étudiant means I am not a student, and Je n'ai pas de livre means I do not have a book, with ne becoming n' before a vowel. In casual speech ne is often dropped, but in writing and polite speech it is kept, so learn the full form first.

A common mistake is to use être for age and hunger, saying Je suis vingt ans or Je suis faim. Use avoir for those. Another mistake is to forget agreement: Elle est étudiante needs the feminine e, and Ils sont étudiants needs the masculine plural s, while Elles sont étudiantes needs both. A third mistake is to keep je before a vowel verb without elision, saying Je ai instead of J'ai. A fourth mistake is to use tu with a stranger; use vous as the default in this course.

Practice exercise: Write the full present forms of être and avoir from memory, then check them. Write twelve sentences: four with être for identity, four with avoir for possession, two for age with avoir, and two for hunger or thirst with avoir. Turn four of them into questions using intonation and four into negatives with ne and pas. For example: Q: Vous avez un chien? A: Oui, j'ai un chien. Or: Non, je n'ai pas de chien. Read each pair aloud, checking agreement and elision.

## Lesson 4: Regular Verbs, Present Tense, and Daily Routine

Most French verbs fall into regular groups, and learning the pattern for one verb lets you use many others. This lesson teaches the largest group, verbs ending in -er, plus a first set of -ir and -re verbs, and it uses them for daily routine, because routine sentences are the most useful first sentences after introductions.

The -er group includes: parler to speak, aimer to like or love, manger to eat, habiter to live, travailler to work, étudier to study, regarder to watch, écouter to listen to, jouer to play, acheter to buy. The present endings for -er verbs are: je -e, tu -es, il and elle -e, nous -ons, vous -ez, ils and elles -ent. The endings -e, -es, and -ent are all silent, so je parle, tu parles, il parle, and ils parlent all sound the same, parl. Only nous parlons and vous parlez sound different in this pattern. That means context and pronouns matter for understanding, and you must say the pronoun; you cannot drop it as in some other languages.

Examples: Je parle français means I speak French, with français meaning French language. Nous parlons anglais means we speak English, with anglais meaning English. Elle mange une pomme means she eats an apple. Ils regardent un film means they watch a film, with film meaning film. Vous habitez à Paris means you live in Paris, with à meaning in or at for a city. J'habite à Londres means I live in London. To say you like something, use aimer: J'aime le café means I like coffee. Je n'aime pas le thé means I do not like tea, with thé meaning tea. Aimer is a strong word in French for people, so for a friend you would usually say J'aime bien Marie, I like Marie well, rather than a bare J'aime Marie, which can sound like love.

Other regular verbs: finir to finish, from the -ir group, with endings je finis, nous finissons, vous finissez, ils finissent; and vendre to sell, from the -re group, with endings je vends, nous vendons, vous vendez, ils vendent. Examples: Je finis mon travail means I finish my work, with travail meaning work. Nous vendons des livres means we sell books. These groups are smaller than the -er group, but they are regular enough to learn as patterns. Irregular verbs such as aller, to go, faire, to do or make, and venir, to come, will be taught in later lessons, and they should not be forced into these regular patterns.

Daily routine vocabulary includes: se lever to get up, se coucher to go to bed, manger to eat, boire to drink, irregular but common, travailler to work, étudier to study, dormir to sleep, irregular, sortir to go out, rentrer to return home. A short routine might be: Je me lève à sept heures, je mange le petit-déjeuner, je travaille, je rentre à la maison, je regarde un film, je me couche à dix heures. Petit-déjeuner means breakfast, déjeuner means lunch, dîner means dinner. Se lever and se coucher are reflexive verbs, meaning the action is done to oneself, and the me, te, se changes with the person: Tu te lèves, Il se lève, Nous nous levons, Vous vous levez, Ils se lèvent. That reflexive pronoun is part of the verb, not an extra word to drop.

Time words: aujourd'hui today, demain tomorrow, hier yesterday, tous les jours every day, le matin in the morning, l'après-midi in the afternoon, le soir in the evening. Examples: Aujourd'hui, je travaille means today I work. Demain, j'étudie means tomorrow I study. Hier, j'ai regardé un film would need the past tense, which is Lesson 6, so for now use present with time words: Hier is introduced here for recognition, and full past sentences come next.

A common mistake is to drop the pronoun because the verb ending looks enough; in French, say Je parle, not just Parle, for I speak. Another mistake is to pronounce the silent -ent ending, saying parlent with a hard t. Another is to use aimer for people without bien, sounding stronger than intended. A fourth mistake is to forget the reflexive pronoun in se lever, saying Je lève instead of Je me lève, which means I lift something else, not I get up.

Practice exercise: Write your daily routine in eight sentences, using at least six different -er verbs and two reflexive verbs, with time words. Include one negative sentence and one question. Then conjugate parler, manger, and finir in the present from memory, and check the endings, especially the silent -e, -es, and -ent. Read your routine aloud, checking that every verb has a pronoun and that silent endings stay silent.

## Lesson 5: Numbers to 100, Time, Days, and Places

Larger numbers, time, and days in French are regular with a few famous exceptions, and learning them now makes shopping, travel, and appointments possible. This lesson teaches numbers to 100, clock time, days and months, and place words with à, en, and de.

Numbers: 20 vingt, 30 trente, 40 quarante, 50 cinquante, 60 soixante, 70 soixante-dix, literally sixty-ten, 80 quatre-vingts, literally four-twenties, 90 quatre-vingt-dix, literally four-twenty-ten, 100 cent. To make numbers between tens, add the ones with a hyphen: 21 vingt et un, with et for 21, 31, 41, 51, and 61, but not for 71, 81, and 91; 22 vingt-deux, 25 vingt-cinq, 33 trente-trois, 47 quarante-sept, 68 soixante-huit, 75 soixante-quinze, 83 quatre-vingt-trois, 99 quatre-vingt-dix-neuf. Note that 71 is soixante et onze, 81 is quatre-vingt-un, and 91 is quatre-vingt-onze. Those forms follow the logic of 70 and 90, and they are worth learning as set forms. For money, euros are used in France: vingt euros means twenty euros. For age, use ans as in Lesson 3: J'ai trente ans means I am thirty.

Days of the week are: lundi Monday, mardi Tuesday, mercredi Wednesday, jeudi Thursday, vendredi Friday, samedi Saturday, dimanche Sunday. They are not capitalised in French, unlike in English. Months are: janvier January, février February, mars March, avril April, mai May, juin June, juillet July, août August, septembre September, octobre October, novembre November, décembre December. They are also not capitalised. Dates use le plus number plus month: le 3 novembre means 3 November, and le premier novembre means 1 November, with premier meaning first. A full date is day, month, year, in that order, as in le 3 novembre 2026, which is opposite to the common American order.

Clock time uses heure for hour and minute for minute: Il est une heure means it is one o'clock, with une because heure is feminine; Il est deux heures means it is two o'clock; Il est trois heures et demie means it is half past three, with demie meaning half for a feminine noun; Il est quatre heures moins le quart means it is quarter to four, with moins meaning minus and quart meaning quarter. For minutes past, say et plus minutes: Il est cinq heures dix means it is 5:10. For minutes to the hour, say moins plus minutes: Il est six heures moins dix means it is 5:50. To ask the time, say Quelle heure est-il, meaning what time is it, and to say at a time, use à: à sept heures means at seven o'clock. Midi means midday, and minuit means midnight: Il est midi means it is midday.

Place words and prepositions: à means at, in, or to for a city or a specific place: à Paris, à l'école, à la maison, at home. En means in for a feminine country or a region: en France, en Angleterre, with France and Angleterre feminine. Au means in or to for a masculine country: au Canada, au Japon, with Canada and Japon masculine. Aux is the plural form: aux États-Unis, to the United States. De means from or of: de Paris, from Paris, and de la maison, from the house. Other places include: l'école school, le bureau office, le magasin shop, la gare station, l'hôpital hospital, la banque bank, la bibliothèque library, le parc park, le restaurant restaurant, la maison house. Examples: Je vais à l'école means I go to school, with vais from aller, to go. Je suis à la maison means I am at home. Je viens de Paris means I come from Paris, with viens from venir, to come. Je vais en France means I go to France.

A common mistake is to capitalise days and months as in English; in French they are lower case. Another is to use à for a country, saying à France instead of en France, or à Canada instead of au Canada. A third mistake is to say Il est une heures, with a plural s after une heure; one o'clock is singular, une heure, and two o'clock onwards is plural, deux heures. A fourth mistake is to translate half past as demie without heures, or to confuse et demie with moins le quart. Learn the three forms as a set: et demie for half past, et quart for quarter past, moins le quart for quarter to.

Practice exercise: Write numbers 21, 34, 47, 68, 75, 83, 99, and 100 in French words. Then write a timetable with six times, using heures, et demie, et quart, and moins le quart. Then write six place sentences: two with à for a city or school, two with en or au for a country, and two with de for from. Ask and answer: Quelle heure est-il, Quel jour sommes-nous, meaning what day are we, and Où allez-vous, meaning where are you going. Check each preposition and each lower-case day and month.

## Lesson 6: Passé Composé, Adjectives, and Describing Things

The passé composé is the main past tense for spoken French, and it lets you talk about what you did yesterday, last week, and earlier. It is formed with an auxiliary verb, être or avoir, plus a past participle. Most verbs use avoir, and a smaller set of movement and change verbs use être. Adjectives in this lesson add description, and their agreement must be learned with them.

Passé composé with avoir: J'ai mangé means I ate, with mangé as the past participle of manger. J'ai regardé un film means I watched a film. Nous avons parlé means we spoke. Elle a acheté un livre means she bought a book. The participle for -er verbs ends in -é: parler becomes parlé, manger becomes mangé, acheter becomes acheté. For -ir verbs like finir, the participle ends in -i: J'ai fini means I finished. For -re verbs like vendre, the participle ends in -u: J'ai vendu means I sold. Irregular participles must be learned: faire becomes fait, boire becomes bu, voir becomes vu, prendre becomes pris, écrire becomes écrit. Examples: J'ai fait mon travail means I did my work. J'ai vu un film means I saw a film. J'ai bu un café means I drank a coffee.

Passé composé with être: Je suis allé means I went, masculine, and Je suis allée is the feminine form, with allé from aller. Elle est venue means she came, with venue from venir. Nous sommes partis means we left, masculine or mixed, and Nous sommes parties is the feminine form, with parti from partir. The rule for être verbs is that the participle agrees with the subject in gender and number, adding e for feminine and s for plural. Common être verbs include: aller to go, venir to come, partir to leave, arriver to arrive, entrer to enter, sortir to go out, monter to go up, descendre to go down, rester to stay, tomber to fall, naître to be born, mourir to die, retourner to return. A helpful memory aid used in French teaching is the house of être, but the list itself is what matters, not the picture. The negative passé composé puts ne and pas around the auxiliary: Je n'ai pas mangé means I did not eat, and Je ne suis pas allé means I did not go, masculine.

Adjectives agree with the noun they describe. For most adjectives, add e for feminine and s for plural: grand big becomes grande feminine, grands masculine plural, grandes feminine plural. Petit small becomes petite, petits, petites. For adjectives ending in e already, such as rouge red, the masculine and feminine are the same: un livre rouge, une table rouge. For adjectives ending in x, such as heureux happy, the feminine is heureuse, and the plural masculine is heureux, with the feminine plural heureuses. Colour adjectives include: rouge red, bleu blue, bleue feminine, vert green, verte feminine, jaune yellow, noir black, noire feminine, blanc white, blanche feminine, rose pink. Examples: Une grande maison means a big house, with maison feminine. Un petit chien means a small dog, with chien masculine. Les livres rouges means the red books. Position matters: most adjectives come after the noun, as in une maison grande in the sense of a big house, but a small set of common adjectives come before the noun, including beau beautiful, bon good, grand big, petit small, nouveau new, vieux old, jeune young. Examples: Un beau livre means a beautiful book, and Une bonne pomme means a good apple, with bon becoming bonne feminine and beau becoming belle feminine before a feminine noun.

A common mistake is to use être for every passé composé verb, or to forget agreement for être verbs, saying Je suis allé for a woman instead of Je suis allée. Another mistake is to put the participle agreement on avoir verbs in a simple sentence; with avoir, the participle usually does not agree with the subject, only with a preceding direct object, which is a later rule, so for now do not add e or s to avoir participles for the subject. A third mistake is to place every adjective before the noun as in English; in French, most adjectives go after, and only the short common set goes before. A fourth mistake is to forget the feminine form of colours, saying une table bleu instead of une table bleue.

Practice exercise: Write ten past sentences: six with avoir and four with être, checking agreement for être verbs. Then write ten descriptions: five with adjectives after the noun and five with adjectives before the noun from the short set, checking gender and number. Convert three present sentences into passé composé, for example Je mange une pomme becomes J'ai mangé une pomme, and Je vais à Paris becomes Je suis allé à Paris, masculine, or Je suis allée à Paris, feminine. Read them aloud and check each auxiliary and each agreement.

## Lesson 7: Wanting, Liking, Ability, and Polite Requests

Preference and requests in French use vouloir, to want, pouvoir, to be able to or may, aimer, to like, and polite forms with s'il vous plaît and je voudrais, I would like. These verbs are irregular, so their present forms must be learned, and their use must be distinguished: vouloir for wanting, pouvoir for ability and permission, aimer for liking, and je voudrais for a polite want in a shop or restaurant.

Vouloir in the present: je veux I want, tu veux you want casual, il veut he wants, elle veut she wants, nous voulons we want, vous voulez you want polite or plural, ils veulent they want masculine, elles veulent they want feminine. Examples: Je veux un café means I want a coffee. Nous voulons un livre means we want a book. Je veux manger means I want to eat, with manger as the infinitive after vouloir. Je ne veux pas sortir means I do not want to go out. To ask what someone wants, say Que voulez-vous, meaning what do you want, politely, or Qu'est-ce que vous voulez, the fuller question form.

Pouvoir in the present: je peux I can, tu peux you can casual, il peut he can, elle peut she can, nous pouvons we can, vous pouvez you can polite or plural, ils peuvent they can masculine, elles peuvent they can feminine. Examples: Je peux parler français means I can speak French. Elle peut venir means she can come. Puis-je entrer means may I enter, with inversion for a polite question. Pouvez-vous m'aider means can you help me, with aider meaning to help and m' meaning me before a vowel. Pouvoir is for ability and permission; do not use it for knowing how to do something in the sense of savoir, to know, which is a later distinction: Je sais nager means I know how to swim, while Je peux nager means I can swim in the sense of being able to at this moment.

Aimer in the present follows the -er pattern: j'aime, tu aimes, il aime, elle aime, nous aimons, vous aimez, ils aiment. Examples: J'aime le café means I like coffee. J'aime lire means I like reading, with lire meaning to read. Je n'aime pas le thé means I do not like tea. For people, use aimer bien as in Lesson 4: J'aime bien Marie means I like Marie. To ask what someone likes, say Qu'est-ce que vous aimez, meaning what do you like. To say you prefer something, use préférer: Je préfère le café means I prefer coffee, with préférer following the -er pattern but with an accent change in some forms, je préfère, nous préférons.

Polite requests in a shop or restaurant often use je voudrais, the conditional of vouloir, meaning I would like, which is softer than je veux: Je voudrais un café, s'il vous plaît means I would like a coffee, please. Je voudrais voir ce livre means I would like to see this book. Other polite requests use pouvez-vous: Pouvez-vous me montrer ce livre means can you show me this book, with montrer meaning to show. Regardez, s'il vous plaît means look, please, as a polite imperative with vous. Asseyez-vous, s'il vous plaît means please sit down. Shopping language includes: Combien ça coûte means how much does that cost, with coûter meaning to cost; C'est trop cher means it is too expensive, with cher meaning expensive; C'est bon marché means it is cheap, with bon marché meaning good market in origin but used for cheap; Je le prends means I will take it, with le meaning it.

A common mistake is to use je veux in a shop when je voudrais is more polite; je veux is not wrong, but je voudrais sounds better with a stranger. Another mistake is to use pouvoir for knowing how to do something when savoir is needed; that distinction will be practised later, but notice it now. A third mistake is to say J'aime Marie for a simple friendship like, when J'aime bien Marie is safer. A fourth mistake is to answer a liking question with only oui, when a fuller answer is more natural: Oui, j'aime le café is better than a bare oui in conversation.

Practice exercise: Write a shop or restaurant dialogue with ten lines, including one aimer question and answer, one je voudrais request, two vouloir sentences, one pouvoir sentence, and two polite requests with s'il vous plaît. Then write five sentences about your own likes, wants, and abilities, checking each verb: aimer for like, vouloir for want, pouvoir for can or may, je voudrais for a polite want. Read the dialogue aloud, taking both parts, and practise a polite, unhurried tone.

## Lesson 8: Directions, Travel, and Putting It All Together

Travel language brings together numbers, places, directions, and polite requests, and it is a good final test of the course because you must use several patterns in one conversation. This lesson teaches directions, transport, and a review task that uses the whole course.

Direction words are: à droite to the right, à gauche to the left, tout droit straight ahead, devant in front, derrière behind, à côté de beside, entre between, loin far, près near. To ask where something is, say Où est, followed by the place: Où est la gare means where is the station. Où sont les toilettes means where are the toilets, with toilettes plural. To ask how to get somewhere, say Comment aller à, followed by the place: Comment aller à la gare means how do I get to the station. Answers often use: Tournez à droite means turn right, Tournez à gauche means turn left, Allez tout droit means go straight ahead, C'est à droite means it is on the right, C'est à gauche means it is on the left, C'est loin means it is far, C'est près means it is near. Prenez la première rue à droite means take the first street on the right, with rue meaning street and première meaning first.

Transport words include: la voiture car, le bus bus, le métro subway, le train train, l'avion aeroplane, le bateau boat, le vélo bicycle. To say by a means of transport, use en plus the vehicle without an article: en voiture by car, en bus by bus, en train by train, en avion by plane. To say on foot, use à pied: Je vais à l'école à pied means I go to school on foot. To say by bicycle, en vélo is common, and à vélo is also used. Examples: Je vais à Paris en train means I go to Paris by train. Nous allons à Londres en avion means we go to London by plane, with allons from aller. Je prends le bus means I take the bus, with prends from prendre, to take. To ask how long something takes, say Combien de temps, meaning how much time, as in Combien de temps pour aller à Paris, how long to go to Paris. To ask what time something leaves, say À quelle heure part le train, meaning at what time does the train leave, with part from partir.

Travel sentences often combine several lessons: Demain, je vais à Paris en train means tomorrow I go to Paris by train. Je voudrais un billet, s'il vous plaît means I would like a ticket, please, with billet meaning ticket. À quelle heure part le train means at what time does the train leave. Où est la gare means where is the station. Those sentences are not lists of words; they are full, usable questions, and they show why numbers, time, places, and polite requests must be learned together.

Now put the course together. A good short self-introduction and travel paragraph at this level might be: Bonjour, je m'appelle Anna. Je suis étudiante. J'aime lire et je parle français. Demain, je vais à Paris en train. Je voudrais un billet, s'il vous plaît. Où est la gare? Merci beaucoup, au revoir. That paragraph uses greetings, être, aimer, parler, aller, en train, je voudrais, a direction question, and a polite closing. It is not a test of every point; it is a model of how the pieces fit in real speech.

A common mistake is to use à for a means of transport, saying à train instead of en train, or to use en for on foot, saying en pied instead of à pied. Another mistake is to put the direction after the verb as in English without the French structure, or to forget à in Comment aller à la gare. A third mistake is to capitalise days and months in a travel sentence, as in Lesson 5. A fourth mistake is to use tu with a stranger when asking directions; vous is safer, as in Pouvez-vous m'aider, s'il vous plaît, can you help me, please.

Practice exercise, final review: Write a 150-word piece in French that includes: a greeting and introduction, a daily routine with three time words, a past event in the passé composé, a liking sentence, a want sentence, a comparison or description with an adjective, and a travel question with directions or transport. Then read it aloud, check every article, agreement, and auxiliary, and correct any missing ne, pas, à, en, or au. As a second task, list ten verbs you now know, with infinitive, present je form, and one example sentence each. That table is your revision card for the next stage of study. Keep studying in short, regular sessions, practise pronunciation aloud every day, and add new vocabulary only when you can use it in a full sentence, not as an isolated word list.
