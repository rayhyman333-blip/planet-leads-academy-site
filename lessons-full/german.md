## Lesson 1: Sounds, Alphabet, and Greetings

German spelling is highly regular, and learning its sounds early prevents years of guessing. The alphabet has 26 letters plus three umlauted vowels — ä, ö, ü — and the letter ß, called *Eszett* or *scharfes S*, which sounds like a sharp *s* and never appears at the start of a word: *Straße* (street), *groß* (big), *heißen* (to be called). Vowels can be long or short, and length often changes meaning: *Bett* (bed, short *e*) versus *Beet* (flower bed, long *e*), *Stadt* (city, short *a*) versus *Staat* (state, long *a*). Long vowels are marked by doubling (*See, Boot*), by a following silent *h* (*gehen, Sohn*), or by a single vowel in an open syllable (*Vater*). Umlauts change quality, not just length: *ä* sounds like the *e* in "bed" but more open, *ö* is like French *eu*, made by rounding the lips for *e*, and *ü* is made by rounding the lips for *i*. Practise minimal pairs: *schon* (already) versus *schön* (beautiful), *Mutter* (mother) versus *Mütter* (mothers).

Consonant habits matter. The letter *w* sounds like English *v*: *Wasser* (water). The letter *v* usually sounds like *f*: *Vater* (father), though in borrowed words it can sound like *v*. The letter *z* sounds like *ts*: *Zeit* (time), *Zug* (train). The letter *j* sounds like English *y*: *ja* (yes), *Jahr* (year). The combinations *sch* (*Schule*, school), *sp* at the start of a word (*Sport*, pronounced *shport*), and *st* at the start (*Stadt*, pronounced *shtadt*) all begin with a *sh* sound. The *ch* after *a, o,* or *u* is a throaty sound (*Buch, Nacht*), while after *e* or *i* it is softer, near the *h* in English "huge" (*ich, Licht*). Final consonants are devoiced: *Tag* (day) ends in a *k*-like sound, *Hund* (dog) ends in a *t*-like sound, and *lieb* (dear) ends in a *p*-like sound, even though the spelling keeps *g, d,* and *b*. Word stress usually falls on the first syllable of the root, except in many borrowed words and words with prefixes like *ver-* and *be-*.

Greetings depend on formality and region. *Guten Morgen* (good morning), *Guten Tag* (good day/hello), and *Guten Abend* (good evening) are safe everywhere; *Gute Nacht* is only a farewell at night. *Hallo* is friendly and widely usable. *Tschüss* is an informal goodbye; *Auf Wiedersehen* is formal. The formal *Sie* versus informal *du* distinction is essential: use *Sie* with strangers, officials, older people you do not know well, and in professional settings, and *du* with friends, family, children, and peers who invite it. Verbs change with this choice, so the distinction is grammatical, not just polite. Introductions: *Ich heiße Anna* (My name is Anna), *Wie heißen Sie?* (What is your name? formal), *Wie heißt du?* (informal), *Woher kommen Sie?* (Where are you from? formal), *Ich komme aus Berlin* (I come from Berlin), *Freut mich* (Pleased to meet you), *Wie geht es Ihnen?* (How are you? formal), *Wie geht es dir?* (informal), with the reply *Danke, gut. Und Ihnen? / Und dir?*

A common mistake is pronouncing *w* as in English, turning *Wasser* into "wosser." Another is using *du* with a stranger because English has only "you"; when in doubt, use *Sie*. Learners also say *Gute Nacht* on arrival in the evening; use *Guten Abend* for hello and save *Gute Nacht* for parting.

**Practice exercise:** Read these aloud: *Schule, Straße, Zeit, Wasser, schön, Bücher, Nacht, ich*. Then write a five-line introduction using *Guten Tag*, your name, where you come from, and a *Wie geht es…?* exchange, once formally and once informally, and note every verb ending that changes between the two versions.

**Extra worked examples — pronunciation in context:** Say these sentences slowly: *Ich heiße Anna und wohne in München* (My name is Anna and I live in Munich), *Wo ist die Straße zum Bahnhof?* (Where is the street to the station?), *Ich spreche ein bisschen Deutsch* (I speak a little German). Notice *ich* with the soft *ch* after *i*, *Buch* with the throaty *ch* after *u*, and *Straße* with the sharp *ß*. Practise the umlaut pairs aloud: *Mutter/Mütter, Vater/Väter, Sohn/Söhne, Stadt/Städte* — the umlaut often marks the plural, so hearing it helps you hear number. Also practise *schön* (beautiful) versus *schon* (already) and *grüßen* (to greet) versus *grüßen* with a tense *ü*: rounding your lips for *ü* while saying *i* is the trick, and the same lip shape gives you *ö* from *e*. Regional variation is real — the *r* in Berlin sounds different from the *r* in Bavaria, and the *ch* in *ich* varies too — but the standard forms taught here are understood everywhere, so do not worry about sounding regional yet.

## Lesson 2: Sein, Haben, and Describing Yourself

*Sein* (to be) and *haben* (to have) are the two most frequent verbs, and both are irregular. Present tense of *sein*: ich bin, du bist, er/sie/es ist, wir sind, ihr seid, sie/Sie sind. Present tense of *haben*: ich habe, du hast, er/sie/es hat, wir haben, ihr habt, sie/Sie haben. Note that *Sie* (formal you) always takes the same form as *sie* (they): *Sie sind, Sie haben*. Examples: *Ich bin Lehrerin* (I am a teacher, feminine), *Er ist Arzt* (He is a doctor), *Wir sind Freunde* (We are friends), *Ich habe einen Bruder* (I have a brother), *Sie haben zwei Kinder* (They have two children / You have two children, formal — context disambiguates).

German nouns have three genders — masculine (*der*), feminine (*die*), neuter (*das*) — and the gender must be learned with each noun, because endings give only partial clues. Words ending in *-ung, -heit, -keit, -schaft, -ion, -tät* are usually feminine (*die Zeitung, die Freiheit*); words ending in *-chen* and *-lein* are always neuter (*das Mädchen, das Brötchen*); words ending in *-er* for people and many professions are often masculine (*der Lehrer*), though the feminine adds *-in* (*die Lehrerin*). Plural forms are varied and must be learned: *der Mann / die Männer, die Frau / die Frauen, das Kind / die Kinder, der Lehrer / die Lehrer, das Buch / die Bücher, die Stadt / die Städte*. A dictionary entry gives gender and plural; learn all three together.

Adjectives before a noun take endings that depend on the article, gender, case, and number — a system beginners meet gradually. After the definite article in the nominative case, the adjective takes *-e*: *der gute Mann, die gute Frau, das gute Kind, die guten Leute*. After the indefinite article, masculine and neuter nominative take *-er* and *-es*: *ein guter Mann, ein gutes Kind*, while feminine stays *-e*: *eine gute Frau*. After no article, adjectives carry more information: *guter Wein, gutes Brot, gute Milch*. Predicate adjectives, after *sein*, never take endings: *Das Haus ist groß, Die Häuser sind groß*. This distinction — endings only when the adjective stands before the noun — prevents the common error of adding endings after *ist*.

Age uses *sein*, unlike Italian or French: *Ich bin dreißig Jahre alt* (I am thirty years old). Numbers 1–20: eins, zwei, drei, vier, fünf, sechs, sieben, acht, neun, zehn, elf, zwölf, dreizehn, vierzehn, fünfzehn, sechzehn, siebzehn, achtzehn, neunzehn, zwanzig. Note *eins* becomes *ein* before a noun (*ein Buch*), and *sechs* and *sieben* lose sounds in *sechzehn* and *siebzehn*.

A common mistake is guessing gender from English or from the object's nature; *das Mädchen* (the girl) is neuter because of the *-chen* ending, not because of meaning. Another is adding adjective endings after *sein*: *Ich bin gut* is right, *Ich bin guter* is wrong in this position. A third is confusing *ihr* (you plural, informal), *Ihr* (formal you, capitalised), and *sie* (they / she) — capitalisation and context matter.

**Practice exercise:** Write ten sentences about yourself and a friend using *sein* five times and *haben* five times, including age, a profession or description, and something possessed. Then rewrite the same sentences for a formal stranger, changing *du* forms to *Sie* forms and checking every verb ending.

**Extra worked examples — describing people:** *Mein Bruder ist groß und freundlich* (My brother is tall and friendly), *Meine Schwestern sind nett und klug* (My sisters are nice and clever — note *nett* stays *nett* after *sind*, with no ending, while *meine Schwestern* shows the plural). *Wir haben ein kleines Haus, aber einen großen Garten* (We have a small house but a big garden — *kleines* neuter nominative after *ein*, *großen* masculine accusative after *einen*). Opposites to learn: *jung/alt* (young/old), *reich/arm* (rich/poor), *glücklich/traurig* (happy/sad), *stark/schwach* (strong/weak), *schnell/langsam* (fast/slow). Professions: *der Lehrer/die Lehrerin, der Arzt/die Ärztin, der Ingenieur/die Ingenieurin, der Koch/die Köchin*. When you say where someone is from, use *aus* plus the place with no article for cities and most countries: *Ich komme aus Wien* (I come from Vienna), *Sie kommt aus der Schweiz* (She comes from Switzerland — countries with an article, like *die Schweiz, die USA, die Türkei*, keep it: *aus der Schweiz, aus den USA*).

## Lesson 3: Regular Verbs and Word Order

Regular German verbs in the present tense add endings to the stem, which is the infinitive minus *-en* (or *-n* after a vowel). For *machen* (to make/do), the stem is *mach-*: ich mache, du machst, er/sie/es macht, wir machen, ihr macht, sie/Sie machen. The pattern holds for *wohnen* (to live), *arbeiten* (to work), *lernen* (to learn), *spielen* (to play), *kommen* (to come), and *leben* (to live, in the sense of being alive). Verbs whose stem ends in *-t* or *-d* add an *e* before the *du* and *er/sie/es* endings for pronounceability: *arbeiten* gives *du arbeitest, er arbeitet* (not *arbeitst*), and *finden* gives *du findest, er findet*. Verbs whose stem ends in *-s, -ß, -x,* or *-z* drop the *s* of the *du* ending: *du heißt* (not *heißst*), *du reist, du tanzt*.

Separable verbs are a distinctive German feature: the prefix separates and goes to the end of the clause in the present tense. *Anrufen* (to call) becomes *Ich rufe dich morgen an* (I will call you tomorrow — literally "I call you tomorrow up"). *Aufstehen* (to get up): *Ich stehe um sieben Uhr auf*. *Einkaufen* (to shop): *Wir kaufen am Samstag ein*. *Anfangen* (to begin): *Der Film fängt um acht Uhr an*. The prefix carries meaning, so *kommen* (to come) versus *ankommen* (to arrive) versus *mitkommen* (to come along) are different verbs to learn as units. In the infinitive, they stay together (*anzurufen* in a *zu* construction, *aufzustehen*), which is how dictionaries list them.

Word order is the other core lesson. In a main clause, the conjugated verb is always in second position, whatever comes first: *Ich lerne Deutsch* (I learn German), *Heute lerne ich Deutsch* (Today I learn German — note the subject *ich* moves after the verb), *In Berlin wohne ich seit 2020*. This "verb second" rule is strict. In a yes/no question, the verb comes first: *Lernst du Deutsch? Wohnst du in Berlin?* In a question with a question word, the question word comes first and the verb second: *Wo wohnst du? Wann fängst du an? Warum lernst du Deutsch?* Negation uses *nicht* (not) or *kein* (no/not a): *Ich lerne nicht* uses *nicht* for verbs and adjectives, while *kein* negates a noun with an indefinite article: *Ich habe kein Auto* (I have no car), *Das ist keine Frage* (That is not a question). *Kein* declines like *ein*: *kein Mann, keine Frau, kein Kind, keine Leute*.

Time expressions usually follow a useful order: time, manner, place — *Ich fahre morgen mit dem Zug nach Hamburg* (I travel tomorrow by train to Hamburg). This is a tendency, not an absolute rule, but it helps beginners sound natural. Common question words: *wer* (who), *was* (what), *wo* (where), *wohin* (to where), *woher* (from where), *wann* (when), *warum* (why), *wie* (how), *wie viel* (how much), *welche/r/s* (which).

Common mistakes include keeping English word order after a time phrase (*Heute ich lerne Deutsch* is wrong; it must be *Heute lerne ich Deutsch*), forgetting to send the separable prefix to the end (*Ich anrufe dich* is wrong), and using *nicht* where *kein* belongs (*Ich habe nicht Auto* is wrong; it is *kein Auto*).

**Practice exercise:** Write twelve sentences: four with a time phrase first (verb second), four yes/no or *w*-questions, and four with separable verbs, including two negatives — one with *nicht* and one with *kein*. Then convert each statement into a question and check the verb position each time.

**Extra worked examples — verbs in conversation:** A short dialogue: *— Was machst du morgens? — Ich stehe um sieben Uhr auf, frühstücke und fahre zur Arbeit. — Und abends? — Abends lese ich ein Buch oder sehe fern.* (What do you do in the mornings? I get up at seven, have breakfast and drive to work. And in the evenings? In the evenings I read a book or watch TV.) Note *fernsehen* (to watch TV) is separable: *ich sehe fern*. Other separable verbs to meet early: *mitkommen* (to come along: *Kommst du mit?* — Are you coming along?), *zurückkommen* (to come back), *ausgehen* (to go out), *einschlafen* (to fall asleep), *anfangen* (to begin), *aufhören* (to stop). Irregular present forms to learn as whole words: *sein* (bin, bist, ist, sind, seid, sind), *haben* (habe, hast, hat, haben, habt, haben), *werden* (werde, wirst, wird, werden, werdet, werden), *gehen* (gehe, gehst, geht, gehen, geht, gehen), *fahren* (fahre, fährst, fährt, fahren, fahrt, fahren — note the umlaut in *du* and *er/sie/es*), *sehen* (sehe, siehst, sieht, sehen, seht, sehen), *sprechen* (spreche, sprichst, spricht, sprechen, sprecht, sprechen).

## Lesson 4: Cases — Nominative, Accusative, and Dative

German cases show the role of a noun in a sentence, and articles and adjective endings change accordingly. There are four cases; this lesson covers the first three, which carry most everyday conversation. The nominative is the subject: *Der Mann liest* (The man reads). The accusative is usually the direct object: *Ich sehe den Mann* (I see the man — note *der* becomes *den*). The dative is usually the indirect object, the person something is given to or done for: *Ich gebe dem Mann das Buch* (I give the book to the man — *dem* is dative masculine).

Definite articles change as follows. Masculine: nominative *der*, accusative *den*, dative *dem*. Feminine: *die, die, der*. Neuter: *das, das, dem*. Plural: *die, die, den* (plus an *-n* on the noun if it does not already end in *-n* or *-s*: *den Kindern, den Frauen* — *Frauen* already ends in *-n*). Indefinite articles: masculine *ein, einen, einem*; feminine *eine, eine, einer*; neuter *ein, ein, einem*; plural has no indefinite article, and *kein* follows the same pattern (*keinen Mann, keinem Mann*). Personal pronouns also change: ich/mich/mir, du/dich/dir, er/ihn/ihm, sie/sie/ihr, es/es/ihm, wir/uns/uns, ihr/euch/euch, sie/sie/ihnen, Sie/Sie/Ihnen.

Prepositions govern cases, and the common ones must be learned with their case. Accusative prepositions: *durch* (through), *für* (for), *gegen* (against), *ohne* (without), *um* (around/at, for time). Dative prepositions: *aus* (from/out of), *bei* (at/near), *mit* (with), *nach* (to/after), *seit* (since), *von* (from/of), *zu* (to). Two-way prepositions (*an, auf, hinter, in, neben, über, unter, vor, zwischen*) take the accusative for movement towards a place and the dative for location: *Ich gehe in die Schule* (movement, accusative) versus *Ich bin in der Schule* (location, dative); *Ich lege das Buch auf den Tisch* versus *Das Buch liegt auf dem Tisch*. This movement-versus-location distinction is the key to the whole two-way system.

Word order with two objects: the dative object usually comes before the accusative object when both are nouns (*Ich gebe dem Kind das Buch*), but a pronoun accusative comes before a noun dative (*Ich gebe es dem Kind*), and pronouns come in the order accusative before dative only when both are pronouns in a specific pattern learners meet later; the safe beginner rule is: pronouns before nouns, and dative noun before accusative noun. Possessives (*mein, dein, sein, ihr, unser, euer, ihr/Ihr*) decline like *ein*: *meinen Vater* (accusative masculine), *meiner Mutter* (dative feminine), *mit meinem Freund* (dative masculine).

Common mistakes: leaving every article as *der/die/das* regardless of role; using *für* with the dative (*für mich* is accusative — *für* always takes accusative, so *für mir* is wrong); and confusing *in die Schule* (going there) with *in der Schule* (being there). Another is forgetting the plural dative *-n*: *mit den Kind* is wrong; it is *mit den Kindern*.

**Practice exercise:** Write ten sentences using *geben, zeigen* (to show), and *helfen* (to help — dative verb: *Ich helfe dem Mann*, not accusative), each with a dative and an accusative element, and ten prepositional phrases split evenly between movement (accusative) and location (dative) with two-way prepositions. Underline every article and check its case.

**Extra worked examples — cases in real sentences:** *Der Lehrer gibt den Schülern die Bücher* (The teacher gives the books to the pupils — *den Schülern* is dative plural with the *-n* ending, *die Bücher* accusative plural). *Ich helfe meiner Mutter* (I help my mother — *helfen* takes the dative, so *meiner Mutter*, not accusative *meine Mutter*). *Ich danke dir* (I thank you — *danken* is also dative). *Das gehört mir* (That belongs to me — *gehören* takes the dative). *Ich denke an dich* (I think of you — *an* with a person takes the accusative here). *Ich warte auf dich* (I'm waiting for you — *auf* with waiting takes the accusative). Two-way prepositions in context: *Ich stelle die Tasche auf den Tisch* (I put the bag on the table — movement, accusative) versus *Die Tasche steht auf dem Tisch* (The bag is standing on the table — location, dative); *Ich hänge das Bild an die Wand* (movement) versus *Das Bild hängt an der Wand* (location). The verb tells you which reading to choose: verbs of placing (*stellen, legen, hängen, setzen*) signal movement, verbs of being (*stehen, liegen, hängen, sitzen*) signal location.

## Lesson 5: The Past Tense — Perfekt

Spoken German tells the past mainly with the *Perfekt*, formed with the present of *haben* or *sein* plus the past participle, which usually goes to the end of the clause: *Ich habe gelernt* (I learned / I have learned), *Wir sind gegangen* (We went / We have gone). The *Präteritum* (simple past: *ich lernte, ich ging*) is used in writing and storytelling, and for a few very common verbs in speech (*ich war, ich hatte, ich konnte, ich musste, ich wollte*), but beginners should master *Perfekt* first for conversation.

Regular participles use the pattern *ge- + stem + -t*: *machen → gemacht, lernen → gelernt, wohnen → gewohnt, spielen → gespielt, arbeiten → gearbeitet*. Irregular participles use *ge- + stem (often with a vowel change) + -en*: *gehen → gegangen, kommen → gekommen, sehen → gesehen, essen → gegessen, trinken → getrunken, schreiben → geschrieben, lesen → gelesen, sprechen → gesprochen*. Verbs with inseparable prefixes (*be-, ver-, er-, emp-, ent-, ge-, miss-, zer-*) take no *ge-*: *besuchen → besucht, verstehen → verstanden, erzählen → erzählt*. Verbs ending in *-ieren* also take no *ge-*: *studieren → studiert, telefonieren → telefoniert*. Separable verbs put *ge-* between prefix and stem: *anrufen → angerufen, aufstehen → aufgestanden, einkaufen → eingekauft, anfangen → angefangen*.

Most verbs take *haben*. *Sein* is used for verbs of movement or change of state, and for *sein, werden,* and *bleiben*: *Ich bin gegangen, gefahren, gekommen, aufgestanden, geblieben, geworden*. Examples: *Gestern habe ich einen Film gesehen* (Yesterday I saw a film), *Letzte Woche sind wir nach Hamburg gefahren* (Last week we travelled to Hamburg), *Ich habe lange geschlafen* (I slept for a long time), *Sie ist um acht Uhr aufgestanden* (She got up at eight o'clock). Time expressions: *gestern* (yesterday), *vorgestern* (the day before yesterday), *letzte Woche* (last week), *letzten Monat* (last month), *letztes Jahr* (last year), *vor zwei Tagen* (two days ago), *am Montag* (on Monday).

Negation and word order: the participle stays at the end, and *nicht* usually comes before it or before the element being negated: *Ich habe nicht gelernt* (I did not learn), *Ich bin nicht gegangen*. In a subordinate clause, the auxiliary moves to the end after the participle: *Ich weiß, dass du gelernt hast* — a pattern to recognise even before producing it fluently. Modal verbs in the past use the *Präteritum* of the modal plus the infinitive: *Ich konnte nicht kommen* (I could not come), *Wir mussten arbeiten* (We had to work).

Common mistakes: using *haben* for movement verbs (*Ich habe gegangen* is wrong; it is *Ich bin gegangen*), forgetting *ge-* on regular participles, adding *ge-* to *ver-* verbs (*verstanden*, not *ge verstanden*), and placing the participle in the middle of the sentence instead of at the end. Another is translating English "I have been" word-for-word; German *Ich bin gewesen* exists but is used differently, and beginners should use *Ich war* for "I was" in most contexts.

**Practice exercise:** Write a diary entry for yesterday using ten *Perfekt* verbs — five with *haben*, five with *sein*, including two separable verbs and one verb with an inseparable prefix. Then rewrite it with *vorgestern* and change the subject to *wir*, correcting every auxiliary and participle agreement (participles themselves do not change for person, but the auxiliary does).

**Extra worked examples — narrating a weekend:** *Am Samstag bin ich früh aufgestanden, habe gefrühstückt und bin mit Freunden ausgegangen. Wir haben ein Museum besucht, in einem kleinen Restaurant gegessen und Geschenke gekauft. Abends sind wir müde, aber glücklich nach Hause gekommen.* (On Saturday I got up early, had breakfast and went out with friends. We visited a museum, ate in a small restaurant and bought presents. In the evening we came home tired but happy.) Notice the auxiliaries: *aufstehen* and *ausgehen* and *kommen* take *sein*, while *frühstücken, besuchen, essen,* and *kaufen* take *haben*. Sequence words help narration: *zuerst* (first), *dann* (then), *danach* (afterwards), *schließlich* (finally), *plötzlich* (suddenly). Practise retelling the same weekend from a friend's point of view: *Sie ist früh aufgestanden, hat gefrühstückt…* — the participle does not change, but the auxiliary does (*ist* versus *bin*, *hat* versus *habe*). For very common verbs, the spoken past often uses *Präteritum* instead: *Ich war müde* (I was tired) is more natural than *Ich bin müde gewesen*, and *Ich hatte keine Zeit* (I had no time) beats *Ich habe keine Zeit gehabt* in speech.

## Lesson 6: Food, Ordering, and Quantities

German food vocabulary and restaurant phrases are immediately usable. Key words: *das Brot* (bread), *der Käse* (cheese), *die Butter* (butter), *das Fleisch* (meat), *der Fisch* (fish), *das Gemüse* (vegetables), *das Obst* (fruit), *die Kartoffeln* (potatoes), *der Reis* (rice), *die Nudeln* (noodles/pasta), *das Wasser* (water), *das Bier* (beer), *der Wein* (wine), *der Kaffee* (coffee), *der Kuchen* (cake), *das Eis* (ice cream). Meals: *das Frühstück* (breakfast), *das Mittagessen* (lunch), *das Abendessen* (dinner). Note the case: after *Ich hätte gern* (I would like), the noun is accusative: *Ich hätte gern ein Wasser* (neuter, unchanged), *Ich hätte gern einen Kaffee* (masculine accusative *-en*), *Ich hätte gern eine Suppe* (feminine).

Polite ordering phrases: *Ich hätte gern…* is the standard polite form, softer than *Ich will…* (I want…), which sounds demanding. *Einen Tisch für zwei, bitte* (A table for two, please). *Die Speisekarte, bitte* (The menu, please). *Was empfehlen Sie?* (What do you recommend? formal). *Ich nehme…* (I'll have…) is natural when you have decided. *Ich bin Vegetarier/Vegetarierin* (I am vegetarian, masculine/feminine), *Ich bin allergisch gegen Nüsse* (I am allergic to nuts), *Ohne Glutamat, bitte* is less common; *glutenfrei* (gluten-free) is the word to use. To pay: *Die Rechnung, bitte* (The bill, please), *Kann ich mit Karte zahlen?* (Can I pay by card?), *Zusammen oder getrennt?* (Together or separately?) — the waiter may ask this when a group pays. *Stimmt so* means "keep the change," used when paying cash and rounding up.

Quantities: *ein Kilo Äpfel* (a kilo of apples), *ein Liter Milch* (a litre of milk), *eine Flasche Wasser* (a bottle of water), *ein Stück Kuchen* (a piece of cake), *ein bisschen* (a little), *viel/wenig* (much/little — *viel* and *wenig* do not decline when used as adverbs of quantity: *viel Wasser, wenig Zeit*). In shops: *Ich hätte gern 200 Gramm Schinken, bitte* (I would like 200 grams of ham, please), *Ein Brötchen, bitte* (A bread roll, please), *Noch etwas?* (Anything else?) — the shopkeeper's standard question, answered with *Nein, danke* or *Ja, noch…*.

Taste and preference: *lecker* (tasty), *süß* (sweet), *salzig* (salty), *scharf* (spicy/hot), *sauer* (sour), *bitter* (bitter), *frisch* (fresh), *heiß* (hot), *kalt* (cold). Liking uses *gefallen* (to please) or *mögen* (to like) or *gern* with a verb: *Ich mag Kaffee* (I like coffee), *Ich trinke gern Kaffee* (I like drinking coffee — literally "I drink coffee gladly"), *Das Essen schmeckt gut* (The food tastes good), *Mir gefällt das Restaurant* (I like the restaurant — dative *mir*, because the restaurant pleases me). *Gern* is the most flexible beginner tool: *Ich esse gern Pizza, Ich reise gern, Ich lerne gern Deutsch*.

Common mistakes: saying *Ich will* to a waiter instead of *Ich hätte gern*; forgetting the masculine accusative *-en* (*einen Kaffee*, not *ein Kaffee*, after *hätte gern*); and translating "I like" only as *Ich mag*, missing the natural *gern* construction. Another is asking for *ein Milch* — milk is feminine: *eine Milch*, though *ein Glas Milch* (a glass of milk) is more natural in a café.

**Practice exercise:** Write a full restaurant dialogue for two people: greeting, table, drinks, mains, a dietary restriction, dessert, and paying, including *Ich hätte gern, Ich nehme, Die Rechnung, bitte*, a quantity phrase, and a *gern* sentence. Read it aloud, checking every article's case.

**Extra worked examples — at the market and the café:** At a market stall: *— Guten Tag! Ich hätte gern ein halbes Kilo Kirschen. — Gern. Sonst noch etwas? — Ja, noch 200 Gramm Käse und ein bisschen Basilikum. — Das macht fünf Euro fünfzig. — Danke!* (Good day! I'd like half a kilo of cherries. With pleasure. Anything else? Yes, another 200 grams of cheese and a little basil. That comes to five euros fifty. Thank you!) Note *Das macht…* for stating a total, and *Sonst noch etwas?* as the shopkeeper's standard question. At a café: *— Einen Cappuccino und ein Croissant, bitte. — Zum Hieressen oder zum Mitnehmen? — Zum Mitnehmen, bitte.* (A cappuccino and a croissant, please. To eat here or to take away? To take away, please.) Other useful items: *das Croissant, das Brötchen* (bread roll), *der Orangensaft* (orange juice), *das Mineralwasser* (mineral water), *mit Kohlensäure* (sparkling) versus *ohne Kohlensäure* (still), *die Pommes* (chips/fries), *die Currywurst* (curried sausage, a Berlin classic). Tipping in Germany is usually rounding up and saying *Stimmt so* when paying cash, rather than a fixed percentage.

## Lesson 7: Future, Modal Verbs, and the Weather

German expresses the future in two main ways. The most common in speech is the present tense with a future time expression: *Morgen fahre ich nach München* (Tomorrow I travel to Munich), *Nächste Woche besuche ich meine Eltern* (Next week I visit my parents). The formal future uses *werden* plus the infinitive, with the infinitive at the end: *Ich werde Deutsch lernen* (I will learn German), *Wir werden morgen ankommen* (We will arrive tomorrow), *Es wird regnen* (It will rain). Present tense of *werden*: ich werde, du wirst, er/sie/es wird, wir werden, ihr werdet, sie/Sie werden. The *werden* future is also used for predictions, promises, and suppositions: *Er wird schon schlafen* (He will probably already be asleep), *Das wird stimmen* (That is probably right). Beginners should use the present for plans and reserve *werden* for promises, predictions, and formal writing.

Modal verbs — *können* (can), *müssen* (must), *wollen* (want), *sollen* (should), *dürfen* (may/be allowed), *mögen* (like) — change word order: the modal is conjugated in second position and the main verb's infinitive goes to the end: *Ich kann Deutsch sprechen* (I can speak German), *Wir müssen heute arbeiten* (We must work today), *Darf ich hier sitzen?* (May I sit here?). Their present forms are irregular, with vowel changes in the singular: *können*: ich kann, du kannst, er kann, wir können, ihr könnt, sie können; *müssen*: ich muss, du musst, er muss, wir müssen, ihr müsst, sie müssen; *wollen*: ich will, du willst, er will, wir wollen, ihr wollt, sie wollen; *sollen*: ich soll, du sollst, er soll, wir sollen, ihr sollt, sie sollen; *dürfen*: ich darf, du darfst, er darf, wir dürfen, ihr dürft, sie dürfen; *mögen*: ich mag, du magst, er mag, wir mögen, ihr mögt, sie mögen. *Möchten* (would like) is the subjunctive of *mögen* and is extremely polite and common: *Ich möchte einen Kaffee, bitte*.

Days of the week: Montag, Dienstag, Mittwoch, Donnerstag, Freitag, Samstag (or Sonnabend in parts of northern Germany), Sonntag. Months: Januar, Februar, März, April, Mai, Juni, Juli, August, September, Oktober, November, Dezember. Dates: *am 3. Mai* (on the 3rd of May), *im Mai* (in May), *am Montag* (on Monday), *montags* (on Mondays, habitually).

Weather vocabulary: *Wie ist das Wetter?* (What is the weather like?), *Es ist warm/kalt* (It is warm/cold), *Es ist sonnig* (It is sunny), *Es ist bewölkt* (It is cloudy), *Es regnet* (It is raining), *Es schneit* (It is snowing), *Es ist windig* (It is windy), *Es ist neblig* (It is foggy), *Es donnert* (It is thundering), *Es ist schwül* (It is muggy). Seasons: *der Frühling, der Sommer, der Herbst, der Winter*. Temperatures: *Es sind zwanzig Grad* (It is twenty degrees).

Common mistakes: putting the infinitive next to the modal (*Ich kann sprechen Deutsch* is wrong; it is *Ich kann Deutsch sprechen*), using *werden* for every future plan where the present sounds more natural, and confusing *müssen* (must, obligation) with *sollen* (should, recommendation or reported instruction) and *dürfen* (permission). Another is *wollen* versus *möchten*: *Ich will einen Kaffee* sounds demanding; *Ich möchte einen Kaffee* is the polite default.

**Practice exercise:** Write your plans for next week, day by day, using five *werden* futures or present futures, three modal sentences (including one permission question with *dürfen* and one polite request with *möchten*), and a three-day weather forecast. Then rewrite one plan as a prediction with *werden* and explain why the present-tense version would sound different.

**Extra worked examples — making arrangements:** *— Was machst du am Samstag? — Am Samstag werde ich meine Schwester besuchen. Am Sonntag bleiben wir zu Hause, weil es regnen wird. — Und nächste Woche? — Am Montag habe ich eine Besprechung, am Dienstag fahre ich nach Venedig und komme am Donnerstag zurück.* (What are you doing on Saturday? On Saturday I'll visit my sister. On Sunday we'll stay home because it will rain. And next week? On Monday I have a meeting, on Tuesday I travel to Venice and come back on Thursday.) Note how the present tense (*fahre ich, komme ich*) is used for fixed plans, while *werden* marks the prediction (*es wird regnen*). For invitations, use *möchten* or a question: *Möchtest du mit ins Kino kommen?* (Would you like to come to the cinema with me?), *Hast du am Freitag Zeit?* (Do you have time on Friday?), *Lust auf einen Kaffee?* (Fancy a coffee?). Replies: *Gern!* (Gladly!), *Leider nicht, ich muss arbeiten* (Unfortunately not, I have to work), *Vielleicht ein anderes Mal* (Maybe another time). The modal *sollen* for suggestions: *Wir sollten früher losfahren* (We should set off earlier).

## Lesson 8: Travel, Directions, and Everyday Conversation

Travel German brings together cases, separable verbs, and polite forms. Transport words: *der Zug* (train), *der Bus* (bus), *die U-Bahn* (underground), *die Straßenbahn* (tram), *das Taxi, das Flugzeug* (plane), *der Bahnhof* (station), *der Flughafen* (airport), *der Bahnsteig* (platform), *die Fahrkarte* (ticket), *die Hin- und Rückfahrkarte* (return ticket), *die Haltestelle* (stop). Key phrases: *Eine Fahrkarte nach Köln, bitte* (A ticket to Cologne, please), *Wann fährt der Zug ab?* (When does the train depart?), *Von welchem Gleis?* (From which platform?), *Ist das ein direkter Zug?* (Is this a direct train?), *Wo muss ich umsteigen?* (Where do I have to change?), *Wie lange dauert die Fahrt?* (How long does the journey take?), *Entschuldigung, wo ist der Bahnhof?* (Excuse me, where is the station?).

Directions use the imperative and location words: *rechts* (to the right), *links* (to the left), *geradeaus* (straight ahead), *in der Nähe* (nearby), *weit weg* (far away), *gegenüber* (opposite), *neben* (next to), *an der Ecke* (on the corner), *die erste/zweite Straße* (the first/second street). The formal imperative uses *Sie* plus the infinitive: *Gehen Sie geradeaus, dann biegen Sie rechts ab* (Go straight, then turn right). The informal *du* imperative drops the *-st* and usually the final *-e*: *Geh geradeaus, Bieg rechts ab* (note *biegen* is separable: *ab* goes to the end). With *ihr*: *Geht geradeaus*. Polite softeners: *Könnten Sie mir bitte helfen?* (Could you please help me?), *Würden Sie bitte langsamer sprechen?* (Would you please speak more slowly?).

Everyday conversation fills in with small, high-frequency expressions: *Entschuldigung* (excuse me / sorry), *Es tut mir leid* (I am sorry), *Bitte* (please / you're welcome), *Danke schön / Vielen Dank* (thank you very much), *Gern geschehen* (you're welcome), *Kein Problem* (no problem), *Alles klar* (all clear / okay), *Genau* (exactly), *Stimmt* (that's right), *Wirklich?* (Really?), *Ach so!* (I see! / Oh, I get it!), *Na ja* (well…), *Also…* (So… / Well…). Shopping phrases: *Ich schaue mich nur um* (I am just looking around), *Kann ich das anprobieren?* (Can I try this on?), *Haben Sie das in einer größeren/kleineren Größe?* (Do you have this in a larger/smaller size?), *Was kostet das?* (How much does that cost?), *Das ist zu teuer* (That is too expensive), *Ich nehme es* (I'll take it).

Numbers beyond twenty: einundzwanzig, zweiundzwanzig, dreiundzwanzig, vierundzwanzig, fünfundzwanzig, sechsundzwanzig, siebenundzwanzig, achtundzwanzig, neunundzwanzig, dreißig, vierzig, fünfzig, sechzig, siebzig, achtzig, neunzig, hundert, tausend. Note the ones come before the tens (*einundzwanzig*, literally "one-and-twenty") and *eins* becomes *ein* in compounds. Prices: *Das kostet zehn Euro fünfzig* (That costs ten euros fifty). *Euro* does not change in the plural, and *Cent* is used for cents.

Common mistakes: asking *Wo ist…?* without *Entschuldigung* first, which can sound abrupt; using the *du* imperative with strangers instead of the *Sie* form; and confusing *rechts* with *geradeaus*. Another is translating "I am looking for" as *Ich suche für*; German says *Ich suche…* with no preposition: *Ich suche ein Hotel*.

**Practice exercise:** Write a travel day in German: arriving at a station, buying a ticket, asking directions to a hotel, checking in, and asking about dinner nearby. Use at least five direction words, three polite requests, one *Perfekt* sentence about what you did, and one future sentence about tomorrow. End with a short shop dialogue using *Was kostet das?* and a size question.

**Extra worked examples — handling problems politely:** Travel rarely goes perfectly, so learn repair phrases: *Ich habe nicht verstanden, können Sie das bitte wiederholen?* (I didn't understand, can you please repeat that?), *Können Sie bitte langsamer sprechen?* (Can you please speak more slowly?), *Wie sagt man… auf Deutsch?* (How do you say… in German?), *Ich habe den Zug verpasst* (I missed the train), *Ich habe meinen Pass verloren* (I've lost my passport), *Gibt es hier in der Nähe einen Arzt?* (Is there a doctor nearby?), *Ich brauche Hilfe* (I need help), *Rufen Sie einen Krankenwagen!* (Call an ambulance!). At a hotel: *Ich habe eine Reservierung auf den Namen Müller* (I have a reservation under the name Müller), *Wann gibt es Frühstück?* (When is breakfast?), *Kann ich mein Gepäck hier lassen?* (Can I leave my luggage here?), *Bis wann muss ich das Zimmer räumen?* (By when must I vacate the room?). Politeness in German is carried by *bitte, danke, Entschuldigung,* and the *Könnten Sie…?* (Could you…?) form far more than by elaborate grammar: *Könnten Sie mir bitte helfen?* opens almost any request gracefully.
