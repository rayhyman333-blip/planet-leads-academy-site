# Arabic

*Local draft lesson text for Planet Leads Academy. This is study material only. Arabic words are given in Arabic script with a transliteration; transliteration is approximate, and listening to native speakers is the best check. This course teaches Modern Standard Arabic, the written and formal spoken variety used across the Arab world, while noting where everyday speech differs.*

## Lesson 1: The Arabic Alphabet and Sounds

Arabic is written from right to left in a cursive script, which means letters join together within a word and change shape depending on whether they are at the start, middle, or end of a word, or standing alone. That sounds difficult, but there are only 28 letters, and learning their four forms is a matter of practice rather than memorisation of hundreds of symbols. Start by learning the isolated form of each letter and its sound, then learn how it joins.

The letters, in traditional order with approximate sounds, are: ا (alif, a long "a" or a carrier for other vowels), ب (baa, "b"), ت (taa, "t"), ث (thaa, "th" as in "think"), ج (jiim, "j" as in "jump" in Modern Standard Arabic; in Egyptian speech it is often "g"), ح (haa, a breathy "h" from deep in the throat, with no English equivalent), خ (khaa, like the "ch" in Scottish "loch"), د (daal, "d"), ذ (dhaal, "th" as in "this"), ر (raa, a rolled "r"), ز (zaay, "z"), س (siin, "s"), ش (shiin, "sh"), ص (saad, a deep, emphatic "s"), ض (daad, a deep, emphatic "d"), ط (taa, a deep, emphatic "t"), ظ (dhaa, a deep, emphatic "th" as in "this"), ع (ayn, a distinctive throat sound with no English equivalent, made by constricting the throat), غ (ghayn, like a French "r" or a gargled "g"), ف (faa, "f"), ق (qaaf, a deep "q" from the back of the throat), ك (kaaf, "k"), ل (laam, "l"), م (miim, "m"), ن (nuun, "n"), ه (haa, "h"), و (waaw, "w" and also a long "oo"), ي (yaa, "y" and also a long "ee").

Arabic has three short vowels, written as small marks above or below a consonant: fatḥa ( َ ) for a short "a", kasra ( ِ ) for a short "i", and ḍamma ( ُ ) for a short "u". In most everyday texts these marks are omitted, and readers infer the vowels from context and word patterns — one reason reading Arabic fluently takes practice beyond learning the alphabet. Long vowels are written with the letters ا, و, and ي: كتاب (kitaab, "book") has a long "aa" in the middle. The doubled-consonant mark shadda ( ّ ) shows that a consonant is held longer, and the absence of a vowel is shown by sukuun ( ْ ).

Two features deserve early attention. First, the emphatic consonants ص, ض, ط, ظ and the throat sounds ح and ع have no English equivalents, and they change meaning: س (siin) and ص (saad) are different sounds, and mixing them up can turn one word into another. Practise them slowly with a native-speaker recording, feeling where in the mouth and throat each sound is made. Second, six letters do not join to the letter after them: ا, د, ذ, ر, ز, و. A word containing one of these will have a visible break after it, which is normal and not a spelling error.

Common mistakes include reading left to right out of habit, treating every letter's isolated form as its only form, and ignoring the difference between ح and ه (both roughly "h", but ح is deeper and they are different letters) or between س and ص. Write letters by hand from the start: handwriting forces you to learn the joining forms in a way that reading alone does not.

**Practice:** Learn the letters in groups of four or five over several days, writing each one in its isolated, initial, medial, and final forms while saying its sound. Then read and copy these words, sounding them out letter by letter: باب (baab, "door"), كتاب (kitaab, "book"), بيت (bayt, "house"), سلام (salaam, "peace"), مدرسة (madrasa, "school"). Check your handwriting against a printed alphabet chart, paying special attention to letters that do not join to the following letter.

## Lesson 2: Greetings, Introductions, and Politeness

Arabic greetings carry warmth and a religious and cultural history, and using them correctly is one of the fastest ways to be welcomed in conversation. The most universal greeting is السلام عليكم (as-salaamu ʿalaykum, "Peace be upon you"). The standard reply is وعليكم السلام (wa ʿalaykum as-salaam, "And upon you be peace"). This greeting is used at any time of day and in both formal and informal settings, and learning the reply as well as the greeting matters — a greeting is an exchange, not a single phrase.

Time-specific greetings are also common: صباح الخير (ṣabaaḥ al-khayr, "Good morning", literally "morning of goodness"), answered with صباح النور (ṣabaaḥ an-nuur, "Morning of light"), and مساء الخير (masaaʾ al-khayr, "Good evening"), answered with مساء النور (masaaʾ an-nuur). For parting, مع السلامة (maʿa as-salaama, "With peace", i.e. "Goodbye") and إلى اللقاء (ilaa al-liqaaʾ, "Until we meet again") are both widely used. The informal "hi" in many dialects is أهلاً (ahlan, "welcome"), and أهلاً وسهلاً (ahlan wa sahlan) is a warm welcome to a guest.

Introducing yourself uses اسمي… (ismii…, "My name is…"): اسمي أحمد ("My name is Ahmad"). To ask someone's name: ما اسمك؟ (maa ismuk?, "What is your name?", to a man) and ما اسمكِ؟ (maa ismuki?, to a woman). The difference reflects Arabic's grammatical gender, which you will meet fully in Lesson 3: many words change depending on whether you are speaking to a man or a woman. Learn both forms from the start rather than learning only the masculine form and guessing later.

Essential polite expressions are: من فضلك (min faḍlik, "please", to a man) and من فضلكِ (min faḍliki, to a woman); شكراً (shukran, "thank you"); عفواً (ʿafwan, "you're welcome / excuse me"); آسف (aasif, "sorry", said by a man) and آسفة (aasifa, said by a woman); نعم (naʿam, "yes"); لا (laa, "no"). To say you do not understand: لا أفهم (laa afham, "I don't understand"). To ask someone to speak slowly: تكلّم ببطء من فضلك (takallam bi-buṭʾ min faḍlik, "Speak slowly, please", to a man). To say you speak a little Arabic: أتكلم قليلاً من العربية (atakallam qaliilan min al-ʿarabiyya, "I speak a little Arabic").

A simple introduction combines these elements: "السلام عليكم! اسمي سارة. أنا من إنجلترا. أتكلم قليلاً من العربية." ("Peace be upon you! My name is Sarah. I am from England. I speak a little Arabic.") Replace the name and country with your own. Country names you may need: مصر (Miṣr, "Egypt"), السعودية (as-Saʿuudiyya, "Saudi Arabia"), إنجلترا (Injiltiraa, "England"), أمريكا (Amriikaa, "America"), فرنسا (Faransaa, "France").

Common mistakes include using the masculine form when speaking to a woman (ما اسمك؟ to a woman, instead of ما اسمكِ؟), replying to السلام عليكم with the same phrase instead of وعليكم السلام, and shortening شكراً in a way that loses its final vowel and sounds abrupt. Say polite phrases slowly and fully; in Arabic conversation, the greeting and thanks are not filler, they are part of the respect being shown.

**Practice:** Write and rehearse three short dialogues: (1) a greeting and introduction between two strangers, including the correct reply to السلام عليكم, (2) a morning greeting exchange using صباح الخير and its reply, and (3) a polite exchange in a shop with "please", "thank you", and "goodbye". Say each one aloud three times, then change the names, countries, and times of day and repeat, taking care to use the correct masculine or feminine form for the person addressed.

**A worked greeting exchange:** Person A: السلام عليكم. Person B: وعليكم السلام. A: صباح الخير. B: صباح النور. A: ما اسمك؟ B: اسمي عمر. ما اسمك؟ A: اسمي سارة. B: أهلاً وسهلاً. A: شكراً. Notice that the exchange alternates: a greeting is answered, a question is returned, and a welcome is thanked. Learning greetings as pairs — greeting and reply, question and returned question — rather than as isolated phrases is what makes a real conversation flow, because the other person expects the second half of each pair.

## Lesson 3: Nouns, Gender, and the Definite Article

Arabic nouns have two genders, masculine and feminine, and gender affects adjectives, pronouns, and verb forms. There is no neuter gender. Most nouns are masculine by default; feminine nouns are often marked by the ending ة (taa marbuuṭa), pronounced "-a" at the end of a word in isolation: مدرسة (madrasa, "school") is feminine, كتاب (kitaab, "book") is masculine, بيت (bayt, "house") is masculine, مدينة (madiina, "city") is feminine. Some nouns are feminine without this ending and must be learned individually, including many body parts that come in pairs (يد, yad, "hand"; عين, ʿayn, "eye") and the names of most cities and countries. As with Russian, learn each new noun together with its gender.

Arabic has no indefinite article — no equivalent of "a" — so كتاب can mean "a book" or "book" in general. The definite article is ال (al-, "the"), attached to the start of the noun: الكتاب (al-kitaab, "the book"), المدرسة (al-madrasa, "the school"), البيت (al-bayt, "the house"). In pronunciation, the "l" of ال is dropped before "sun letters" — ت, ث, د, ذ, ر, ز, س, ش, ص, ض, ط, ظ, ل, ن — and the following consonant is doubled: الشمس (ash-shams, "the sun", not al-shams), while before "moon letters" (the rest, including ب, ج, ح, خ, ع, غ, ف, ق, ك, م, ه, و, ي) the "l" is pronounced: القمر (al-qamar, "the moon"). The sun/moon distinction is a pronunciation rule, not a spelling change: ال is always written the same way.

Arabic nouns also have three numbers: singular, dual (exactly two), and plural. The dual is formed by adding -aan to the noun: كتابان (kitaabaan, "two books"), مدرستان (madrasataan, "two schools"). Plurals are of two kinds. Sound plurals add a suffix: masculine sound plurals add -uun in the nominative (معلمون, muʿallimuun, "teachers", masculine) and feminine sound plurals add -aat (معلمات, muʿallimaat, "teachers", feminine). Broken plurals change the word's internal pattern, and there are many patterns: كتاب (kitaab, "book") becomes كتب (kutub, "books"), بيت (bayt, "house") becomes بيوت (buyuut, "houses"), مدينة (madiina, "city") becomes مدن (mudun, "cities"). Broken plurals must be learned with each noun, like irregular plurals in English (child/children), and dictionaries list them.

A simple sentence pattern is هذا… ("This is…", masculine) and هذه… ("This is…", feminine): هذا كتاب ("This is a book"), هذه مدرسة ("This is a school"). هذا and هذه agree with the noun that follows, so choosing between them practises gender from the first sentence. Another useful pattern is nominal sentences, which in Arabic have no present-tense verb "to be": أنا معلم ("I am a teacher", said by a man), أنا معلمة ("I am a teacher", said by a woman). The pronoun and the description sit side by side, just as in Russian, and the "is" is understood.

Common mistakes include treating every ة noun as feminine but forgetting unmarked feminine nouns, pronouncing ال as "al-" before sun letters (الشمس as "al-shams"), and using a plural suffix on a noun that takes a broken plural (كتابات instead of كتب for "books"). When you learn a noun, learn three things: its gender, its plural, and whether its plural is sound or broken.

**Practice:** Sort these nouns by gender and give their plurals, checking your answers: كتاب (book), مدرسة (school), بيت (house), مدينة (city), معلم (teacher, masculine), سيارة (sayyaara, "car"). Then write ten sentences beginning هذا or هذه about objects around you, choosing the correct form each time, and add ال to five of them to make definite sentences, for example هذا الكتاب ("This is the book"). Read them aloud, pronouncing ال correctly before sun and moon letters.

**A worked sun-letter check:** Take الشمس ("the sun"): ش is a sun letter, so ال is pronounced "ash-" and the ش is doubled in sound, giving ash-shams. Take القمر ("the moon"): ق is a moon letter, so the ل is pronounced, giving al-qamar. Take المدرسة ("the school"): م is a moon letter, giving al-madrasa. The spelling never changes — ال is always written the same — so the check is purely about pronunciation, and it is worth practising aloud with a list of sun and moon nouns until the doubled consonant after sun letters feels automatic.

## Lesson 4: Pronouns and Present-Tense Verbs

Arabic pronouns distinguish gender and number, including the dual. The independent pronouns are: أنا (anaa, "I"), أنتَ (anta, "you", to a man), أنتِ (anti, "you", to a woman), هو (huwa, "he"), هي (hiya, "she"), نحن (naḥnu, "we"), أنتم (antum, "you", plural, to men or a mixed group), أنتنّ (antunna, "you", plural, to women), هم (hum, "they", masculine/mixed), هنّ (hunna, "they", feminine). The dual pronouns أنتما (antumaa, "you two") and هما (humaa, "they two") exist but are used less in everyday speech; recognise them when you see them, and focus first on the singular and plural forms above.

Arabic verbs are built from a three-letter root that carries a core meaning. The root ك-ت-ب (k-t-b) relates to writing: كتاب (kitaab, "book") and كاتب (kaatib, "writer") share it, and the verb كتب (kataba, "he wrote") is built from it. The present tense is formed from a present stem with prefixes and suffixes that show the person: for the verb يكتب (yaktub, "he writes", from the root k-t-b), the full present conjugation is: أنا أكتب (anaa aktub, "I write"), أنتَ تكتب (anta taktub, "you write", to a man), أنتِ تكتبين (anti taktubiin, "you write", to a woman), هو يكتب (huwa yaktub, "he writes"), هي تكتب (hiya taktub, "she writes"), نحن نكتب (naḥnu naktub, "we write"), أنتم تكتبون (antum taktubuun, "you write", plural), أنتنّ تكتبن (antunna taktubna, "you write", plural, feminine), هم يكتبون (hum yaktubuun, "they write"), هنّ يكتبن (hunna yaktubna, "they write", feminine).

Notice the pattern: the prefixes are أ (for "I"), ت (for "you" and "she"), ي (for "he" and "they masculine"), ن (for "we"), and the suffixes -iin, -uun, -na mark feminine singular "you", plural, and feminine plural respectively. Once you know this pattern for one verb, you can apply it to other verbs of the same form. Other useful verbs in this lesson: يقرأ (yaqraʾ, "he reads"), يتكلم (yatakallam, "he speaks"), يعيش (yaʿiish, "he lives"), يعمل (yaʿmal, "he works"), يدرس (yadrus, "he studies"), يحب (yuḥibb, "he loves/likes"), يفهم (yafham, "he understands"). So: أنا أتكلم العربية قليلاً ("I speak a little Arabic"), هو يعيش في لندن ("He lives in London"), نحن ندرس العربية ("We study Arabic").

Arabic present tense, like Russian, covers both "I write" and "I am writing"; there is no separate continuous form in the basic present tense. Negation of the present tense uses لا (laa) before the verb: لا أفهم ("I don't understand"), لا أتكلم العربية ("I don't speak Arabic"). Word order in verbal sentences is often verb–subject–object (يكتب الطالب الدرس, "The student writes the lesson"), but subject–verb–object is also used, especially when the subject is a pronoun or is emphasised, so أنا أكتب الدرس is perfectly natural.

Common mistakes include using the masculine "you" form for a woman (أنتَ instead of أنتِ, تكتب instead of تكتبين), forgetting that هي takes the same ت prefix as أنتَ (هي تكتب, not هي يكتب), and translating English "do" as a separate verb in questions. Arabic questions often use هل (hal, "do/is") at the start — هل تتكلم العربية؟ ("Do you speak Arabic?") — or simply rising intonation, without an auxiliary verb.

**Practice:** Conjugate يكتب and يقرأ in full for أنا, أنتَ, أنتِ, هو, هي, نحن, and هم. Then write eight sentences about yourself and others using at least four different verbs from this lesson, including one negative sentence with لا and one question with هل. Read them aloud, checking each prefix and suffix against the person you mean.

**A worked verb check:** Take يدرس ("he studies"). The pattern from يكتب applies directly: أنا أدرس, أنتَ تدرس, أنتِ تدرسين, هو يدرس, هي تدرس, نحن ندرس, هم يدرسون. The only new information is the stem درس; the prefixes and suffixes are the same. This is the payoff of learning the pattern rather than memorising isolated forms: each new regular verb costs you one stem, not ten forms. When a verb does not follow the pattern, that is worth noting separately, because irregular verbs are usually the most frequent ones and therefore worth the extra attention.

## Lesson 5: Adjectives, Possession, and Description

Arabic adjectives agree with the noun they describe in gender, number, and definiteness, and they normally follow the noun, the opposite order from English. A new book is كتاب جديد (kitaab jadiid, literally "book new"), a new school is مدرسة جديدة (madrasa jadiida, "school new", with the feminine ending ة added to the adjective), and the new book is الكتاب الجديد (al-kitaab al-jadiid, "the book the new"). The definiteness rule is important: if the noun has ال, the adjective must have ال too; if the noun has no ال, the adjective has none. الكتاب جديد (al-kitaab jadiid) without ال on the adjective is not "the new book" but a sentence: "The book is new."

Useful adjectives: جديد (jadiid, "new"), قديم (qadiim, "old"), كبير (kabiir, "big"), صغير (ṣaghiir, "small"), جميل (jamiil, "beautiful"), جيد (jayyid, "good"), مهم (muhimm, "important"), سريع (sariiʿ, "fast"), سهل (sahl, "easy"), صعب (ṣaʿb, "difficult"). Colours follow the same agreement pattern but with a special masculine/feminine pair: أحمر (aḥmar, "red", masculine) and حمراء (ḥamraaʾ, "red", feminine); أزرق (azraq, "blue", masculine) and زرقاء (zarqaaʾ, "blue", feminine); أخضر (akhḍar, "green", masculine) and خضراء (khaḍraaʾ, "green", feminine); أصفر (aṣfar, "yellow", masculine) and صفراء (ṣafraaʾ, "yellow", feminine); أبيض (abyaḍ, "white", masculine) and بيضاء (bayḍaaʾ, "white", feminine); أسود (aswad, "black", masculine) and سوداء (sawdaaʾ, "black", feminine). Learn colour pairs together, because the feminine form is not made by the usual ة rule alone.

Possession in Arabic is shown by suffixes attached to the noun, not by a separate word like "my" in most cases: كتابي (kitaabii, "my book"), كتابك (kitaabuk, "your book", to a man), كتابكِ (kitaabuki, "your book", to a woman), كتابه (kitaabuh, "his book"), كتابها (kitaabuhaa, "her book"), كتابنا (kitaabunaa, "our book"), كتابكم (kitaabukum, "your book", plural), كتابهم (kitaabuhum, "their book"). The suffix changes with the owner, while the noun itself stays the same — the mirror image of Russian, where the possessive word changes with the noun. A second possession pattern, the iḍāfa (construct state), joins two nouns: كتاب الطالب (kitaab aṭ-ṭaalib, "the student's book", literally "book of the student"), بيت المعلم (bayt al-muʿallim, "the teacher's house"). In an iḍāfa, the first noun never takes ال, and definiteness comes from the second noun.

Common mistakes include putting the adjective before the noun as in English (جديد كتاب instead of كتاب جديد), giving ال to the noun but not the adjective in "the new book" (الكتاب جديد, which means "The book is new"), and using the masculine colour form for a feminine noun (سيارة أحمر instead of سيارة حمراء, since سيارة, "car", is feminine). Check gender, number, and definiteness together every time you write a noun with an adjective.

**Practice:** Write "new", "big", "beautiful", and "good" with كتاب, مدرسة, بيت, and سيارة, first indefinite ("a new book") and then definite ("the new book"), giving all the combinations with correct endings and ال placement. Then write six sentences using possessive suffixes (my book, her school, our house, their car, and two of your choice) and two iḍāfa phrases, and read them aloud, checking each adjective's position and agreement.

**A worked description check:** "The beautiful new school" is المدرسة الجديدة الجميلة: the noun has ال, so both adjectives have ال, and both follow the noun with feminine endings. "A beautiful new school" is مدرسة جديدة جميلة, with no ال anywhere. "The school is beautiful" is المدرسة جميلة, with ال only on the noun — and that, as Lesson 5 explains, is a sentence, not a phrase. If you are unsure whether you have written a phrase or a sentence, check ال: matching ال on noun and adjective means a phrase; ال on the noun alone means a sentence.

## Lesson 6: Past Tense and the Verb System

Arabic past tense is formed with suffixes attached to the past stem, and it is in some ways more regular than the present tense, because there are no prefixes to learn — only suffixes. For the verb كتب (kataba, "he wrote"), the past conjugation is: أنا كتبتُ (anaa katabtu, "I wrote"), أنتَ كتبتَ (anta katabta, "you wrote", to a man), أنتِ كتبتِ (anti katabti, "you wrote", to a woman), هو كتب (huwa kataba, "he wrote"), هي كتبتْ (hiya katabat, "she wrote"), نحن كتبنا (naḥnu katabnaa, "we wrote"), أنتم كتبتم (antum katabtum, "you wrote", plural), أنتنّ كتبتنّ (antunna katabtunna, "you wrote", plural, feminine), هم كتبوا (hum katabuu, "they wrote"), هنّ كتبن (hunna katabna, "they wrote", feminine).

The pattern is consistent across regular verbs: the suffixes -tu, -ta, -ti, -a (no suffix for "he"), -at, -naa, -tum, -tunna, -uu, -na attach to the stem. So from درس (darasa, "he studied"): أنا درستُ, أنتَ درستَ, هي درستْ, نحن درسنا, هم درسوا. From قرأ (qaraʾa, "he read"): أنا قرأتُ, هو قرأ, هي قرأتْ. Verbs whose roots contain و, ي, or a hamza (ء) change in ways that must be learned verb by verb, but the suffix pattern above remains the framework, and regular verbs like كتب, درس, and عمل (ʿamila, "he worked") are the right place to master it.

Negation of the past tense uses ما (maa) before the verb: ما كتبتُ ("I didn't write"), ما درستُ ("I didn't study"). This differs from the present tense, which uses لا, so the negative word itself tells you the tense — a useful clue when reading. Useful past-tense sentences: أمس قرأتُ كتاباً جديداً ("Yesterday I read a new book"), أمس درستُ العربية ("Yesterday I studied Arabic"), هو عمل في المدرسة ("He worked in the school"), نحن عشنا في لندن ("We lived in London"). أمس (ams, "yesterday") is the time word you will use most with the past tense, alongside اليوم (al-yawm, "today") and غداً (ghadan, "tomorrow") for other tenses.

Arabic also has a future tense, formed by adding سـ (sa-) or سوف (sawfa) before the present-tense verb: سأكتب (sa-aktub, "I will write"), سوف أدرس (sawfa adrus, "I will study"). سـ is shorter and more common in everyday speech, while سوف is slightly more formal and emphatic. Knowing present, past, and this future marker gives you the three time frames for everyday conversation, even though Arabic's full verb system, including imperatives and subjunctives, is larger and is learned gradually.

Common mistakes include using present-tense prefixes in the past (أنا يكتب for a past action), confusing the "she" past suffix -at with the present prefix ت (هي كتبتْ is past; هي تكتب is present — the position of the ت, suffix versus prefix, is the difference), and negating the past with لا instead of ما. When you write a past-tense sentence, check three things: the suffix matches the subject, the time word fits (أمس with past), and the negative word is ما if there is one.

**Practice:** Conjugate درس and عمل in the past tense for أنا, أنتَ, أنتِ, هو, هي, نحن, and هم. Then write six sentences about yesterday using أمس and a past-tense verb, including one negative sentence with ما and one sentence about someone else (هو or هي) so you practise the third-person forms. Finally, rewrite two of your sentences in the future with سـ, and read all of them aloud, checking each suffix.

**A worked tense contrast:** Compare أنا أدرس العربية ("I study / am studying Arabic", present) with أنا درستُ العربية ("I studied Arabic", past) and سأدرس العربية ("I will study Arabic", future). The stem درس stays recognisable in all three, and the tense is carried by the prefix in the present, the suffix in the past, and سـ in the future. Reading a sentence's tense therefore means looking at the verb's edges: a prefix like أ or ي points to the present, a suffix like -تُ points to the past, and سـ points to the future.

## Lesson 7: Numbers, Time, and Days

Arabic numbers 1 to 10 are: واحد (waaḥid, 1), اثنان (ithnaan, 2), ثلاثة (thalaatha, 3), أربعة (arbaʿa, 4), خمسة (khamsa, 5), ستة (sitta, 6), سبعة (sabʿa, 7), ثمانية (thamaaniya, 8), تسعة (tisʿa, 9), عشرة (ʿashara, 10). Tens are: عشرون (ʿishruun, 20), ثلاثون (thalaathuun, 30), أربعون (arbaʿuun, 40), خمسون (khamsuun, 50), ستون (sittuun, 60), سبعون (sabʿuun, 70), ثمانون (thamaanuun, 80), تسعون (tisʿuun, 90), مئة (miʾa, 100). Compound numbers put the unit before the ten, joined by و (wa, "and"): واحد وعشرون (waaḥid wa ʿishruun, 21), خمسة وثلاثون (khamsa wa thalaathuun, 35). Arabic numerals in writing are often the Western digits 0–9 in many countries, while Eastern Arabic numerals (٠١٢٣٤٥٦٧٨٩) are used in others; recognise both, and use Western digits while learning, as they are widely understood.

Numbers in Arabic interact with gender and with the noun that follows them in a famous pattern. The numbers 1 and 2 agree with the noun in gender: كتاب واحد (one book, masculine), مدرسة واحدة (one school, feminine), كتابان اثنان (two books). The numbers 3 to 10 show reverse agreement, or polarity: a masculine-sounding number goes with a feminine noun and vice versa, because the numbers 3–10 historically carry the feminine ة ending when counting masculine nouns: ثلاثة كتب (thalaathat kutub, "three books" — ثلاثة looks feminine but counts the masculine كتب), ثلاث مدارس (thalaath madaris, "three schools" — ثلاث looks masculine but counts the feminine مدارس). With 3–10, the noun is in the plural. With 11 and above, the noun is singular, and the number pattern changes again: أحد عشر كتاباً (eleven books, with the noun singular and in the accusative), عشرون كتاباً (twenty books). This system is genuinely complex, and even native speakers simplify it in everyday speech, especially with large numbers — so learn the 1–10 pattern thoroughly, recognise the 11+ pattern, and do not let perfection with large numbers stop you from using small numbers confidently.

Telling the time uses الساعة (as-saaʿa, "the hour / o'clock"). To ask the time: كم الساعة؟ (kam as-saaʿa?, "What time is it?") To answer on the hour: الساعة الثالثة (as-saaʿa ath-thaalitha, "It is three o'clock", using the ordinal "third"). Minutes past the hour use و ("and"): الساعة الثالثة وعشر دقائق (three o'clock and ten minutes). Half past uses النصف (an-niṣf, "half"): الساعة الثالثة والنصف ("half past three"). Minutes before the next hour use إلا (illaa, "except"): الساعة الرابعة إلا عشر دقائق ("ten minutes to four", literally "four except ten minutes"). Days of the week, starting from Sunday in the traditional Arabic week, are: الأحد (al-aḥad, Sunday), الاثنين (al-ithnayn, Monday), الثلاثاء (ath-thulaathaaʾ, Tuesday), الأربعاء (al-arbiʿaaʾ, Wednesday), الخميس (al-khamiis, Thursday), الجمعة (al-jumuʿa, Friday), السبت (as-sabt, Saturday). Friday, الجمعة, is the day of congregational prayer and the weekly holiday in many Arab countries, and the weekend in much of the region is Friday and Saturday rather than Saturday and Sunday.

Useful time words: اليوم (al-yawm, "today"), غداً (ghadan, "tomorrow"), أمس (ams, "yesterday"), صباحاً (ṣabaaḥan, "in the morning"), مساءً (masaaʾan, "in the evening"), الآن (al-aan, "now").

Common mistakes include using the same number form for masculine and feminine nouns in the 3–10 range (ثلاثة مدارس instead of ثلاث مدارس), putting the noun in the plural after 11 and above (عشرون كتب instead of عشرون كتاباً), and translating English time expressions word for word instead of using و for past and إلا for "to". Check gender polarity for 3–10 and singular-versus-plural for 11+ before you speak a number phrase.

**Practice:** Write the correct phrases for: 1 book, 2 books, 3 books, 5 schools, 10 books, 11 books, 21 books, using the patterns above and checking gender polarity. Then write the times 3 o'clock, half past 3, 3:10, and ten to 4 in Arabic. Finally, write a short paragraph about your week using اليوم, غداً, and أمس and at least three days of the week, and read it aloud.

## Lesson 8: Everyday Conversation — Shops, Travel, and Putting It Together

This final lesson combines everything so far into the conversations a visitor actually has: buying something, asking directions, ordering food, and handling problems politely. The grammar is not new; the skill is choosing the right phrase quickly and understanding the answer.

In a shop, start with السلام عليكم, then ask: بكم هذا؟ (bi-kam haadhaa?, "How much is this?", for a masculine item) or بكم هذه؟ (bi-kam haadhihi?, for a feminine item). The answer will be a number plus a currency word, so Lesson 7's numbers are essential here. To say you want something: أريد… (uriid…, "I want…") plus the noun, for example أريد هذا الكتاب ("I want this book"). To ask for something politely: من فضلك, أعطني… ("Please, give me…"), for example من فضلك, أعطني خبزاً ("Please, give me bread"). Useful shop words: محل (maḥall, "shop"), خبز (khubz, "bread"), ماء (maaʾ, "water"), حليب (ḥaliib, "milk"), شاي (shaay, "tea"), قهوة (qahwa, "coffee"), ثمن (thaman, "price"), رخيص (rakhiiṣ, "cheap"), غالٍ (ghaalin, "expensive").

Asking directions uses أين…؟ (ayna…?, "Where is…?"): أين المترو؟ ("Where is the metro?") أين المحطة؟ ("Where is the station?") أين الحمام؟ ("Where is the bathroom?") Understand the common answers: إلى الأمام (ilaa al-amaam, "straight ahead"), إلى اليسار (ilaa al-yasaar, "to the left"), إلى اليمين (ilaa al-yamiin, "to the right"), قريب (qariib, "near"), بعيد (baʿiid, "far"). If you do not understand, say لا أفهم, and ask تكلّم ببطء من فضلك, or ask someone to show you: أرني من فضلك (arinii min faḍlik, "Show me, please", to a man).

In a café or restaurant: القائمة من فضلك (al-qaaʾima min faḍlik, "The menu, please"), أريد شاياً من فضلك ("I would like tea, please"), الحساب من فضلك (al-ḥisaab min faḍlik, "The bill, please"). شكراً after paying, and مع السلامة when leaving. If something is wrong, stay polite: عفواً, هذا ليس طلبي (ʿafwan, haadhaa laysa ṭalabii, "Excuse me, this is not my order") is far more effective than a complaint without عفواً.

A complete short exchange brings the course together. Visitor: "السلام عليكم! بكم هذا الكتاب؟" Shopkeeper: "خمسون جنيهاً." Visitor: "حسناً. أريد هذا الكتاب. شكراً!" Shopkeeper: "عفواً. مع السلامة!" Visitor: "مع السلامة!" Notice how much of this you already know: greetings and thanks from Lesson 2, gender and the definite article from Lesson 3, present-tense verbs from Lesson 4, adjectives from Lesson 5, numbers from Lesson 7, and the verb أريد from this lesson. Learning a language is largely learning to recombine a small set of patterns in new situations, and that recombination is what this lesson practises.

Common mistakes at this stage are reverting to English word order under pressure, forgetting adjective-after-noun order (جديد الكتاب instead of الكتاب الجديد), and freezing when you do not understand every word of an answer. You do not need every word: listen for numbers, direction words, and نعم/لا, and ask for repetition or slower speech when you need it. Communication, not perfection, is the goal of an everyday conversation.

**Practice:** Write and rehearse three complete dialogues: buying a book in a shop, asking for directions to the metro and understanding the answer, and ordering tea in a café and asking for the bill. Each dialogue must include a greeting, a polite request, a number or a price, and a goodbye. Perform them aloud, swapping roles if you have a partner, and then change one detail in each — a different item, a different destination, a different drink — and perform them again without rewriting the whole dialogue.

**Handling a misunderstanding:** If a shopkeeper answers with a long sentence you do not follow, do not pretend to understand. Say عفواً, لا أفهم, then ask a narrower question: بكم هذا؟ or هل هذا الكتاب؟ A yes-or-no question is easier to answer and easier to understand than an open one. Pointing at the item while asking also removes ambiguity. A short, polite repair — excuse me, I don't understand, please speak slowly — keeps the conversation going far better than silence or a guessed answer, and it is a skill worth practising as deliberately as any verb form.
