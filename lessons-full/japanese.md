## Lesson 1: Sounds, Greetings, and Being Polite

Japanese has three writing systems, but this lesson starts with sound and speech, because you can speak before you can read everything. The two sound-based alphabets are hiragana and katakana. Hiragana is used for native Japanese words and grammar. Katakana is used mainly for foreign words, names, and emphasis. Kanji are characters borrowed from Chinese and used for many nouns, verbs, and adjectives. You do not need to master all three systems in this lesson. You need a reliable set of greetings and a clear sense of pronunciation.

Japanese pronunciation is regular. Each vowel has one main sound: a sounds like the a in father, i like the ee in see, u like the oo in boot but with lips less rounded, e like the e in bed, and o like the o in go but shorter. Consonants combine with vowels to make syllables such as ka, ki, ku, ke, ko. There are no silent letters in the way English has, and stress is not like English stress. Instead, Japanese uses pitch: the voice rises and falls across a word. Beginners are usually understood even if pitch is not perfect, so focus first on clear vowels and clean syllables. Do not add an extra vowel at the end of English words when you say Japanese words, and do not swallow the u in desu completely at first; saying de-su clearly is safer than guessing a reduced form.

Start with these greetings. Ohayou gozaimasu means good morning in polite speech. Konnichiwa means good afternoon, and it is also used as a general hello during the day. Konbanwa means good evening. Oyasumi nasai means good night when someone is going to sleep. Sayounara means goodbye, but in daily life people often say jaa mata, meaning see you later, or shitsurei shimasu when leaving a workplace or a formal setting. Arigatou gozaimasu means thank you very much. Sumimasen is very useful: it can mean excuse me, sorry, and thank you in situations where someone has helped you. Hai means yes, and iie means no, although in conversation people often answer with a fuller sentence rather than a bare hai or iie.

Politeness matters in Japanese. The ending desu makes a sentence polite and neutral. Watashi wa Tanaka desu means I am Tanaka, with watashi meaning I and wa marking the topic. You do not need to say watashi in every sentence; Japanese often leaves out a subject that is clear from context. For example, if someone asks your name, Tanaka desu is enough. Bowing accompanies greetings, and a small nod is a safe starting point. Do not use casual forms with a teacher, a customer, or someone older until you are invited to do so. The polite form in this course is the default, and casual forms will be introduced later and labelled as casual.

A common mistake is to translate word for word from English. Konnichiwa is not used at all hours, and ohayou gozaimasu is not a general hello at night. Another mistake is to say anata, meaning you, too often. Japanese usually uses a person's name plus san, as in Tanaka-san, rather than you. San is a polite title, similar to Mr. or Ms., and it is safer than guessing a first name. Do not attach san to your own name when introducing yourself; that sounds odd, because san is for the other person.

Practice exercise: Say each greeting aloud three times, slowly, then at normal speed. Write a short self-introduction: your name, followed by desu, then yoroshiku onegaishimasu, a polite phrase used when meeting someone that roughly means please treat me well. Then practise a short exchange with a partner or aloud alone: A: Konnichiwa. B: Konnichiwa. A: Tanaka desu. Yoroshiku onegaishimasu. B: Yoroshiku onegaishimasu. Record yourself if you can, and check that each vowel is clear and that you are not rushing the final syllable.

## Lesson 2: Hiragana Basics, Numbers 1–10, and Counting People and Things

Hiragana has 46 basic characters. Each character stands for a syllable, not a single letter. This lesson introduces the first two rows and the vowel row, and it teaches numbers 1 to 10, because numbers appear in prices, times, ages, and counting from the very start.

The vowels are a, i, u, e, o. In hiragana they are written あ, い, う, え, お. The k row is か (ka), き (ki), く (ku), け (ke), こ (ko). The s row is さ (sa), し (shi), す (su), せ (se), そ (so). Notice that し is pronounced shi, not si. That is a regular pattern in Japanese romanisation: some sounds change when combined. The t row is た (ta), ち (chi), つ (tsu), て (te), と (to). Again, ち is chi and つ is tsu, not ti and tu. Learning these changes early prevents a habit of reading Japanese as if it were English spelling.

Practise reading by sound, not by translating each character into English letters in your head forever. Cover the romanisation, look at あ, and say a. Then look at か and say ka. Short, repeated reading sessions work better than one long session. Write each character several times, following a standard stroke order if you have a chart, and say the sound as you write. Writing and speaking together helps memory more than silent reading alone.

Numbers 1 to 10 are: 1 ichi, 2 ni, 3 san, 4 yon or shi, 5 go, 6 roku, 7 nana or shichi, 8 hachi, 9 kyuu or ku, 10 juu. You will meet two readings for 4, 7, and 9. Yon is the common choice for 4 in counting, because shi also sounds like the word for death and is avoided in some contexts. Nana is common for 7 in counting and telephone numbers. Kyuu is common for 9. Do not worry about every alternative yet; learn the main form first, then notice the others when they appear.

Counting in Japanese is more than a number. Different kinds of things use different counters. For general small objects, the counter is ko: ikko for one, niko for two, sanko for three. For people, the counter is nin: hitori for one person, futari for two people, sannin for three people. Notice that one and two people are irregular: hitori and futari, not ichinin and ninin. For long thin objects, such as pencils, the counter is hon: ippon, nihon, sanbon. These sound changes are regular in context, and you will learn them by example rather than by memorising a rule in the abstract.

Worked examples: Ringo ga ikko arimasu means there is one apple, with ringo meaning apple and arimasu meaning there is for an inanimate thing. Hon ga nihon arimasu means there are two pens or pencils. Hito ga sannin imasu means there are three people, with imasu used for living beings and arimasu for things. That distinction between imasu and arimasu is important, and it returns in later lessons. For now, remember: people and animals take imasu, objects and places take arimasu.

A common mistake is to use a bare number where Japanese needs a counter. Saying ringo san is incomplete in the same way that saying apple three is incomplete in English; you need sanko or mittsu, the general counter form, depending on the sentence. Another mistake is to pronounce roku as locku or to stress the first syllable heavily. Keep vowels even and short, except for long vowels marked in romanisation with a line or a doubled vowel, such as kyuu.

Practice exercise: Count aloud from 1 to 10, then backwards. Then make ten sentences: five with ko for objects and five with nin for people, using the vocabulary in this lesson. For example: Hon ga ippon arimasu. Hito ga futari imasu. Check your counter forms, then read your sentences aloud and ask: did I use imasu for people and arimasu for things? Write the hiragana for a, i, u, e, o and ka, ki, ku, ke, ko from memory, then check them against a chart and correct any stroke or sound errors.

## Lesson 3: Particles Wa, Ga, No, and Simple Sentences

Particles are small words that follow a noun and show its job in the sentence. They are one of the most important parts of Japanese grammar, and they are also one of the places where learners make the most mistakes, because English shows the same jobs with word order instead. In Japanese, word order is flexible, but particles are not optional. Learning particles early makes later sentences much clearer.

Wa marks the topic: what the sentence is about. Watashi wa gakusei desu means as for me, I am a student. Ga marks the subject in a narrower sense, often for new information, existence, or ability. Neko ga imasu means there is a cat, with neko meaning cat. In a sentence like Watashi wa neko ga suki desu, meaning I like cats, wa marks the topic, I, and ga marks what is liked. Do not treat wa and ga as interchangeable. A useful starting rule is: wa introduces the topic you are talking about, and ga points to the specific thing that does, is, or is liked in that sentence. You will refine this distinction over time; at this stage, notice which particle is used in each example and copy the pattern.

No shows possession or description, similar to of or 's in English. Watashi no hon means my book. Tanaka-san no kuruma means Mr. Tanaka's car, with kuruma meaning car. No can also link a description to a noun: Nihon no tabemono means Japanese food, with Nihon meaning Japan and tabemono meaning food. To make a question about possession, you can say Kore wa dare no desu ka, meaning whose is this, with kore meaning this and dare meaning who. The question particle ka at the end turns a statement into a question. Watashi wa gakusei desu becomes Watashi wa gakusei desu ka, meaning am I a student, or are you a student, depending on context. Japanese questions often drop the subject, so context does the work.

Basic sentence patterns in this lesson are: noun + wa + noun + desu, for identity and description; noun + ga + arimasu or imasu, for existence; and noun + no + noun, for possession. Examples: Kore wa hon desu, this is a book. Sore wa pen desu, that is a pen. Are wa kuruma desu, that over there is a car. Kore, sore, and are mean this near me, that near you, and that over there, away from both of us. They are a set, and mixing them up is a common error. Point at an object near you and say kore; point at an object near your partner and say sore; point at a distant object and say are.

Adjectives also use desu. Ookii means big, chiisai means small, atarashii means new, furui means old. Kore wa ookii desu means this is big. To say something is not, use dewa arimasen in polite speech: Kore wa ookii dewa arimasen means this is not big. For nouns, the negative is similar: Watashi wa sensei dewa arimasen means I am not a teacher, with sensei meaning teacher. Do not add not as a separate English word; use the Japanese negative pattern.

A common mistake is to leave out wa because English does not need a topic marker. Watashi gakusei desu sounds incomplete to a Japanese listener, even if the meaning is guessed. Another mistake is to use no for every link between words. No links nouns, but it does not replace all English prepositions. Later lessons introduce ni, de, e, and kara, each with its own job. For now, use no only for possession and noun description, and use wa and ga as shown in the examples.

Practice exercise: Write twelve sentences. Four should use wa + desu for identity, four should use no for possession, and four should use ga + imasu or arimasu for existence. Then turn four of them into questions with ka and answer them with hai or iie plus a full sentence. For example: Q: Kore wa hon desu ka. A: Hai, hon desu. Or: Iie, hon dewa arimasen. Pen desu. Read each pair aloud, and check that every noun that needs a particle has one.

## Lesson 4: Verbs in the Present, Masu Form, and Daily Routine

Japanese verbs in polite speech usually end in masu in the present tense. This form is safe for most everyday situations, and it is the form used throughout this course until casual forms are introduced. The present masu form also covers the near future in Japanese, so Ashita ikimasu can mean I will go tomorrow, with ashita meaning tomorrow and ikimasu meaning go.

Three useful verb groups appear at this stage, but you do not need to memorise group theory to start speaking. Group one verbs change their final vowel before masu: kaku, to write, becomes kakimasu; yomu, to read, becomes yomimasu; hanasu, to speak, becomes hanashimasu. Group two verbs drop ru and add masu: taberu, to eat, becomes tabemasu; miru, to see or watch, becomes mimasu. The irregular verbs are suru, to do, which becomes shimasu, and kuru, to come, which becomes kimasu. Benkyou shimasu means to study, combining benkyou, study, with shimasu. That pattern, noun + shimasu, is very productive: undou shimasu means to exercise, and shigoto shimasu means to work.

Word order in a basic verb sentence is subject + object + verb, with the object marked by o, pronounced wo in this particle. Watashi wa hon o yomimasu means I read a book. Watashi wa gohan o tabemasu means I eat a meal, with gohan meaning rice or a meal. The verb comes last, which feels backwards at first for English speakers, but it is consistent. Do not move the verb to the middle because English does. Keep the verb at the end, and let particles show who does what.

Time words usually come near the start of the sentence, after the topic. Kyou means today, ashita means tomorrow, kinou means yesterday. Mainichi means every day. Asa means morning, hiru means midday, yoru means night. Examples: Watashi wa mainichi benkyou shimasu, I study every day. Watashi wa asa gohan o tabemasu, I eat breakfast in the morning. Watashi wa yoru hon o yomimasu, I read a book at night. To make a negative, change masu to masen: Watashi wa niku o tabemasen means I do not eat meat, with niku meaning meat. To ask a question, add ka: Watashi wa ashita ikimasu ka is not a question about yourself unless you are confirming; more naturally, Ashita ikimasu ka means will you go tomorrow, with the subject understood from context.

Daily routine vocabulary in this lesson includes: okimasu, to get up; nemasu, to sleep; hatarakimasu, to work; kaerimasu, to return home; aimasu, to meet; kaimasu, to buy. A short routine might be: Watashi wa asa shichiji ni okimasu, I get up at seven in the morning, using ni for a specific time. That time pattern will be expanded in Lesson 5, but you can already use it with clock times. Watashi wa gakkou e ikimasu means I go to school, with e marking direction and gakkou meaning school. Watashi wa uchi e kaerimasu means I return home, with uchi meaning home.

A common mistake is to use the dictionary form, such as taberu, in a polite conversation where tabemasu is expected. The dictionary form is useful for study and for later grammar, but masu is the safe default for speaking politely. Another mistake is to forget o before the object, producing Watashi wa hon yomimasu, which sounds incomplete. A third mistake is to add a subject pronoun in every sentence. Once the topic is clear, Japanese drops it: Gohan o tabemasu is enough when everyone knows who is eating.

Practice exercise: Write your daily routine in eight sentences, using at least six different masu verbs, time words, and the o particle. Include one negative sentence and one question. Then read the routine aloud, keeping the verb at the end of each sentence. As a second task, convert five dictionary forms into masu forms: kaku, yomu, taberu, miru, suru. Check them against the patterns in this lesson, and note which verbs changed their vowel and which simply dropped ru.

## Lesson 5: Time, Days, Places, and Particles Ni, De, E, Kara, Made

Time and place need precise particles, and this lesson gives you the set you will use every day. Ni marks a specific point in time and a destination or location for existence. De marks the place where an action happens. E marks direction of movement. Kara means from, and made means until or up to. Mixing ni and de is the most common error in this area, so learn them as a pair: ni for when and where something is, de for where something is done.

Days of the week are: getsuyoubi Monday, kayoubi Tuesday, suiyoubi Wednesday, mokuyoubi Thursday, kinyoubi Friday, doyoubi Saturday, nichiyoubi Sunday. Bi means day, and the first character shows the element: getsu for moon, ka for fire, sui for water, moku for wood, kin for gold, do for earth, nichi for sun. Months use numbers plus gatsu: ichigatsu January, nigatsu February, sangatsu March, shigatsu April, gogatsu May, rokugatsu June, shichigatsu July, hachigatsu August, kugatsu September, juugatsu October, juuichigatsu November, juunigatsu December. Notice the sound changes in shigatsu, shichigatsu, and kugatsu; they follow the same pattern as numbers and are worth learning as set forms.

Clock time uses ji for hour and fun or pun for minute, with the reading changing after certain numbers. Ichiji means one o'clock, niji two o'clock, sanji three o'clock. For minutes, ippun means one minute, nifun two minutes, sanpun three minutes, yonpun four minutes, gofun five minutes. Han means half, so sanji han means half past three. To say at a time, add ni: shichiji ni okimasu, I get up at seven. To ask the time, say Nanji desu ka, what time is it, with nan meaning what and ji meaning hour. Nan is also used for what day: Nanyoubi desu ka, what day of the week is it.

Place vocabulary includes: gakkou school, kaisha company or office, mise shop, eki station, byouin hospital, ginkou bank, toshokan library, kouen park, resutoran restaurant, uchi home. Examples with particles: Watashi wa gakkou de benkyou shimasu, I study at school, using de because studying is an action done at school. Watashi wa gakkou ni imasu, I am at school, using ni because this is existence, not an action. Watashi wa gakkou e ikimasu, I go to school, using e for direction. Watashi wa uchi kara gakkou e ikimasu, I go from home to school. Watashi wa kuji kara goji made hatarakimasu, I work from nine until five, with kuji meaning nine o'clock and goji five o'clock.

Kara and made also work for places and times in questions: Doko kara kimashita ka means where did you come from, with doko meaning where and kimashita the past form of kimasu, to come. Past tense in masu form changes masu to mashita: ikimashita went, tabemashita ate, mimashita saw. That past pattern is introduced here because talking about where you came from naturally needs it, and it will be developed in Lesson 6. For now, use mashita for completed actions and masu for present and future.

A common mistake is to say Watashi wa gakkou ni benkyou shimasu, using ni for the place of an action. That sounds wrong because benkyou suru is an action, so de is needed. Another mistake is to use de for a destination: gakkou de ikimasu is wrong; use e or ni for going to a place. A third mistake is to put made without kara when a start point is meant, or to forget that made means up to, not only until in a vague sense. Kuji made means up to nine, not after nine.

Practice exercise: Write a timetable for your day with six time points, using ji, fun where needed, and ni. Then write six place sentences: two with de for actions, two with ni for being somewhere, and two with e or kara and made for movement. Ask and answer three questions: Nanji desu ka, Nanyoubi desu ka, and Doko e ikimasu ka, meaning where are you going. Read the whole set aloud, and underline each particle, checking that de, ni, and e are used for action, existence, and direction respectively.

## Lesson 6: Past Tense, Adjectives I and Na, and Describing Things

Past tense lets you talk about what happened, and adjectives let you describe what you saw. Together they make your Japanese much more useful, because you can now report, compare, and explain, not only state present facts.

In polite speech, the past of masu verbs is mashita: tabemasu becomes tabemashita, ate; ikimasu becomes ikimashita, went; yomimasu becomes yomimashita, read. The negative past is masen deshita: tabemasen deshita means did not eat. For desu sentences, the past is deshita: Gakusei deshita means was a student. The negative past of desu is dewa arimasen deshita: Gakusei dewa arimasen deshita means was not a student. Examples: Kinou, watashi wa eiga o mimashita, yesterday I watched a film, with eiga meaning film. Kinou, watashi wa gakkou e ikimasen deshita, yesterday I did not go to school. Senshuu means last week, sengetsu last month, kyonen last year, and these time words work in the same position as kyou and ashita.

Japanese adjectives fall into two groups. I-adjectives end in i and change their ending: ookii big becomes ookikatta in the past, meaning was big; chiisai small becomes chiisakatta, was small; atarashii new becomes atarashikatta, was new. The negative present of an i-adjective changes i to kunai: ookikunai means not big, and the negative past is kunakatta: ookikunakatta means was not big. Examples: Kono hon wa omoshiroi desu, this book is interesting, with omoshiroi meaning interesting. Kono hon wa omoshirokunakatta desu, this book was not interesting. Note that desu is often kept after the adjective in polite speech even though the adjective itself already changes; that combination is normal and safe for learners.

Na-adjectives need na before a noun and desu after, without na, in a sentence ending: kirei means beautiful or clean, and kirei na hana means a beautiful flower, with hana meaning flower. Kirei desu means it is beautiful. The negative is dewa arimasen: Kirei dewa arimasen means it is not beautiful. The past is deshita: Kirei deshita means it was beautiful. Other na-adjectives include: yuumei famous, benri convenient, shizuka quiet, genki healthy or energetic. Examples: Kono machi wa shizuka desu, this town is quiet, with machi meaning town. Ano resutoran wa yuumei desu, that restaurant is famous.

To link adjectives to nouns, remember the difference: i-adjectives go directly before the noun, as in ookii hon, a big book, and atarashii kuruma, a new car. Na-adjectives need na, as in kirei na hana and benri na eki, a convenient station. To say very, use totemo: Totemo ookii desu means it is very big. To say a little, use sukoshi: Sukoshi chiisai desu means it is a little small. To compare, use yori for than: Kono hon wa ano hon yori omoshiroi desu, this book is more interesting than that book. That pattern is useful and regular, and it avoids needing a separate comparative adjective form as in English.

A common mistake is to treat a na-adjective like an i-adjective and say kirei hon, without na. Another is to add na to an i-adjective, saying ookii na hon, which is wrong. A third mistake is to change the past of a na-adjective as if it were an i-adjective; na-adjectives do not change their stem, they use deshita. A fourth mistake is to forget that i-adjectives already carry tense, and to say ookikatta deshita, doubling the past. Use ookikatta desu for the polite past, not a double past.

Practice exercise: Write ten sentences: three in the past with mashita, three describing things with i-adjectives, three with na-adjectives, and one comparison with yori. Then convert three present sentences into negative past sentences, for example changing Omoshiroi desu into Omoshirokunakatta desu. Read them aloud and check each adjective: does it end in i and change, or does it need na and deshita? That check will catch most errors before they become habits.

## Lesson 7: Wanting, Liking, Ability, and Making Requests

This lesson adds the language of preference and polite requests, which you need for shopping, eating out, travelling, and asking for help. Japanese expresses wanting, liking, and ability with patterns that look different from English, so learn the pattern, not just the word.

Suki means liked, and kirai means disliked, but they behave like adjectives with ga, not like verbs with o. Watashi wa neko ga suki desu means I like cats. Watashi wa sakana ga kirai desu means I dislike fish, with sakana meaning fish. To ask what someone likes, say Nani ga suki desu ka, what do you like, with nani meaning what. To say you like doing something, use the verb in its dictionary form plus no ga suki: Hon o yomu no ga suki desu means I like reading books. That no turns the verb phrase into a noun-like thing that can be liked. Do not say Hon o yomu o suki desu; the pattern needs no ga.

Wanting a thing uses hoshii: Watashi wa atarashii hon ga hoshii desu means I want a new book. Wanting to do something uses the verb stem plus tai: Tabetai desu means I want to eat, from taberu. Ikitai desu means I want to go, from iku. Miritai desu means I want to see or watch, from miru. Tai behaves like an i-adjective: the negative is takunai, as in Ikitakunai desu, I do not want to go. Tai is for your own wants in a statement; when asking about someone else's wants, use tai desu ka, as in Nani o tabetai desu ka, what do you want to eat. Do not use tai to order someone else to do something; it describes a wish, not a command.

Ability uses dekiru, to be able to: Nihongo ga dekimasu means I can speak Japanese, with Nihongo meaning Japanese language. Eigo ga dekimasu means I can speak English. To ask if someone can do something, use the verb plus koto ga dekimasu: Kanji o kaku koto ga dekimasu ka means can you write kanji. Koto, like no, turns a verb phrase into a thing that can be talked about. A simpler pattern for language ability is just language + ga dekimasu, which is enough for most introductions.

Requests in polite speech often use the te form of the verb plus kudasai, meaning please do. The te form is introduced here in its most useful pattern, and it will be developed fully in Lesson 8. For now, learn these set forms: Mite kudasai means please look, from miru. Kiite kudasai means please listen, from kiku, to listen. Yonde kudasai means please read, from yomu. Itte kudasai means please say, from iu, to say. Matte kudasai means please wait, from matsu, to wait. To ask permission, use te mo ii desu ka: Mite mo ii desu ka means may I look. To refuse permission politely, the pattern is te wa ikemasen: Haitte wa ikemasen means you must not enter, from hairu, to enter. These forms are polite and safe, and they are much better than a bare command in a shop, classroom, or workplace.

Shopping vocabulary helps here: ikura means how much, kore how much is this, Kore wa ikura desu ka. Yasui means cheap, takai means expensive or high. Motto means more. Sukoshi means a little. Examples: Kore wa yasui desu, this is cheap. Motto ookii no ga hoshii desu, I want a bigger one, with no standing for one. Kore o misete kudasai means please show me this. That sentence combines kore, o, misete from miseru, to show, and kudasai, and it is a sentence you can use in a real shop.

A common mistake is to say Watashi wa hon o suki desu, using o with suki. Suki needs ga. Another mistake is to use hoshii for wanting to do something; hoshii is for things, tai is for actions. A third mistake is to use tai as a direct order: Tabetai desu means I want to eat, not eat this. If you want to ask someone to do something, use te kudasai, not tai. A fourth mistake is to answer a liking question with only hai, when a fuller answer is more natural: Hai, suki desu is better than a bare hai in a conversation.

Practice exercise: Write a short dialogue in a shop or restaurant with ten lines. Include one suki question and answer, one hoshii sentence, two tai sentences, one dekimasu sentence, and two te kudasai requests. Then write five sentences about your own likes, wants, and abilities, and check each one: suki with ga, hoshii for a thing, tai for an action, dekimasu for ability. Read the dialogue aloud with a partner or alone, taking both parts, and practise the polite tone, not a rushed or commanding tone.

## Lesson 8: Te Form, Connecting Actions, and Putting It All Together

The te form connects verbs, and it is the key to speaking in longer, natural sentences. Once you can use te, you can say what you did and then what you did next, ask someone to do something, and describe a sequence without stopping after every short sentence. This lesson introduces the te form patterns and then brings the whole course together in a review task.

Te forms follow patterns by verb group. For group two verbs, drop ru and add te: taberu becomes tabete, miru becomes mite. For group one verbs, the ending changes: kaku becomes kaite, yomu becomes yonde, hanasu becomes hanashite, kiku becomes kiite, matsu becomes matte, kau, to buy, becomes katte. For the irregular verbs, suru becomes shite, and kuru becomes kite. Benkyou suru becomes benkyou shite. These changes are regular within their groups, but they must be learned by example at first. Do not guess a te form from the masu form alone; check the group pattern and the examples in this lesson.

The most common use of te is to join actions in order: Asa okite, gohan o tabete, gakkou e ikimashita means in the morning I got up, ate a meal, and went to school. The te verbs are not in the past themselves; the final verb carries the tense. If the final verb is masu, the whole sequence is present or future: Okite, gohan o tabete, gakkou e ikimasu means I get up, eat, and go to school. If the final verb is mashita, the whole sequence is past. That rule simplifies long sentences: only the last verb needs a tense ending.

Te plus kudasai makes a polite request, as in Lesson 7: Mite kudasai, please look; Matte kudasai, please wait; Yonde kudasai, please read. Te plus imasu describes an action in progress or a continuing state: Hon o yonde imasu means I am reading a book. Nihongo o benkyou shite imasu means I am studying Japanese. That pattern is very common, and it is different from the simple present: Yomimasu can mean I read in general, while Yonde imasu means I am reading now or I am in the middle of reading. Te plus mo ii desu ka asks permission: Haitte mo ii desu ka means may I enter. Te plus wa ikemasen forbids: Haitte wa ikemasen means you must not enter.

Te also links a resulting state: Tsukarete imasu means I am tired, from tsukareru, to get tired. That is a state resulting from an action, and it shows why te plus imasu is more than a present continuous tense. Other examples include: Mado ga aite imasu, the window is open, from aku, to open; Denki ga tsuite imasu, the light is on, from tsuku, to turn on. These are states, and they are natural in daily speech.

Now put the course together. A good short self-introduction at this level might be: Hajimemashite. Watashi wa Tanaka desu. Nihongo o benkyou shite imasu. Hon o yomu no ga suki desu. Mainichi, asa okite, gohan o tabete, gakkou e ikimasu. Kinou, tomodachi to eiga o mimashita. Totemo omoshirokatta desu. Yoroshiku onegaishimasu. That paragraph uses greetings, desu, suki with no ga, te for sequence, past tense, an i-adjective in the past, and a polite closing. It is not a test of every grammar point; it is a model of how the pieces fit in real speech. Tomodachi means friend, and to after a noun means with: Tomodachi to means with a friend.

A common mistake is to put a tense ending on every verb in a sequence, producing Okimashita, tabemashita, ikimashita as three separate past sentences when a te sequence would be smoother. Separate sentences are not wrong, but te is more natural for a sequence. Another mistake is to use te kudasai as a casual command without kudasai, which can sound rude. Keep kudasai in polite requests. A third mistake is to confuse te imasu for a habit with te imasu for an action now; context usually makes it clear, and adding ima, now, or mainichi, every day, helps: Ima, hon o yonde imasu means I am reading now.

Practice exercise, final review: Write a 150-word piece in Japanese, using romanisation or hiragana as you prefer, that includes: a greeting and introduction, a daily routine with three te-linked actions, a past event with one i-adjective and one na-adjective, a liking sentence, a want sentence, and a polite request. Then read it aloud, check every particle, and correct any missing wa, ga, o, ni, de, or e. As a second task, list ten verbs you now know in dictionary form, masu form, te form, and past mashita form. That table is your revision card for the next stage of study. Keep studying in short, regular sessions, review hiragana daily, and add new vocabulary only when you can use it in a full sentence, not as an isolated word list.
