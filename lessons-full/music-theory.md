# Music Theory

*Local draft lesson text for Planet Leads Academy. This is study material only.*

## Lesson 1: Notes, Pitch, and the Staff

Music theory begins with a simple question: how do we write down a sound so that someone else can reproduce it? Pitch is how high or low a sound feels. A note is a written symbol for a pitch of a particular length. The staff is the set of five horizontal lines on which notes are placed, and a note's vertical position on or between those lines tells you its pitch: higher on the staff means higher in pitch.

Western music names pitches with seven letters, A through G, which then repeat: after G comes A again, an octave higher. An octave is the distance between a pitch and the next pitch with the same letter name, and the two sound similar in a special way — a melody played an octave higher is recognisably the same melody. The piano keyboard makes this visible: groups of two and three black keys repeat across the keyboard, and the white key immediately to the left of each group of two black keys is C. Find C on a keyboard or a keyboard diagram and you have an anchor for everything else.

The treble clef, also called the G clef, is used for higher pitches and curls around the second line from the bottom, which is G. Notes on the lines of the treble staff, from bottom to top, are E, G, B, D, F — remembered by "Every Good Boy Does Fine". Notes in the spaces, from bottom to top, are F, A, C, E, which spells "FACE". The bass clef, or F clef, is used for lower pitches; its two dots surround the fourth line from the bottom, which is F. Its lines are G, B, D, F, A ("Good Boys Do Fine Always") and its spaces are A, C, E, G ("All Cows Eat Grass").

When a pitch falls above or below the staff, short extra lines called ledger lines extend the staff. Middle C sits on a ledger line just below the treble staff and just above the bass staff, and it is the meeting point of the two: the same pitch written in either clef. Notes also carry accidentals: a sharp (♯) raises a pitch by one semitone, a flat (♭) lowers it by one semitone, and a natural (♮) cancels a previous sharp or flat. A semitone is the smallest step in this system, the distance between neighbouring keys on a piano, black or white.

Reading music is a skill built by repetition, not by memorising a diagram once. Work in both directions: given a note on the staff, name it; given a name, place it on the staff. Start with a small range around middle C and widen it gradually, and say the names aloud or sing them, because connecting the symbol, the name, and the sound makes the learning stick far better than silent staring.

Common mistakes include reading only lines or only spaces and guessing the other, confusing the treble and bass mnemonics, and treating a sharp as a different note name rather than a raised version of a note (F♯ is still an F, raised). Another is ignoring the clef: the same position on the staff means a different pitch in treble and bass clef, so always check the clef first.

**Practice:** On a blank treble staff, write the notes C, E, G, C (an octave higher), and A in order, then read them back without looking at your answers. Repeat with a bass staff using C, G, E, and A below middle C. Then find those pitches on a piano keyboard, real or pictured, and play or point to each one while saying its name. Do this for five minutes a day for a week, widening the range by one note at each end when the current range feels easy.

## Lesson 2: Rhythm, Beat, and Metre

If pitch answers "which note?", rhythm answers "when, and for how long?" Music moves in time, and rhythm is the organisation of sounds in time. The beat is the steady pulse you would tap your foot to. Most music you hear has a regular beat, and rhythm is built by dividing that beat and grouping beats into patterns.

Note values describe relative lengths. A semibreve (whole note) is an open note head with no stem. A minim (half note) is an open note head with a stem. A crotchet (quarter note) is a filled note head with a stem. A quaver (eighth note) is a filled note head with a stem and one flag or beam. Each step down halves the length: one semibreve equals two minims, four crotchets, or eight quavers. A dot after a note increases its length by half: a dotted crotchet lasts as long as a crotchet plus a quaver. A rest is silence of a specified length, with its own symbols matching each note value, and rests matter as much as notes — a well-placed silence shapes a phrase just as a sound does.

Metre groups beats into bars (measures). The time signature at the start of a piece tells you the grouping: the top number says how many beats are in each bar, and the bottom number says which note value gets one beat. In 4/4, there are four crotchet beats per bar — the most common metre in popular music. In 3/4, there are three crotchet beats per bar, giving the swaying feel of a waltz. In 6/8, there are six quaver beats per bar, usually felt as two groups of three, which is why 6/8 feels different from 3/4 even though both contain six quavers. Count 6/8 as "ONE-two-three, TWO-two-three" to feel its two main pulses.

Beams join quavers and shorter notes together in groups that show the beats. Good beaming is not cosmetic: it lets a reader see at a glance where each beat falls. In 4/4, quavers are normally beamed in groups that add up to one crotchet beat, and longer groups must not hide the halfway point of the bar. When you write rhythm, check that each bar adds up to exactly the number of beats the time signature requires — no more, no less. Counting aloud while clapping is the most reliable check: if you cannot count it steadily, it is not yet clear.

Syncopation is emphasis on a part of the bar that is not normally accented. In 4/4, beats 1 and 3 are naturally stronger than 2 and 4. Placing a strong sound on a weak beat, or just before a strong beat, creates the off-balance energy heard in much jazz, funk, and popular music. Syncopation works because the listener still feels the underlying steady beat; remove that steady pulse and syncopation just sounds like mistakes.

Common mistakes include confusing the time signature's numbers with a fraction (3/4 does not mean "three quarters of something"), forgetting that a dotted note is longer by half rather than twice as long, and writing bars that do not add up. Another is clapping a rhythm at a changing speed: keep the pulse steady even when the notes get faster or slower, using a metronome at a slow tempo if needed.

**Practice:** Set a metronome or a steady tapping pulse at a slow tempo. Clap the following in 4/4, counting "1, 2, 3, 4" throughout: bar 1, four crotchets; bar 2, a minim then two crotchets; bar 3, a dotted crotchet, a quaver, and two crotchets; bar 4, a crotchet rest then three crotchets. Then write a four-bar rhythm of your own in 3/4, check each bar adds up to three crotchet beats, and clap it steadily. Repeat until you can clap it without hesitating at the bar lines.

## Lesson 3: Major Scales and Key Signatures

A scale is an ordered set of pitches, usually spanning an octave, that gives a piece its raw material. The major scale is the most familiar: sing "do, re, mi, fa, sol, la, ti, do" and you have sung a major scale. Its pattern of steps, measured in tones (whole steps, two semitones) and semitones (half steps), is: tone, tone, semitone, tone, tone, tone, semitone. In C major, which uses only white keys, that pattern gives C, D, E, F, G, A, B, C. The semitones fall between the 3rd and 4th notes (E–F) and the 7th and 8th notes (B–C), and that placement is what makes the scale sound "major" — bright and settled, to most listeners.

Every major scale follows the same pattern, but starting on a different note forces some notes to be sharpened or flattened to keep the pattern intact. Start on G: G, A, B, C, D, E, F, G would give a tone between E and F where a semitone is needed and a semitone between F and G where a tone is needed, so F must be raised to F♯, giving G major: G, A, B, C, D, E, F♯, G. Start on F: F, G, A, B, C, D, E, F needs a semitone between A and B, so B becomes B♭, giving F major. This is why key signatures exist: instead of writing a sharp or flat before every affected note, the sharps or flats are collected at the start of each staff line and apply throughout, until cancelled.

Key signatures follow a fixed order. Sharps are added in the order F♯, C♯, G♯, D♯, A♯, E♯, B♯, and flats in the order B♭, E♭, A♭, D♭, G♭, C♭, F♭. The circle of fifths organises keys by how many sharps or flats they have, each step adding one: C major has none, G major has one sharp, D major has two, and so on in one direction; F major has one flat, B♭ major has two, in the other. You do not need to memorise the whole circle at once, but understanding that it is systematic — not a list of unrelated facts — makes it far easier to learn.

Each note of a scale has a degree number and a name that describes its role. In C major: 1 is the tonic (C), the home note; 2 is the supertonic (D); 3 is the mediant (E); 4 is the subdominant (F); 5 is the dominant (G), the second most important note, which pulls strongly back toward the tonic; 6 is the submediant (A); 7 is the leading note (B), which pulls upward to the tonic because it is only a semitone below it. That pull — dominant to tonic, leading note to tonic — is the engine of much Western harmony, and you will meet it again in Lesson 7.

A common mistake is writing a scale with a repeated letter and a missing letter, such as G, A, B, C, D, E, G♭, G. Every scale in this system uses each letter name exactly once before repeating at the octave, so a correct scale has seven different letters. If your scale repeats a letter, a note is wrong. Another mistake is forgetting that a key signature applies to every octave of that pitch, not just the line or space where the sharp or flat is written.

**Practice:** Write out the major scales for C, G, D, F, and B♭, using the tone–tone–semitone–tone–tone–tone–semitone pattern and checking that each uses seven different letter names. Then identify the key signature of each: how many sharps or flats, and which ones. Finally, play or sing the C major and G major scales slowly, listening for the two semitone steps, and notice how the leading note (B in C major, F♯ in G major) feels as though it wants to rise to the tonic.

## Lesson 4: Minor Scales and Modes

Minor scales sound darker or sadder than major scales to many listeners, but the difference is structural, not merely emotional: the pattern of tones and semitones is different. The natural minor scale follows the pattern tone, semitone, tone, tone, semitone, tone, tone. In A minor, using only white keys, that gives A, B, C, D, E, F, G, A. Compare it with C major: the same notes, but starting on A, so the semitones fall in different places — between the 2nd and 3rd notes and the 5th and 6th notes. A minor and C major are relative major and minor: they share a key signature (in this case, none), and every major key has a relative minor starting on its 6th degree.

Composers found the natural minor's 7th degree unsatisfying for harmony, because it is a whole tone below the tonic and does not pull upward to it the way the major scale's leading note does. So two variants arose. The harmonic minor raises the 7th degree by a semitone, creating a leading note: A harmonic minor is A, B, C, D, E, F, G♯, A. The gap between F and G♯ is three semitones, an augmented second, which gives harmonic minor its distinctive, slightly exotic sound. The melodic minor raises both the 6th and 7th degrees when ascending (A, B, C, D, E, F♯, G♯, A) and returns to the natural form when descending (A, G, F, E, D, C, B, A), a convention from vocal music where the raised notes help the line rise smoothly and the natural notes help it fall. In modern practice, jazz and popular musicians often use the ascending form in both directions, so be aware that conventions differ by style.

Modes are scales built by starting the major scale on a different degree, which gives seven modes in total. The most useful beyond major (Ionian) and natural minor (Aeolian) are Dorian (starting on the 2nd degree: in C major's notes, D, E, F, G, A, B, C, D — minor-like, but with a raised 6th that makes it brighter than natural minor) and Mixolydian (starting on the 5th degree: G, A, B, C, D, E, F, G — major-like, but with a flattened 7th, common in folk, rock, and blues). You do not need all seven modes at once; Dorian and Mixolydian cover a great deal of folk and popular music, and recognising their characteristic note — the raised 6th in Dorian, the flattened 7th in Mixolydian — is more valuable than memorising mode names in the abstract.

A frequent mistake is treating minor as simply "major starting somewhere else" and missing that harmonic and melodic minor exist because of a real musical need — the pull to the tonic. Another is writing melodic minor the same ascending and descending without noting which convention you are following. Always label which minor form you mean when writing or analysing, because the notes differ.

**Practice:** Write A natural, harmonic, and melodic minor, and then D natural, harmonic, and melodic minor (remember D minor shares F major's key signature, one flat, B♭, and the harmonic form raises C to C♯). Listen to or play each form and describe in your own words how the raised 7th changes the feeling of the scale's ending. Then take the notes of C major and write the Dorian scale starting on D and the Mixolydian scale starting on G, marking the one note in each that differs from natural minor or major respectively.

**Hearing the difference:** Play A natural minor ascending (A, B, C, D, E, F, G, A) and then A harmonic minor (A, B, C, D, E, F, G#, A). The only change is the last step, yet the ending feels far more directed in the harmonic version, because G# pulls up to A as a leading note. Now play the melodic ascending form (A, B, C, D, E, F#, G#, A) and notice how the raised sixth smooths the jump between F and G#. Composers choose between these forms bar by bar, not once for a whole piece, so a single piece in A minor may use all three.

## Lesson 5: Intervals

An interval is the distance between two pitches, and it is the basic unit for describing melody and harmony. Intervals have two parts: a number, counting the letter names from the lower note to the upper note inclusive, and a quality, describing the exact size in semitones. From C to E is a third, because it spans three letter names (C, D, E); from C to G is a fifth (C, D, E, F, G). Always count letter names, not keys: C to F♯ is a fourth, even though F♯ is a black key, because it spans C, D, E, F.

Quality names depend on the interval's number. Unisons, fourths, fifths, and octaves are called perfect when they match the major scale's version: C to C is a perfect unison, C to F is a perfect fourth, C to G is a perfect fifth, C to C an octave higher is a perfect octave. Seconds, thirds, sixths, and sevenths are called major when they match the major scale: C to D is a major second, C to E is a major third, C to A is a major sixth, C to B is a major seventh. Lower a major interval by one semitone and it becomes minor: C to E♭ is a minor third, C to B♭ is a minor seventh. Raise a perfect or major interval by one semitone and it becomes augmented; lower a perfect or minor interval by one semitone and it becomes diminished. So C to F♯ is an augmented fourth, and C to G♭ is a diminished fifth. Both span the same number of semitones — six — but they are different intervals, spelled differently and used differently, and they sound the same on a piano. This distinction, between enharmonic equivalents (same sound, different spelling), is essential: theory cares about spelling, not only sound.

Intervals also have a melodic form (notes one after another) and a harmonic form (notes together). A melody is a sequence of melodic intervals, and recognising common ones by ear is a practical skill: a perfect fifth opening, like the start of "Twinkle, Twinkle, Little Star" (C to G), a major third, or an octave leap each have a recognisable character. Inversion turns an interval upside down: move the lower note up an octave, or the upper note down an octave, and the two intervals add up to nine. A major third inverts to a minor sixth, a perfect fifth to a perfect fourth, and major becomes minor, minor becomes major, perfect stays perfect, augmented becomes diminished, and vice versa. Inversion is useful because it means learning one interval teaches you its partner.

Common mistakes include counting keys instead of letter names, which mislabels intervals involving black keys; forgetting that quality is relative to the lower note's major scale, so E to G♯ is a major third (E major's third is G♯) while E to G is a minor third; and ignoring direction — intervals are normally measured upward from the lower note unless stated otherwise, and a descending interval needs to be described as such.

**Practice:** Identify these intervals, giving number and quality: C–E, C–E♭, D–A, F–B, G–B, A–C, B–F. Then invert each one and name the inversion. Finally, choose a familiar melody and work out its first five melodic intervals by counting letter names from the notes, checking your work by singing each interval slowly and listening for whether it sounds like a step (second), a skip (third or fourth), or a leap (fifth or more).

**A worked identification:** To identify D to B, first count letter names: D, E, F, G, A, B is six letters, so it is a sixth. In D major, the sixth note is B, so D to B is a major sixth. To identify D to Bb, the same count gives a sixth, but Bb is a semitone lower than B, so it is a minor sixth. Working in two steps — number first, then quality from the lower note's major scale — prevents the common error of guessing quality from the sound alone, which cannot distinguish, for example, an augmented fourth from a diminished fifth.

## Lesson 6: Triads and Seventh Chords

When three or more notes sound together, they form a chord. The most basic chord, the triad, is built by stacking thirds: take a root note, add the note a third above it, and add another third above that. In C major, stacking thirds on C gives C, E, G — a C major triad. The three notes have roles: the root (C) names the chord, the third (E) decides whether it is major or minor, and the fifth (G) stabilises it.

There are four types of triad. A major triad has a major third and a perfect fifth above the root: C, E, G. A minor triad has a minor third and a perfect fifth: C, E♭, G. A diminished triad has a minor third and a diminished fifth: C, E♭, G♭. An augmented triad has a major third and an augmented fifth: C, E, G♯. In a major key, the triads built on each scale degree follow a fixed pattern: I, IV, and V are major; ii, iii, and vi are minor; vii° is diminished. In C major: C, Dm, Em, F, G, Am, B°. Roman numerals are used for this analysis, with uppercase for major and lowercase for minor, and ° for diminished. This pattern is the same in every major key, transposed: in G major it is G, Am, Bm, C, D, Em, F♯°.

A triad does not have to have its root at the bottom. In first inversion, the third is the lowest note (E, G, C for a C major triad); in second inversion, the fifth is lowest (G, C, E). Inversions change the chord's stability and smoothness of movement between chords: a bass line that steps from C to D to E can be supported by chords in inversion that keep the other voices close, rather than jumping the whole chord each time. Figured bass symbols describe inversions compactly, but for this course, naming the chord and its inversion in words is enough.

Adding a fourth note a third above the triad's fifth gives a seventh chord. The most common is the dominant seventh, built on the dominant (V) of a major key: in C major, G, B, D, F. It contains a tritone — the augmented fourth/diminished fifth between B and F — which creates tension that resolves strongly to the tonic chord, and that tension-and-release is why dominant sevenths are so common at the ends of phrases. Other seventh chords include the major seventh (C, E, G, B, with a major seventh above the root) and the minor seventh (C, E♭, G, B♭), each with its own colour, widely used in jazz and popular music.

Common mistakes include building a chord from the wrong root (stacking thirds must start from the root and use consecutive letter names, so C, E, G is correct but C, E, A is not a C triad); confusing a chord's root with its lowest note when the chord is inverted; and writing a minor triad by flattening the wrong note — in a minor triad only the third is flattened relative to major, not the fifth.

**Practice:** Build triads on every degree of G major, naming each chord and labelling it major, minor, or diminished, and checking against the I–vii° pattern. Then write C major in root position, first inversion, and second inversion, and play or sing each version, noticing how the lowest note changes the chord's feel. Finally, write a G dominant seventh chord (G, B, D, F) and a C major triad after it, and listen to how the seventh chord's tension settles into the tonic.

## Lesson 7: Harmony, Progressions, and Cadences

Harmony is what happens when chords move in sequence, and a chord progression is a sequence of chords that supports a melody. Not all progressions are equal: some movements feel strong and final, others feel open or surprising, and composers choose progressions to shape a listener's expectations. The most important relationship is between the tonic (I), the home chord, and the dominant (V), which creates tension that resolves to the tonic. The subdominant (IV) sits between them, moving away from home without the same urgency as the dominant.

Four cadences — the punctuation marks at the ends of phrases — cover most of what you need to recognise. A perfect (authentic) cadence moves V to I and sounds finished, like a full stop; it is the standard ending of a piece or a major section. An imperfect (half) cadence ends on V, or moves I, ii, or IV to V, and sounds unfinished, like a comma or a question, leaving the listener expecting more. A plagal cadence moves IV to I and sounds gentle and settled; it is the "Amen" cadence heard at the end of many hymns. An interrupted (deceptive) cadence moves V to vi instead of the expected I, surprising the listener by landing on the relative minor chord instead of home — the musical equivalent of a sentence taking an unexpected turn.

Recognising a cadence means looking at the last two chords of a phrase and asking two questions: what are the chords, and does the phrase feel finished or open? V–I feels finished; anything ending on V feels open; IV–I feels finished but softer; V–vi feels like a sidestep. The melody matters too: a perfect cadence is strongest when the melody also ends on the tonic, while a melody ending on another note can soften even a V–I progression.

Beyond cadences, a handful of progressions recur across popular music. I–V–vi–IV (in C major: C, G, Am, F) underpins countless songs because it balances departure and return: tonic, dominant tension, a sidestep to the relative minor, and a gentle subdominant before returning home. The twelve-bar blues follows I, I, I, I, IV, IV, I, I, V, IV, I, V (or I), a structure that has supported a century of blues, rock, and jazz. Learning to hear the root movement of a progression — whether the bass is moving by step, by fourth or fifth, or by third — is more useful than memorising chord lists, because root movement predicts how strong each change feels: movement by fourth or fifth (such as V to I) is strongest, movement by step is smoothest, and movement by third is gentlest.

Common mistakes include labelling a cadence from the chords alone without listening to whether the phrase feels finished; confusing plagal (IV–I) with perfect (V–I) because both end on I; and treating every V–vi as a mistake rather than a deliberate surprise. Another is analysing chords in isolation instead of in their key: the same three notes can be I in one key and IV or V in another, so always establish the key first.

**Practice:** Take a short familiar song or hymn and identify its key, then write out its chords phrase by phrase using Roman numerals. Label the cadence at the end of each phrase as perfect, imperfect, plagal, or interrupted, and write one sentence for each explaining why — what the two chords are and whether the phrase feels finished, open, or sidestepped. Then compose your own four-chord progression ending in a perfect cadence and a second version ending in an interrupted cadence, and compare how differently the same opening chords feel with a different ending.

## Lesson 8: Melody, Phrasing, and Putting Analysis Together

Melody is a sequence of pitches in time, and good melodies are not random: they balance repetition and variety, stepwise movement and leaps, and tension and release. Most melodies move mainly by step (seconds), with occasional skips (thirds and fourths) and rarer leaps (fifths and beyond). A leap is usually followed by stepwise movement in the opposite direction, which "fills in" the gap and keeps the melody singable — a principle sometimes called the gap-fill rule. A melody that leapt in the same direction repeatedly would quickly run out of range and feel frantic.

Phrasing groups a melody into musical sentences. A phrase is a complete musical thought, usually two or four bars long, ending with a cadence (Lesson 7). Phrases often come in pairs: an antecedent phrase that ends openly, often with an imperfect cadence, followed by a consequent phrase that answers it and ends with a perfect cadence. Hearing this question-and-answer shape is one of the fastest ways to understand a piece's structure: you can feel the first phrase asking something and the second resolving it, even before you can name the chords.

When you analyse a short piece, work in order. First, identify the clef, key signature, and time signature. Second, find the tonic and confirm the key — the first and last notes and chords are strong clues, but check the whole piece, because a piece can start on a chord other than the tonic. Third, mark the phrases and their cadences. Fourth, analyse the harmony with Roman numerals, noting inversions where they matter to the bass line. Fifth, describe the melody: its range, its balance of steps and leaps, any repeated motifs (short patterns that recur, perhaps varied), and any sequences (a motif repeated at a different pitch level). A short written analysis that covers those five points, even for an eight-bar tune, demonstrates real understanding far better than a long list of note names.

Composition uses the same tools in reverse. Start with a short motif of three or four notes, repeat it with a small variation, answer it with a consequent phrase, and support it with a simple progression such as I–V–vi–IV or I–IV–V–I, ending the first phrase on V and the second on I. Keep the range singable — within about an octave — and check that every bar adds up, every chord belongs to the key (or is a deliberate, labelled exception), and the cadences do the job you intended. Then play or sing your melody: theory that cannot be heard is only half learned, and your ear will catch awkward leaps and clumsy phrase endings faster than your eye.

Common mistakes in analysis include starting with details before establishing the key, which makes every Roman numeral uncertain; treating a repeated note pattern as unimportant when it is the piece's main motif; and writing an analysis that only lists facts without saying how the music feels or works. In composition, the commonest mistake is writing a melody that is all leaps or all repetition — variety within a clear, repeated shape is the balance to aim for.

**Practice:** Choose a short, familiar eight-bar melody and write a full analysis following the five steps above: key, phrases and cadences, Roman-numeral harmony, and a melody description covering range, steps and leaps, and motifs. Then compose your own eight-bar melody in C major or G major in 4/4, in antecedent–consequent form, with a simple chord progression underneath. Play or sing it, revise anything that feels awkward, and write a short note explaining your phrase structure and cadence choices.

**Analysing a phrase in practice:** Take the opening of a familiar tune and mark where you breathe or where a singer would naturally pause; those pauses usually mark phrase endings. Check the chords at each pause against Lesson 7's cadences. If the first pause lands on V and the second on I, you have found an antecedent–consequent pair. If a pause lands somewhere unexpected, that is often the most interesting moment in the piece — a deceptive cadence, a phrase extension, or a repeated motif — and it is worth a sentence in your analysis explaining what the composer did and why it holds your attention.
