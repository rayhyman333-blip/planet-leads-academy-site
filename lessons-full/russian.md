# Russian

*Local draft lesson text for Planet Leads Academy. This is study material only. Russian words are given in Cyrillic with a pronunciation guide; pronunciation guides are approximate and listening to native speakers is the best check.*

## Lesson 1: The Cyrillic Alphabet and Sounds

Russian is written in the Cyrillic alphabet, which has 33 letters. It looks unfamiliar at first, but it is a largely phonetic system: once you learn the letters, you can read most words aloud even before you know what they mean. That is a real advantage over English spelling, where the same letters can represent many sounds. Some Cyrillic letters look like Latin letters but sound different, so learn each letter's Russian sound rather than trusting its appearance.

Start with the letters that look and sound familiar: А а sounds like "a" in "father"; К к like "k"; М м like "m"; О о like "o" in "more" (but shorter, and in unstressed syllables it weakens toward "a"); Т т like "t". Next, the look-alikes that deceive: В в sounds like "v", not "b"; Н н sounds like "n", not "h"; Р р is a rolled "r", not "p"; С с sounds like "s", not "c"; Х х sounds like the "ch" in Scottish "loch", not "x". Then the new shapes: Б б is "b"; Г г is "g" as in "go"; Д д is "d"; Ж ж is like the "s" in "measure"; З з is "z"; Л л is "l"; П п is "p"; Ф ф is "f"; Ц ц is "ts" as in "cats"; Ч ч is "ch" as in "chair"; Ш ш is "sh"; Щ щ is a softer, longer "sh" sound; Й й is a short "y" as in "boy"; Э э is "e" as in "bed"; Ю ю is "yu"; Я я is "ya".

Two letters are not pronounced as sounds on their own. The hard sign Ъ separates a consonant from a following vowel so the vowel keeps its full "y" sound, and the soft sign Ь softens (palatalises) the preceding consonant, which means pronouncing it with the tongue raised toward the roof of the mouth. Softening changes meaning in Russian: брат (brat, "brother") and брать (brat', "to take") differ by that soft sign. Vowels also pair up: А/Я, Э/Е, О/Ё, У/Ю. The second member of each pair adds a "y" sound at the start of a word or after a vowel, but after a consonant it mainly signals that the consonant is soft. Ё ё is pronounced "yo" and is always stressed, which is a useful clue when reading.

Stress in Russian is free, meaning it can fall on any syllable, and it changes vowel pronunciation: an unstressed О sounds closer to "a", and an unstressed Е can sound closer to "i". This is why Russian learners must learn stress with each new word, not guess it from spelling. Dictionaries and textbooks mark stress, and this course marks it in early lessons with an accent where helpful, for example ма́ма (mama, "mother").

Common mistakes include reading В as "b" and Р as "p" because of their Latin look, pronouncing every О as a full "o" regardless of stress, and ignoring the soft sign as if it were silent decoration. Read slowly at first and exaggerate the difference between hard and soft consonants; speed comes later, accuracy first.

**Practice:** Learn the alphabet in three groups over several days rather than all at once: first the familiar-looking letters, then the deceptive look-alikes, then the new shapes and the two signs. Read these words aloud, sounding out each letter: мама (mother), папа (father), дом (house), кот (cat), Москва (Moscow), спасибо (thank you), нет (no), да (yes). Write each letter by hand, upper and lower case, saying its sound as you write it, until you can read a short Russian word without translating letter by letter in your head.

## Lesson 2: Greetings, Introductions, and Being Polite

Russian greetings change with the time of day and with formality, and getting this right matters socially. The neutral, polite greeting for most of the day is Здравствуйте (zdravstvuyte, "hello", formal). It is long, but it is the single most useful word in this lesson: use it with strangers, shopkeepers, and anyone older or in authority. The informal greeting between friends and peers is Привет (privet, "hi"). For parting, До свидания (do svidaniya, "goodbye", formal) and Пока (poka, "bye", informal) cover most situations. Time-specific greetings are Доброе утро (dobroye utro, "good morning"), Добрый день (dobry den', "good afternoon"), and Добрый вечер (dobry vecher, "good evening").

Introducing yourself uses the verb "to be called": Меня зовут… (menya zovut…, "My name is…", literally "They call me…"). To ask someone's name, the polite form is Как вас зовут? (kak vas zovut?, "What is your name?", formal) and the informal form is Как тебя зовут? (kak tebya zovut?). The difference between вас/вы (formal "you", also plural "you") and тебя/ты (informal "you") is fundamental in Russian. Use вы with strangers, older people, and in formal settings; use ты with close friends, children, and peers who have invited it. When in doubt, use вы — it is polite, and being invited to switch to ты is itself a friendly moment.

Essential polite words are: Пожалуйста (pozhaluysta, "please" and also "you're welcome"), Спасибо (spasibo, "thank you"), Извините (izvinite, "excuse me / sorry", formal), Простите (prostite, "forgive me / sorry"), and Да (da, "yes") and Нет (net, "no"). To say you do not understand: Я не понимаю (ya ne ponimayu, "I don't understand"). To ask someone to speak slowly: Говорите, пожалуйста, медленнее (govorite, pozhaluysta, medlenneye, "Please speak more slowly"). To say you speak a little Russian: Я немного говорю по-русски (ya nemnogo govoryu po-russki, "I speak a little Russian").

A simple self-introduction brings this together: "Здравствуйте! Меня зовут Анна. Я из Англии. Я немного говорю по-русски." ("Hello! My name is Anna. I am from England. I speak a little Russian.") Replace the name and country with your own. Note Я (ya, "I") and the pattern Я из… ("I am from…") plus a country name. Country names you may need: Россия (Rossiya, "Russia"), Англия (Angliya, "England"), Америка (Amerika, "America"), Франция (Frantsiya, "France"), Германия (Germaniya, "Germany").

Common mistakes include using Привет with a stranger or an official, which can sound over-familiar; shortening Здравствуйте to a mumbled version that is hard to understand — it is better to say it slowly and clearly; and mixing вы and ты in the same conversation, which sounds inconsistent. Pick one level of formality for a conversation and stay with it unless the other person switches.

**Practice:** Write and rehearse three short dialogues: (1) a formal greeting and introduction to a stranger, (2) an informal greeting between two friends, and (3) a polite exchange in a shop that includes "please", "thank you", and "goodbye". Say each one aloud three times, then change the names, countries, and times of day and repeat. Record yourself if you can and compare your pronunciation of Здравствуйте and Пожалуйста with a native-speaker recording.

**A worked introduction, step by step:** Start with the greeting that fits the situation — Здравствуйте for a stranger, Привет for a friend. Then give your name with Меня зовут… and ask theirs with Как вас зовут? or Как тебя зовут?, matching the formality you chose. Add one fact about yourself, such as Я из Англии or Я студент ("I am a student"), so the other person has something to respond to. End the exchange with a polite closing rather than stopping abruptly: Спасибо and До свидания in a formal meeting, or Пока with a friend. A complete greeting, introduction, and closing, even a short one, sounds far more natural than a greeting alone.

## Lesson 3: Nouns, Gender, and Plurals

Every Russian noun has a gender: masculine, feminine, or neuter. Gender is grammatical, not a statement about the real world — a table is masculine, a book is feminine — and it matters because adjectives, pronouns, and some verb forms must agree with the noun's gender. The good news is that gender is usually visible in the noun's ending in the nominative case, the dictionary form used for the subject of a sentence.

The basic rules are: nouns ending in a consonant are usually masculine (дом, dom, "house"; стол, stol, "table"; брат, brat, "brother"). Nouns ending in -а or -я are usually feminine (мама, mama, "mother"; книга, kniga, "book"; семья, sem'ya, "family"). Nouns ending in -о or -е are usually neuter (окно, okno, "window"; море, more, "sea"; имя, imya, "name" — an exception to memorise, as it declines irregularly). Nouns ending in -ь (soft sign) can be masculine or feminine and must be learned individually: день (den', "day") is masculine, ночь (noch', "night") is feminine. When you learn a new noun, learn its gender with it, just as you learn its stress.

Russian has no articles — no equivalent of "a" or "the" — so книга can mean "a book" or "the book" depending on context. Word order is more flexible than in English because case endings show each word's role, but the neutral order is subject–verb–object, and keeping to it while you are learning avoids confusion.

Plurals in the nominative case follow patterns. Masculine nouns ending in a consonant usually add -ы (стол → столы, "tables"; дом → домы is not used — дом → дома, "houses", is an exception with stressed -а, and exceptions like this are common enough that you should learn plurals of frequent nouns as you meet them). After the letters г, к, х, ж, ч, ш, щ, -ы becomes -и (книга is feminine, but for masculine nouns: урок, urok, "lesson" → уроки). Feminine nouns ending in -а change -а to -ы (книга → книги, because г triggers -и; мама → мамы), and those ending in -я change -я to -и (семья → семьи). Neuter nouns ending in -о change -о to -а (окно → окна) and those ending in -е change -е to -я (море → моря). These are the regular patterns; Russian has many irregular plurals, such as друг (drug, "friend") → друзья (druz'ya, "friends") and ребёнок (rebyonok, "child") → дети (deti, "children"), which are best learned as vocabulary.

A simple sentence pattern to practise is Это… ("This is…"): Это дом. ("This is a house.") Это книга. ("This is a book.") Это мой брат. ("This is my brother.") Here мой (moy, "my", masculine) will change with gender in Lesson 5, but Это itself never changes, which makes it a reliable sentence starter while you are learning.

Common mistakes include assuming gender from English or from the thing's real-world associations, forgetting the spelling rule that turns -ы into -и after г, к, х, ж, ч, ш, щ, and treating every plural as regular. When a plural looks surprising, check it in a dictionary rather than forcing the regular pattern — irregular plurals are frequent in the most common words, which is precisely why they are worth memorising.

**Practice:** Sort these nouns by gender and give their nominative plurals, checking your answers: стол (table), книга (book), окно (window), мама (mother), брат (brother), море (sea), урок (lesson), ночь (night), день (day), семья (family). Then write ten sentences beginning Это… about objects around you, using a new noun in each, and read them aloud, paying attention to the stress of each noun.

**A worked gender check:** Take the noun школа (shkola, "school"). It ends in -а, so it is feminine, and its plural is школы (the -а becomes -ы, since л is not one of the letters that change -ы to -и). Now take урок (urok, "lesson"). It ends in a consonant, so it is masculine, and its plural is уроки, because к is one of the letters after which -ы becomes -и. Working through the ending, then the gender, then the plural rule in that order turns a guess into a checkable decision, and it is the habit that prevents most beginner errors with plurals.

## Lesson 4: Present-Tense Verbs

Russian verbs change their endings according to who is doing the action, and the present tense has two main conjugation patterns. Learning the pattern, rather than memorising each verb separately, lets you conjugate hundreds of verbs once you know their infinitive (dictionary form, usually ending in -ть) and which conjugation they follow.

First-conjugation verbs include читать (chitat', "to read"). Its stem is чита-, and its endings are: я читаю (I read), ты читаешь (you read, informal), он/она/оно читает (he/she/it reads), мы читаем (we read), вы читаете (you read, formal/plural), они читают (they read). The endings -ю, -ешь, -ет, -ем, -ете, -ют attach to the stem. Another first-conjugation verb is делать (delat', "to do/make"): я делаю, ты делаешь, он делает, мы делаем, вы делаете, они делают.

Second-conjugation verbs include говорить (govorit', "to speak") and смотреть (smotret', "to watch/look"). Their endings are -ю, -ишь, -ит, -им, -ите, -ят (or -ат after certain consonants): я говорю, ты говоришь, он говорит, мы говорим, вы говорите, они говорят. Note the consonant change in the "I" form of many second-conjugation verbs, for example любить (lyubit', "to love") → я люблю, where б becomes бл. Learn the first-person form with each new second-conjugation verb, because that is where such a change appears.

Russian present tense covers both "I read" and "I am reading" — there is no separate continuous form as in English. Я читаю книгу can mean "I read a book" (habitually) or "I am reading a book" (right now); context decides. Russian also has no present tense of the verb "to be" in ordinary statements: Я студент (ya student, "I am a student") has no word for "am". This feels strange at first, but it means you can make simple statements by putting the subject and the description side by side.

Useful everyday verbs to learn now: жить (zhit', "to live"), работать (rabotat', "to work"), учиться (uchit'sya, "to study"), знать (znat', "to know"), хотеть (khotet', "to want" — irregular: я хочу, ты хочешь, он хочет, мы хотим, вы хотите, они хотят), любить (lyubit', "to love"), and понимать (ponimat', "to understand"). With these and the patterns above, you can already say: Я живу в Лондоне. ("I live in London.") Я работаю в школе. ("I work in a school.") Я люблю музыку. ("I love music.") Я не понимаю. ("I don't understand.") Negation in Russian is simple: put не (ne, "not") before the verb.

Common mistakes include using first-conjugation endings on a second-conjugation verb (я говорию instead of я говорю), forgetting the first-person consonant change, and inserting a word for "am/is/are" into present-tense statements. Another is translating English "do" as a separate verb in questions — Russian questions use intonation and word order, not an auxiliary verb: Ты говоришь по-русски? ("Do you speak Russian?") is a statement with rising intonation and a question mark, not a different verb form.

**Practice:** Conjugate читать, делать, говорить, and смотреть in full, writing all six persons for each. Then write eight sentences about yourself using я, мы, and они with at least four different verbs from this lesson, including one negative sentence with не. Read them aloud, then turn three of them into questions by changing only the intonation and adding a question mark.

**A worked conjugation check:** Take the verb жить ("to live"), a first-conjugation verb with stem жив-. Its forms are я живу, ты живёшь, он живёт, мы живём, вы живёте, они живут. Notice the ё in живёшь and живёт, which appears because the stress falls on the ending; in an unstressed ending the same vowel would be written е, as in читаешь. This stress-dependent spelling is why you should learn verbs with their stress marked. Now try работать ("to work"): я работаю, ты работаешь, он работает, мы работаем, вы работаете, они работают — the same endings, with the stress staying on the stem.

## Lesson 5: Adjectives, Possessives, and Agreement

Russian adjectives agree with the noun they describe in gender, number, and case. That means the same adjective has different endings depending on the noun, and learning the pattern once lets you describe any noun correctly. In the nominative case, masculine adjectives usually end in -ый or -ий (новый, novy, "new"; хороший, khoroshiy, "good"), feminine adjectives end in -ая or -яя (новая, novaya; хорошая, khoroshaya), neuter adjectives end in -ое or -ее (новое, novoye; хорошее, khorosheye), and plurals end in -ые or -ие (новые, novye; хорошие, khoroshie). The choice between -ый and -ий, and between -ая and -яя, depends on the stem's final consonant and on spelling rules, but the gender pattern itself is consistent: новый дом (a new house, masculine), новая книга (a new book, feminine), новое окно (a new window, neuter), новые книги (new books, plural).

Useful adjectives to learn now: большой (bol'shoy, "big"), маленький (malen'kiy, "small"), хороший (khoroshiy, "good"), плохой (plokhoy, "bad"), красивый (krasivyy, "beautiful"), интересный (interesnyy, "interesting"), старый (staryy, "old"), новый (novyy, "new"), русский (russkiy, "Russian"), английский (angliyskiy, "English"). Colours are adjectives too and follow the same pattern: красный (krasnyy, "red"), синий (siniy, "dark blue"), голубой (goluboy, "light blue"), зелёный (zelyonyy, "green"), жёлтый (zhyoltyy, "yellow"), белый (belyy, "white"), чёрный (chornyy, "black"). Note that Russian distinguishes dark blue (синий) and light blue (голубой) as separate basic colours, a difference English speakers must learn as vocabulary, not as shades of one colour.

Possessive pronouns — "my", "your", "his", "her", "our", "their" — also agree with the noun they belong to, not with the owner. Мой (moy, "my") changes: мой брат (my brother, masculine), моя сестра (moya sestra, "my sister", feminine), моё окно (moyo okno, "my window", neuter), мои книги (moi knigi, "my books", plural). Твой (tvoy, "your", informal) follows the same pattern: твой дом, твоя книга, твоё окно, твои книги. Ваш (vash, "your", formal/plural) gives ваш дом, ваша книга, ваше окно, ваши книги. Наш (nash, "our") gives наш дом, наша книга, наше окно, наши книги. Его (yego, "his"), её (yeyo, "her"), and их (ikh, "their") do not change for gender or number — a useful simplification: его книга, её книга, их книги all use the same form.

Word order with adjectives is usually adjective before noun, as in English: интересная книга ("an interesting book"), большой дом ("a big house"). Putting the adjective after the noun is possible for emphasis or in poetry, but keep it before the noun while you are learning.

Common mistakes include using the masculine adjective form for every noun (новый книга instead of новая книга), using мой for a feminine noun (мой сестра instead of моя сестра), and making его/её/их agree when they should stay unchanged. Another is translating "blue" without choosing between синий and голубой — when in doubt, describe the shade, but learn to hear the distinction in native speech.

**Practice:** Write the phrases for "new", "good", "big", and "interesting" combined with дом, книга, окно, and книги, giving all sixteen combinations correctly. Then write eight sentences describing objects around you, each with a possessive and an adjective, for example "Это моя интересная книга." ("This is my interesting book.") Check every ending against the noun's gender and number before reading the sentences aloud.

**A worked agreement check:** Describe a book as interesting and new: интересная новая книга. Both adjectives are feminine because книга is feminine, and both come before the noun. Describe a house the same way: интересный новый дом, with masculine endings. Describe windows: интересные новые окна, with neuter plural endings. If you change the noun, every adjective must change with it. A useful self-check is to cover the noun and ask whether the adjective ending alone tells you the noun's gender; if it does not, an ending is probably wrong.

## Lesson 6: Past Tense and Everyday Cases

Russian past tense is simpler than English past tense in one respect: it is formed from the infinitive, and it agrees with the subject's gender and number rather than with person. Remove the infinitive ending -ть and add -л for masculine, -ла for feminine, -ло for neuter, and -ли for plural. From читать (to read): он читал (he read), она читала (she read), оно читало (it read), они читали (they read). From говорить: он говорил, она говорила, оно говорило, они говорили. There is no separate "was reading" form in the past of imperfective verbs — читал covers both "read" and "was reading", and context decides, just as in the present tense.

Aspect is the deeper idea behind Russian verbs, and it deserves an honest introduction even though full mastery takes time. Most Russian verbs come in pairs: an imperfective verb for ongoing, repeated, or incomplete actions (читать, "to read / to be reading") and a perfective verb for a completed, single action (прочитать, "to read through / to finish reading"). In the past tense, the difference is clear: Я читал книгу means "I was reading / used to read a book", while Я прочитал книгу means "I read (finished) the book". Learn verbs in pairs when you can, and do not worry if aspect feels difficult at first — it is one of the hardest parts of Russian for English speakers, and steady exposure matters more than memorising rules in one sitting.

This lesson also introduces two cases you need constantly. The accusative case marks the direct object — the thing acted on. For inanimate masculine nouns and all neuter nouns, the accusative looks the same as the nominative: Я читаю книгу (feminine, accusative -у: книгу, not книга), Я вижу дом (masculine inanimate, unchanged: дом). For feminine nouns ending in -а, the accusative changes -а to -у (книга → книгу; мама → маму), and for those ending in -я it changes -я to -ю (семья → семью). For animate masculine nouns (people and animals), the accusative looks like the genitive: Я вижу брата ("I see my brother", брата, not брат). The prepositional case is used after the prepositions в ("in") and на ("on") for location: в Москве (in Moscow), в школе (at school), на столе (on the table). Note the pattern: Я живу в Москве (location, prepositional) versus Я еду в Москву (direction, accusative) — location and direction use different cases, and mixing them up is a classic learner error.

Useful past-tense sentences: Вчера я читал интересную книгу. ("Yesterday I read / was reading an interesting book.") Вчера она работала дома. ("Yesterday she worked at home.") Мы были в Москве. ("We were in Moscow.") Here был/была/было/были is the past tense of "to be", which does appear in the past even though it is omitted in the present.

Common mistakes include using the present-tense person endings in the past (я читаю for a past action), forgetting gender agreement in the past (она читал instead of она читала), and using the nominative for a feminine direct object (я читаю книга instead of книгу). Check the object's gender and animacy before choosing its form.

**Practice:** Write the past tense of читать, говорить, работать, and жить for он, она, оно, and они. Then write six sentences about yesterday using Вчера ("yesterday") and a past-tense verb, including one location sentence with в + prepositional and one direction sentence with в + accusative, and label the case of each object noun. Read them aloud and check each past-tense ending against the subject's gender.

**A worked case check:** In the sentence Я вижу маму ("I see Mum"), маму is accusative because мама is the direct object and feminine nouns ending in -а change to -у. In Я живу в Москве, Москве is prepositional because it follows в for location. In Я еду в Москву, Москву is accusative because в here shows direction. The same preposition, в, takes different cases for location and direction, so always ask first: am I saying where something is, or where it is going? That question, answered before you choose the ending, prevents the most persistent case error in this lesson.

## Lesson 7: Numbers, Time, and Days

Russian numbers are essential for prices, times, ages, and addresses, and they interact with nouns in ways that surprise English speakers. The numbers 1 to 10 are: один (odin, 1), два (dva, 2), три (tri, 3), четыре (chetyre, 4), пять (pyat', 5), шесть (shest', 6), семь (sem', 7), восемь (vosem', 8), девять (devyat', 9), десять (desyat', 10). From 11 to 19, most numbers add -надцать to a stem: одиннадцать (11), двенадцать (12), тринадцать (13), четырнадцать (14), пятнадцать (15), шестнадцать (16), семнадцать (17), восемнадцать (18), девятнадцать (19). Tens are: двадцать (20), тридцать (30), сорок (40), пятьдесят (50), шестьдесят (60), семьдесят (70), восемьдесят (80), девяносто (90), сто (100). Compound numbers join tens and units: двадцать один (21), тридцать пять (35).

Numbers change the form of the noun that follows them, and this is the part to learn carefully. With один, the noun is nominative singular, and один itself agrees in gender: один стол (one table, masculine), одна книга (one book, feminine), одно окно (one window, neuter). With два, три, and четыре, the noun takes the genitive singular form: два стола, три книги, четыре окна — note that for feminine nouns the genitive singular looks like книга → книги, and for masculine nouns стол → стола. With пять and above, the noun takes the genitive plural: пять столов, пять книг, пять окон. The same pattern applies to compound numbers, decided by the final digit: двадцать один стол (21 tables, nominative singular, because it ends in один), двадцать два стола (22, genitive singular), двадцать пять столов (25, genitive plural). This rule applies to counting objects; prices and other contexts have their own details, but this pattern is the foundation.

Telling the time uses час (chas, "hour", also "o'clock") with the same number rules: один час, два часа, пять часов. To ask the time: Сколько времени? (skol'ko vremeni?, "What time is it?") or Который час? (kotoryy chas?). To say the time on the hour: Сейчас три часа. ("It is three o'clock now.") Minutes past the hour are expressed with the next hour in mind in formal Russian (for example, "half past three" is expressed as half of the fourth hour), but for everyday use, saying the hour and then the minutes — три часа десять минут ("three hours, ten minutes") — is widely understood and is a safe starting point while you learn the fuller system.

Days of the week are: понедельник (ponedel'nik, Monday), вторник (vtornik, Tuesday), среда (sreda, Wednesday), четверг (chetverg, Thursday), пятница (pyatnitsa, Friday), суббота (subbota, Saturday), воскресенье (voskresen'ye, Sunday). Note that the Russian week traditionally starts on Monday, and понедельник literally means "after the no-day" (after Sunday, the day of no work). Useful time words: сегодня (segodnya, "today"), завтра (zavtra, "tomorrow"), вчера (vchera, "yesterday"), утром (utrom, "in the morning"), днём (dnyom, "in the afternoon"), вечером (vecherom, "in the evening"), ночью (noch'yu, "at night").

Common mistakes include using the nominative plural after all numbers (пять книги instead of пять книг), forgetting that один changes with gender (один книга instead of одна книга), and translating English time expressions word for word. Always let the final digit of the number decide the noun's form, and check it before speaking.

**Practice:** Write out the correct noun forms for these counts: 1 книга, 2 книга, 5 книга, 21 стол, 23 стол, 25 стол, 1 окно, 3 окно, 7 окно. Then write the times 1 o'clock, 3 o'clock, and 5 o'clock in Russian, and write a short paragraph about your week using сегодня, завтра, and вчера and at least three days of the week. Read the paragraph aloud, checking each number–noun combination.

**A worked counting check:** Count books: одна книга (1, nominative singular, feminine form of один), две книги (2, genitive singular, and note две rather than два for feminine nouns), пять книг (5, genitive plural). Count tables: один стол (1), два стола (2), пять столов (5). The pattern is the same, but два/две changes with gender and the noun's genitive forms differ. Say each phrase aloud, because the endings -а/-я for the genitive singular and -ов/-ей or no ending for the genitive plural become familiar much faster through speaking than through reading a table.

## Lesson 8: Everyday Conversation — Shops, Travel, and Putting It Together

This final lesson combines everything so far into the conversations a visitor actually has: buying something, asking directions, ordering food, and handling problems politely. The grammar is not new; the skill is choosing the right phrase quickly and understanding the answer.

In a shop, start with Здравствуйте, then ask: Сколько это стоит? (skol'ko eto stoit?, "How much does this cost?") The answer will be a number plus рублей (rubley, "rubles") or another currency word, so Lesson 7's numbers are essential here. To say you want something: Я хочу… ("I want…") plus the accusative form, for example Я хочу эту книгу ("I want this book", эту being the accusative feminine form of "this"). To ask for something: Дайте, пожалуйста… ("Give me, please…"), for example Дайте, пожалуйста, хлеб ("Give me bread, please"). Useful shop words: магазин (magazin, "shop"), хлеб (khleb, "bread"), вода (voda, "water"), молоко (moloko, "milk"), чай (chay, "tea"), кофе (kofe, "coffee", indeclinable and masculine in modern standard usage), цена (tsena, "price"), дёшево (dyoshevo, "cheap"), дорого (dorogo, "expensive").

Asking directions uses Где…? (gde…?, "Where is…?"): Где метро? ("Where is the metro?") Где вокзал? ("Where is the station?") Где туалет? ("Where is the toilet?") Understand the common answers: прямо (pryamo, "straight ahead"), налево (nalevo, "to the left"), направо (napravo, "to the right"), рядом (ryadom, "nearby"), далеко (daleko, "far"). If you do not understand, say Я не понимаю, and ask Говорите, пожалуйста, медленнее, or ask someone to show you: Покажите, пожалуйста ("Show me, please").

In a café or restaurant: Меню, пожалуйста ("The menu, please"), Я хочу чай, пожалуйста ("I would like tea, please"), Счёт, пожалуйста (schyot, pozhaluysta, "The bill, please"). Спасибо after paying, and До свидания when leaving. If something is wrong, stay polite: Извините, это не мой заказ ("Excuse me, this is not my order") is far more effective than a complaint without извините.

A complete short exchange brings the course together. Visitor: "Здравствуйте! Сколько стоит эта книга?" Shopkeeper: "Пятьсот рублей." Visitor: "Хорошо. Я хочу эту книгу. Спасибо!" Shopkeeper: "Пожалуйста. До свидания!" Visitor: "До свидания!" Notice how much of this you already know: greetings and thanks from Lesson 2, gender and the accusative from Lessons 3 and 6, numbers from Lesson 7, and the verb хочу from Lesson 4. Learning a language is largely learning to recombine a small set of patterns in new situations, and that recombination is what this lesson practises.

Common mistakes at this stage are reverting to English word order under pressure, forgetting the accusative for a feminine object (я хочу эта книга instead of эту книгу), and freezing when you do not understand every word of an answer. You do not need every word: listen for numbers, direction words, and да/нет, and ask for repetition or slower speech when you need it. Communication, not perfection, is the goal of an everyday conversation.

**Practice:** Write and rehearse three complete dialogues: buying a book in a shop, asking for directions to the metro and understanding the answer, and ordering tea in a café and asking for the bill. Each dialogue must include a greeting, a polite request, a number or a price, and a goodbye. Perform them aloud, swapping roles if you have a partner, and then change one detail in each — a different item, a different destination, a different drink — and perform them again without rewriting the whole dialogue.

**Handling a misunderstanding:** If a shopkeeper answers with a long sentence you do not follow, do not pretend to understand. Say Извините, я не понимаю, then ask a narrower question: Сколько это стоит? or Это книга? A yes-or-no question is easier to answer and easier to understand than an open one. Pointing at the item while asking also removes ambiguity. Native speakers are generally glad to help a learner who is clearly trying, and a short, polite repair — sorry, I don't understand, please repeat slowly — keeps the conversation going far better than silence or a guessed answer.
