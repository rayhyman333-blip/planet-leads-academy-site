## Lesson 1: Sounds, Alphabet, and Greetings

Italian spelling is unusually regular: once you learn how letters sound, you can read most words aloud with confidence. The alphabet has 21 letters in common use, with j, k, w, x, and y appearing mainly in borrowed words. The five vowels — a, e, i, o, u — are pure and short, unlike the gliding vowels of English. Say *a* as in "father," *e* as in "bed" or "they" depending on whether it is open or closed, *i* as in "machine," *o* as in "more" without the English glide, and *u* as in "rule." Practise pairs such as *casa* (house), *pane* (bread), *vino* (wine), *sole* (sun), and *luna* (moon), keeping each vowel distinct and steady.

Consonants deserve attention because a few combinations change sound. The letter *c* before *a, o,* or *u* sounds like English *k*: *casa, come, cucina*. Before *e* or *i* it sounds like *ch* in "church": *cena* (dinner), *cinema*. The letter *g* follows the same pattern: *gatto* (cat) with a hard *g*, but *gelato* and *giardino* (garden) with a soft *j* sound. The combination *ch* restores the hard sound before *e* and *i*: *che* (what/that), *chiesa* (church). The combination *gh* does the same for *g*: *ghiaccio* (ice). The letters *sc* before *e* or *i* sound like *sh*: *scena* (scene), *scienza* (science). Double consonants are held longer, not just said louder: *palla* (ball) is not *pala* (shovel), *anno* (year) is not *ano* (anus, a word to avoid confusing), and *notte* (night) has a longer *t* than *note* (notes). Stress usually falls on the second-to-last syllable, but written accents mark exceptions: *città* (city), *perché* (why/because), *caffè* (coffee).

Greetings follow the time of day and the level of formality. *Buongiorno* works from morning until early afternoon, *buonasera* from late afternoon onward, and *buonanotte* is only a farewell at night, not a greeting on arrival. *Ciao* is friendly and informal, used with friends, family, and peers, for both hello and goodbye. *Salve* is a polite middle ground, safe with strangers when you are unsure. To ask how someone is, say *Come stai?* informally or *Come sta?* formally. A simple reply is *Bene, grazie. E tu?* (Well, thanks. And you?) or, formally, *E Lei?* Introductions are simple: *Mi chiamo Sara* (My name is Sara), *Piacere* (Pleased to meet you), *Di dove sei?* (Where are you from?), *Sono di Roma* (I am from Rome).

A common mistake is pronouncing every Italian word with English vowel glides, turning *bene* into "bay-nay." Keep vowels short and clean. Another is using *ciao* with a shopkeeper or official in a first meeting; it is not rude in the way some guides claim, but *buongiorno* or *salve* is safer and warmer. Learners also confuse *buonanotte* with a general evening greeting; reserve it for parting at night.

**Practice exercise:** Read these aloud slowly, then write a five-line self-introduction: *Buongiorno, mi chiamo ___. Sono di ___. Come stai? Bene, grazie.* Record yourself if you can, and check that each vowel stays pure and each double consonant is held a beat longer.

**Extra worked examples — pronunciation in context:** Say these sentences slowly, stressing the underlined syllable in your mind: *La casa è grande* (the house is big), *Il bambino mangia la mela* (the child eats the apple), *Dove si trova la stazione?* (Where is the station?). Notice how *che* in *Che ore sono?* (What time is it?) uses the hard *k* sound because of the *h*, while *cena* uses *ch*. Listen for double consonants in *il caffè è ottimo* and *la pizza è buona*: hold the *f* and *z* sounds a fraction longer than a single consonant. Record yourself reading a short menu — *antipasto, primo, secondo, contorno, dolce* — and check that *gn* in *lasagna* sounds like the *ny* in 'canyon' and *gl* in *famiglia* sounds like the *lli* in 'million' in careful speech. These two sounds, *gn* and *gli*, are the ones learners most often flatten to *n* and *l*, so practising them in real words beats drilling them alone.

## Lesson 2: Essere, Avere, and Describing Yourself

Two verbs carry most beginner conversation: *essere* (to be) and *avere* (to have). Italian verbs change their endings for each person, so the subject pronoun is often dropped: *sono* already means "I am," and *ho* already means "I have." Learn the full present tense of both, because they appear everywhere, including in idioms where English uses "to be" but Italian uses "to have."

*Essere:* io sono, tu sei, lui/lei è, noi siamo, voi siete, loro sono. *Avere:* io ho, tu hai, lui/lei ha, noi abbiamo, voi avete, loro hanno. Note the spelling: *ho, hai, ha, hanno* all carry an *h* that is silent; it distinguishes *ho* (I have) from *o* (or) and *ha* (he/she has) from *a* (to/at). Examples: *Sono italiana* (I am Italian, feminine), *Sono italiano* (masculine), *Siamo amici* (We are friends), *Hai un fratello?* (Do you have a brother?), *Hanno due figli* (They have two children).

Adjectives agree in gender and number with the noun they describe. Most adjectives ending in *-o* become *-a* for feminine, *-i* for masculine plural, and *-e* for feminine plural: *bello/bella/belli/belle* (beautiful), *alto/alta/alti/alte* (tall). Adjectives ending in *-e*, such as *grande* (big) or *felice* (happy), use *-e* for both singular genders and *-i* for both plurals: *ragazzo felice, ragazza felice, ragazzi felici, ragazze felici*. Nationality adjectives follow the same rule: *inglese* stays *inglese* in the singular for both genders and becomes *inglesi* in the plural. Position matters: most descriptive adjectives follow the noun (*una casa grande*), while a few common ones like *bello, buono, grande* (in the sense of great), and *nuovo* often come before it (*un bel giorno*, *una buona idea*).

Age in Italian uses *avere*, not *essere*: *Ho trent'anni* (I am thirty years old, literally "I have thirty years"). Hunger, thirst, cold, heat, fear, and sleepiness also use *avere*: *Ho fame* (I am hungry), *Ho sete* (I am thirsty), *Ho freddo* (I am cold), *Ho paura* (I am afraid), *Ho sonno* (I am sleepy). Translating these word-for-word from "I am" is the single most common beginner error, and it produces sentences Italians will misunderstand or find odd.

Numbers 1–20 are worth memorising now: uno, due, tre, quattro, cinque, sei, sette, otto, nove, dieci, undici, dodici, tredici, quattordici, quindici, sedici, diciassette, diciotto, diciannove, venti. Note *ventuno, ventotto* drop the final vowel of *venti* before a vowel, a pattern that continues with *trenta, quaranta*, and so on.

**Practice exercise:** Write ten sentences about yourself and a friend, using *essere* five times and *avere* five times, including your age, a physical description, and something you have or do not have. Then translate these: "We are tired" is *Siamo stanchi* — check why *stanco* agrees with *noi* in the masculine plural here, and rewrite it for an all-female group.

**Extra worked examples — describing people:** *Mio fratello è alto e gentile* (My brother is tall and kind), *Le mie sorelle sono simpatiche e intelligenti* (My sisters are nice and intelligent — note the feminine plural *-e* on *simpatiche* and *-i* on *intelligenti*, because *-e* adjectives take *-i* in the plural for both genders). *Noi abbiamo una casa piccola ma accogliente* (We have a small but welcoming house). Try opposites: *giovane/vecchio* (young/old), *ricco/povero* (rich/poor), *felice/triste* (happy/sad), *forte/debole* (strong/weak). When you describe a mixed group, the masculine plural wins: one brother and one sister are *alti*, not *alte*. When you say where someone is from, the preposition is *di*: *Sono di Milano* (I am from Milan), *È di Napoli* (He/she is from Naples). Practise asking *Quanti anni hai?* (How old are you, informal?) and answering *Ho vent'anni* — note the elision in *vent'anni* from *venti anni*, a spelling detail that appears in many age phrases.

## Lesson 3: Regular Verbs in the Present Tense

Italian verbs fall into three conjugations, identified by their infinitive endings: *-are* (parlare, to speak), *-ere* (vedere, to see), and *-ire* (partire, to leave; finire, to finish). Regular verbs follow predictable endings, and learning one model verb per group unlocks hundreds of others. The present tense covers both "I speak" and "I am speaking" — Italian has no separate continuous present for most everyday use, though *stare + gerund* (*sto parlando*) exists for an action in progress.

For *-are* verbs, take *parlare* and remove *-are* to get the stem *parl-*, then add: io parlo, tu parli, lui/lei parla, noi parliamo, voi parlate, loro parlano. Other examples: *abitare* (to live) — abito, abiti, abita, abitiamo, abitate, abitano; *lavorare* (to work) — lavoro, lavori, lavora, lavoriamo, lavorate, lavorano; *mangiare* (to eat) — mangio, mangi, mangia, mangiamo, mangiate, mangiano. Note that verbs ending in *-giare* or *-ciare* drop the *i* before an ending that starts with *i*: *tu mangi*, not *mangii*, and *noi mangiamo* keeps the *i* because the ending starts with *a*.

For *-ere* verbs, *vedere* gives: vedo, vedi, vede, vediamo, vedete, vedono. Similarly *credere* (to believe): credo, credi, crede, crediamo, credete, credono; *scrivere* (to write): scrivo, scrivi, scrive, scriviamo, scrivete, scrivono. For *-ire* verbs there are two patterns. The plain pattern, like *partire*: parto, parti, parte, partiamo, partite, partono; *dormire* (to sleep): dormo, dormi, dorme, dormiamo, dormite, dormono. The *-isc-* pattern, like *finire*: finisco, finisci, finisce, finiamo, finite, finiscono; *capire* (to understand): capisco, capisci, capisce, capiamo, capite, capiscono; *preferire* (to prefer): preferisco. There is no reliable rule for which *-ire* verbs take *-isc-*; you learn them verb by verb, and dictionaries mark them.

Negation is simple: put *non* before the verb. *Non parlo italiano* (I do not speak Italian), *Non capisco* (I do not understand). Questions need no word-order change: raise your intonation, or add a question word. *Parli italiano?* (Do you speak Italian?), *Dove abiti?* (Where do you live?), *Che cosa fai?* (What do you do?). *Che* and *cosa* both mean "what"; *che cosa* together is the fullest form, *cosa* alone is common in speech.

Reflexive verbs, used for daily routines, add a pronoun before the verb: *mi sveglio* (I wake up), *ti svegli, si sveglia, ci svegliamo, vi svegliate, si svegliano* for *svegliarsi*. Others: *alzarsi* (to get up), *lavarsi* (to wash oneself), *vestirsi* (to get dressed), *chiamarsi* (to be called): *Mi chiamo Marco* is literally "I call myself Marco."

A common mistake is keeping the English pronoun and the verb ending redundant in every sentence: *Io parlo* is correct but *Parlo* alone is more natural unless you want emphasis or contrast. Another is mixing conjugations: *parlano* is right for *-are*, but *vedono* for *-ere* and *partono* for *-ire* show the third-person plural ending changes by group (*-ano, -ono, -ono*).

**Practice exercise:** Conjugate *abitare, credere,* and *finire* in full, then write a paragraph about your daily routine using at least six verbs, including one reflexive verb and one negative sentence. Swap papers mentally with a partner sentence: change every *io* form to *noi* and check each ending again.

**Extra worked examples — verbs in conversation:** A short dialogue: *— Che cosa fai di solito la mattina? — Mi alzo alle sette, faccio colazione e vado al lavoro. — E la sera? — Di solito leggo un libro o guardo un film.* (What do you usually do in the morning? I get up at seven, have breakfast and go to work. And in the evening? I usually read a book or watch a film.) Note *faccio* (I do/make) is irregular, and *vado* (I go) is irregular too — two verbs you will use daily, so learn them as whole words rather than deriving them. Irregular present forms to meet early: *andare* (vado, vai, va, andiamo, andate, vanno), *fare* (faccio, fai, fa, facciamo, fate, fanno), *dire* (dico, dici, dice, diciamo, dite, dicono), *venire* (vengo, vieni, viene, veniamo, venite, vengono), *potere* (posso, puoi, può, possiamo, potete, possono), *volere* (voglio, vuoi, vuole, vogliamo, volete, vogliono), *dovere* (devo, devi, deve, dobbiamo, dovete, devono). Use *potere* for ability and permission, *volere* for wants, and *dovere* for obligations, each followed by an infinitive: *Posso entrare? Devo studiare. Voglio imparare l'italiano.*

## Lesson 4: Articles, Nouns, and Gender

Every Italian noun has a gender — masculine or feminine — and number, and the article must match both. This is not about biological sex for most objects: *il tavolo* (the table) is masculine, *la sedia* (the chair) is feminine, by convention you memorise with the word. Learn every new noun with its article, not alone: *il libro, la penna, lo studente, l'amica*.

Definite articles (the): masculine singular *il* before most consonants (*il libro*), *lo* before *s, z, ps, gn,* or *y* plus consonant (*lo studente, lo zaino, lo psicologo*) and before *sc* + consonant (*lo schermo*), *l'* before a vowel (*l'amico, l'ora*); masculine plural *i* (*i libri*), *gli* before vowels and the *lo* cases (*gli studenti, gli amici*). Feminine singular *la* before consonants (*la casa*), *l'* before vowels (*l'amica*); feminine plural *le* in all cases (*le case, le amiche* — note *le* never shortens to *l'*). Indefinite articles (a/an): masculine *un* (*un libro*), *uno* in the *lo* cases (*uno studente, uno zaino*); feminine *una* (*una casa*), *un'* before vowels (*un'amica*).

Noun endings give strong clues. Most nouns ending in *-o* are masculine (*il ragazzo, il treno*), most ending in *-a* are feminine (*la ragazza, la stazione* — note *stazione* ends in *-e* but is feminine, an exception to learn), most ending in *-e* can be either (*il cane* masculine, *la notte* feminine). Plurals: *-o* becomes *-i* (*ragazzo/ragazzi*), *-a* becomes *-e* (*ragazza/ragazze*), *-e* becomes *-i* (*cane/cani, notte/notti*). Nouns ending in a stressed vowel (*la città, il caffè*) or a consonant (borrowed words like *il computer, lo sport*) do not change in the plural: *due città, due computer*. Nouns ending in *-ca* or *-ga* insert *h* in the plural to keep the hard sound: *amica/amiche, bottega/botteghe*.

Possessives agree with the thing possessed, not the possessor, and usually keep the article: *il mio libro* (my book), *la mia penna* (my pen), *i miei amici, le mie amiche*. With singular family members, the article drops: *mia madre, mio padre, mio fratello, mia sorella* — but *i miei genitori* (plural) and *la mia mamma* (with a diminutive or adjective) keep it. This is a frequent source of errors for English speakers, who expect the possessive to match the owner.

Prepositions combine with definite articles into articulated prepositions: *di + il = del, a + la = alla, da + lo = dallo, in + i = nei, su + gli = sugli, per* does not combine. Example: *Vado al mercato* (I go to the market, *a + il*), *Il libro è sulla tavola* (*su + la*), *Vengo da Roma* uses no article because city names take none. Country names usually take the article (*l'Italia, la Francia*) but not after *in* for a simple destination: *Vado in Italia*.

**Practice exercise:** Take twenty nouns from a dictionary or word list, write each with its definite and indefinite article in singular and plural, and then write ten sentences using *di, a, in,* and *da* with articles, including two articulated prepositions. Circle any noun whose gender surprised you and memorise it with its article.

**Extra worked examples — articles in real sentences:** *Il gatto è sul divano* (The cat is on the sofa — *su + il = sul*), *La chiave è nella borsa* (The key is in the bag — *in + la = nella*), *Vengo dal medico* (I come from the doctor — *da + il = dal*), *Parlo con gli amici* (I speak with friends — *con + gli* stays *con gli*, since *con* usually does not contract in modern Italian except in *col* for *con + il*, which is optional). Mass nouns and abstract nouns often take the article where English drops it: *Mi piace la musica* (I like music), *L'amore è importante* (Love is important), *Il pane è fresco* (Bread is fresh). But after *di* in a general sense, the article may drop: *Ho bisogno di pane* (I need bread) versus *Il pane che ho comprato è buono* (The bread I bought is good). The rule of thumb is: specific, known thing — article; general, unspecified quantity — often no article after *di*. When in doubt in a shop, the partitive from Lesson 6 (*del pane, della frutta*) is the safe, natural choice.

## Lesson 5: The Past Tense — Passato Prossimo

Italian tells completed past actions mainly with the *passato prossimo*, formed with the present of *avere* or *essere* plus the past participle. For regular verbs, the participle is formed by replacing *-are* with *-ato* (*parlato*), *-ere* with *-uto* (*veduto*, though many *-ere* verbs are irregular), and *-ire* with *-ito* (*partito, finito*). With *avere*, the participle does not change: *Ho parlato* (I spoke / I have spoken), *Hai mangiato* (You ate), *Abbiamo visto* — note *visto* is the irregular participle of *vedere*. With *essere*, the participle agrees with the subject: *Sono andata* (I went, feminine), *Sono andato* (masculine), *Siamo partiti* (We left, masculine or mixed group), *Siamo partite* (all-female group).

Which auxiliary? Most transitive verbs (verbs with a direct object) take *avere*: *Ho comprato un libro, Ho letto il giornale*. Verbs of movement and change of state, and all reflexive verbs, take *essere*: *andare* (sono andato), *venire* (sono venuto), *partire* (sono partito), *arrivare* (sono arrivato), *entrare, uscire, rimanere, diventare, nascere* (sono nato), *morire* (sono morto), and reflexives like *svegliarsi* (*Mi sono svegliato*). The mnemonic is not perfect, but movement and state-change verbs are the right starting guide; dictionaries mark the auxiliary for each verb.

Irregular participles must be memorised because they are extremely common: *fare → fatto* (done/made), *dire → detto* (said), *scrivere → scritto* (written), *leggere → letto* (read), *vedere → visto* (seen), *prendere → preso* (taken), *mettere → messo* (put), *aprire → aperto* (opened), *chiudere → chiuso* (closed), *bere → bevuto* (drunk), *conoscere → conosciuto* (known/met). Examples: *Ieri ho fatto la spesa* (Yesterday I did the shopping), *L'anno scorso sono andata a Roma* (Last year I went to Rome), *Abbiamo visto un bel film* (We saw a good film).

Time expressions anchor the past: *ieri* (yesterday), *ieri sera* (last night), *l'altro ieri* (the day before yesterday), *stamattina* (this morning), *la settimana scorsa* (last week), *il mese scorso* (last month), *l'anno scorso* (last year), *due giorni fa* (two days ago). Negation wraps the auxiliary: *Non ho parlato, Non sono andata*. Questions keep the same order: *Hai mangiato? Sei andato al mercato?*

A common mistake is using *essere* for every verb, or forgetting agreement with *essere*: *Sono andato* for a woman is wrong; it must be *Sono andata*. Another is translating the English present perfect and simple past as different Italian tenses at beginner level; in conversation, *passato prossimo* covers both "I went" and "I have gone" in most of Italy, while the *passato remoto* is a literary and southern spoken form you will meet later, not a beginner requirement.

**Practice exercise:** Write a diary entry for yesterday using ten *passato prossimo* verbs — five with *avere* and five with *essere* — including two irregular participles and one negative sentence. Then rewrite it changing the subject from *io* (feminine, if applicable, or masculine) to *noi* and correct every agreement.

**Extra worked examples — narrating a weekend:** *Sabato mattina mi sono svegliato presto, ho fatto colazione e sono uscito con gli amici. Abbiamo visitato un museo, abbiamo pranzato in un ristorante piccolo e abbiamo comprato dei regali. La sera siamo tornati a casa stanchi ma contenti.* (On Saturday morning I woke up early, had breakfast and went out with friends. We visited a museum, had lunch in a small restaurant and bought some presents. In the evening we came home tired but happy.) Notice the auxiliary switch: reflexive *svegliarsi* takes *essere*, *fare* takes *avere*, movement *uscire* and *tornare* take *essere*, while *visitare, pranzare* in the sense of having lunch, and *comprare* take *avere*. Agreement shows in *siamo tornati* (masculine/mixed) versus *siamo tornate* (all-female). Sequence words help narration: *prima* (first), *poi* (then), *dopo* (afterwards), *infine* (finally), *all'improvviso* (suddenly). Practise retelling the same weekend from a friend's point of view: *Lei si è svegliata presto, ha fatto colazione…* and watch every participle agreement change.

## Lesson 6: Food, Ordering, and Quantities

Food vocabulary is both practical and culturally rich, and ordering in Italy rewards politeness and a few set phrases. Key words: *il pane* (bread), *il formaggio* (cheese), *la pasta, il riso* (rice), *la carne* (meat), *il pesce* (fish), *la frutta* (fruit), *la verdura* (vegetables), *l'acqua* (water), *il vino* (wine), *la birra* (beer), *il caffè* (coffee), *il dolce* (dessert), *il conto* (the bill). Meals: *la colazione* (breakfast), *il pranzo* (lunch), *la cena* (dinner), *uno spuntino* (a snack). At a café, *un caffè* usually means an espresso; ask for *un cappuccino* or *un latte macchiato* if that is what you want, and note that cappuccino is traditionally a morning drink, though no one will refuse it later.

Useful phrases: *Vorrei…* (I would like…) is the polite standard, softer than *Voglio…* (I want…), which can sound demanding. *Vorrei un tavolo per due, per favore* (I would like a table for two, please). *Il menù, per favore. Avete un menù in inglese?* (The menu, please. Do you have a menu in English?) *Prendo…* (I'll have…) is natural when ordering: *Prendo la pasta al pomodoro*. *È buono?* (Is it good?), *Che cosa consiglia?* (What do you recommend?), *Sono allergico/a alle noci* (I am allergic to nuts — change the ending for gender), *Senza glutine, per favore* (Gluten-free, please). To pay: *Il conto, per favore. Posso pagare con la carta?* (The bill, please. Can I pay by card?)

Quantities use *di* plus the partitive article, or simply the noun after a quantity word: *un po' di pane* (a little bread), *un chilo di mele* (a kilo of apples), *un litro d'acqua* (a litre of water), *una bottiglia di vino* (a bottle of wine), *due etti di prosciutto* (two hundred grams of ham — *etto* is a common market unit). *Vorrei del formaggio* (I would like some cheese) uses *del* (*di + il*); *della frutta, degli spinaci, delle patate* follow the same articulated-preposition pattern from Lesson 4. In shops, *Mi dà…?* (Will you give me…?) is polite and common: *Mi dà tre etti di pomodori, per favore?*

Taste and preference adjectives: *buono* (good/tasty), *delizioso, saporito* (tasty), *dolce* (sweet), *salato* (salty), *piccante* (spicy), *amaro* (bitter), *fresco* (fresh), *caldo* (hot), *freddo* (cold). *Mi piace* (I like, literally "it pleases me") takes the thing liked as subject: *Mi piace la pasta* (singular), *Mi piacciono gli spaghetti* (plural). This indirect construction confuses learners: the verb agrees with the food, not with you. *Ti piace il pesce? Non mi piacciono le olive.*

A common mistake is ordering *un latte* expecting a milky coffee; in Italy that is a glass of milk. Another is saying *Voglio* to a waiter when *Vorrei* is expected. A third is using *piacere* as if it were "to like" directly: *Io piaccio la pasta* is wrong; it means "the pasta likes me" in a different sense entirely.

**Practice exercise:** Write a full restaurant dialogue for two people: greeting, table request, drinks, starters, mains, a dietary restriction, dessert, and paying. Include *vorrei, prendo, mi piace/mi piacciono*, a quantity phrase, and a polite question to the waiter. Read it aloud, checking every article and adjective ending.

**Extra worked examples — at the market and the café:** At a market stall: *— Buongiorno! Mi dà mezzo chilo di ciliegie? — Certo. Altro? — Sì, anche due etti di formaggio e un po' di basilico. — Sono cinque euro e cinquanta. — Grazie!* (Good morning! Will you give me half a kilo of cherries? Certainly. Anything else? Yes, also two hundred grams of cheese and a little basil. That's five euros fifty. Thank you!) Note *mezzo chilo* (half a kilo) and *un po' di* (a little of). At a café: *— Un cappuccino e un cornetto, per favore. — Al banco o al tavolo? — Al banco, grazie.* (A cappuccino and a croissant, please. At the counter or at a table? At the counter, thanks.) In Italy, drinking at the counter (*al banco*) is often cheaper than table service (*al tavolo* or *servizio al tavolo*), a practical detail worth knowing before you order. Other useful items: *il cornetto* (croissant), *la brioche* (same family), *il succo d'arancia* (orange juice), *la spremuta* (freshly squeezed juice), *l'acqua naturale* (still water) versus *l'acqua frizzante* (sparkling water).

## Lesson 7: Future, Plans, and the Weather

The future tense in Italian is formed from the infinitive, with endings added directly: for *-are* verbs, the *a* of the infinitive changes to *e* (*parlare → parler-*), and for *-ere* and *-ire* verbs the stem stays close to the infinitive. Endings are the same for all groups: io *-ò*, tu *-ai*, lui/lei *-à*, noi *-emo*, voi *-ete*, loro *-anno*. So *parlare* gives: parlerò, parlerai, parlerà, parleremo, parlerete, parleranno. *Vedere*: vedrò, vedrai, vedrà, vedremo, vedrete, vedranno — note the *e* drops. *Partire*: partirò, partirai, partirà, partiremo, partirete, partiranno. Irregular stems are common and worth memorising: *essere → sar-* (sarò), *avere → avr-* (avrò), *andare → andr-* (andrò), *fare → far-* (farò), *potere → potr-* (potrò), *volere → vorr-* (vorrò), *dovere → dovr-* (dovrò), *sapere → sapr-* (saprò), *venire → verr-* (verrò), *rimanere → rimarr-* (rimarrò), *vivere → vivr-* (vivrò).

In everyday speech, Italians often use the present tense for near-future plans, especially with a time expression: *Domani vado a Milano* (Tomorrow I am going to Milan) is more common than *Domani andrò a Milano*, though both are correct. The future is also used for probability and supposition: *Saranno le otto* (It must be about eight o'clock), *Avrà trent'anni* (He must be about thirty). This "future of probability" is a distinct use that English does not mark the same way, and recognising it prevents confusion when you hear it.

Plans and intentions also use *avere intenzione di* (to intend to), *pensare di* (to plan to / think of), and *stare per* (to be about to): *Ho intenzione di studiare italiano* (I intend to study Italian), *Penso di partire venerdì* (I plan to leave on Friday), *Sto per uscire* (I am about to go out). Days of the week: lunedì, martedì, mercoledì, giovedì, venerdì, sabato, domenica — note the first five end in an accent and do not change in the plural, and the article is *il lunedì* for "on Mondays" habitually but no article for a single day: *Vado lunedì* (I am going on Monday).

Weather vocabulary: *Che tempo fa?* (What is the weather like?), *Fa caldo* (It is hot), *Fa freddo* (It is cold), *Fa bel tempo / brutto tempo* (The weather is nice/bad), *Piove* (It is raining), *Nevica* (It is snowing), *C'è il sole* (It is sunny), *C'è vento* (It is windy), *È nuvoloso* (It is cloudy), *C'è nebbia* (It is foggy), *Fa umidità* is less common; *C'è afa* means muggy heat. Seasons: *la primavera, l'estate, l'autunno, l'inverno*. Temperatures: *Ci sono venti gradi* (It is twenty degrees).

Common mistakes include keeping the infinitive *a* in *-are* futures (*parlarò* is wrong; it is *parlerò*), forgetting the dropped *e* in *vedrò, potrò, saprò*, and overusing the future for tomorrow's plans where the present sounds more natural. Another is confusing *tempo* (weather/time) with *ora* (hour/time of day): *Che ore sono?* asks the time, *Che tempo fa?* asks the weather.

**Practice exercise:** Write your plans for next week, day by day, using five future-tense verbs (including one irregular stem), three present-tense near-future sentences, and a weather forecast for three days. Then rewrite one plan as a supposition: "It will be sunny" as a prediction versus "It must be sunny" as a guess, and explain the difference in Italian.

**Extra worked examples — making arrangements:** *— Che cosa fai sabato? — Sabato andrò a trovare mia sorella. Domenica resteremo a casa perché pioverà. — E la prossima settimana? — Lunedì avrò una riunione, martedì partirò per Venezia e tornerò giovedì.* (What are you doing on Saturday? On Saturday I'll go and visit my sister. On Sunday we'll stay home because it will rain. And next week? On Monday I'll have a meeting, on Tuesday I'll leave for Venice and I'll come back on Thursday.) Note the future after *perché* for a predicted reason (*perché pioverà*), and the present tense alternative that sounds equally natural for a fixed plan: *Martedì parto per Venezia*. For invitations, use the conditional or *volere* plus infinitive rather than a bare future: *Vorrei invitarti a cena* (I'd like to invite you to dinner), *Ti va di venire al cinema?* (Do you fancy coming to the cinema?). Replies: *Volentieri!* (Gladly!), *Mi dispiace, non posso* (I'm sorry, I can't), *Forse un'altra volta* (Maybe another time). The future of probability appears in small talk: *Sarà stanco* (He must be tired), *Avranno fame* (They must be hungry).

## Lesson 8: Travel, Directions, and Everyday Conversation

Travel Italian brings together everything so far: polite requests, numbers, directions, and past and future tenses. Start with transport words: *il treno* (train), *l'autobus* (bus), *la metropolitana* (underground), *il taxi, l'aereo* (plane), *la stazione* (station), *l'aeroporto* (airport), *il binario* (platform), *il biglietto* (ticket), *l'andata e ritorno* (return ticket), *la fermata* (stop). Key phrases: *Un biglietto per Firenze, per favore* (A ticket to Florence, please), *A che ora parte il treno?* (What time does the train leave?), *Da quale binario?* (From which platform?), *È diretto?* (Is it direct?), *Dove si cambia?* (Where do I change?), *Quanto dura il viaggio?* (How long is the journey?), *Scusi, dov'è la stazione?* (Excuse me, where is the station?).

Directions use imperative forms and location words: *a destra* (to the right), *a sinistra* (to the left), *dritto/diritto* (straight ahead), *vicino a* (near), *lontano da* (far from), *di fronte a* (opposite), *accanto a* (next to), *tra… e…* (between), *all'angolo* (on the corner). The polite imperative (Lei form) uses the present subjunctive forms for regular verbs, which beginners often meet as set phrases: *Vada dritto, poi giri a destra* (Go straight, then turn right), *Prenda la seconda strada a sinistra* (Take the second street on the left). The informal imperative for *-are* verbs swaps the *tu* ending: *parla!* becomes the command, while *-ere* and *-ire* keep their present forms: *prendi!, parti!* With *tu*, object pronouns attach to the end: *Dimmi!* (Tell me!), *Scusami!* (Excuse me!), *Guardami!* (Look at me!).

Everyday conversation fills in with small, high-frequency expressions: *Scusi* (excuse me, formal), *Scusa* (informal), *Permesso* (may I come through / may I enter), *Prego* (you're welcome / please / after you), *Grazie mille* (thank you very much), *Di niente* (you're welcome, literally "of nothing"), *Non c'è di che* (don't mention it), *Mi dispiace* (I am sorry), *Non importa* (it doesn't matter), *Va bene* (okay / that's fine), *D'accordo* (agreed), *Magari!* (If only! / I wish! / Maybe, with enthusiasm), *Allora…* (So… / Well…), *Infatti* (indeed / in fact), *Comunque* (anyway). Shopping phrases: *Sto solo guardando* (I am just looking), *Posso provare?* (Can I try it on?), *Avete una taglia più grande/piccola?* (Do you have a larger/smaller size?), *Quanto costa?* (How much does it cost?), *È troppo caro* (It is too expensive), *Prendo questo* (I'll take this one).

Numbers beyond twenty follow a clear pattern: ventuno, ventidue, ventitré, ventiquattro, venticinque, ventisei, ventisette, ventotto, ventinove, trenta, quaranta, cinquanta, sessanta, settanta, ottanta, novanta, cento, mille, duemila. Prices: *Costa dieci euro e cinquanta* (It costs ten euros fifty). Note *euro* does not change in the plural, and Italians say *un euro, due euro*.

Common mistakes: asking *Dov'è…?* without *Scusi* first, which can sound abrupt; using *tu* commands with strangers instead of the polite form; and confusing *a destra* with *diritto* (straight) — *diritto* as a noun also means "law" or "right," so context matters. Another is translating "I am looking for" as *Sto cercando per*; Italian says *Cerco…* with no preposition: *Cerco un albergo*.

**Practice exercise:** Write a travel day in Italian: arriving at a station, buying a ticket, asking directions to a hotel, checking in, and asking about dinner nearby. Use at least five direction words, three polite requests, one past-tense sentence about what you did, and one future-tense sentence about tomorrow. End with a short dialogue in a shop using *Quanto costa?* and a size question.

**Extra worked examples — handling problems politely:** Travel rarely goes perfectly, so learn repair phrases: *Non ho capito, può ripetere?* (I didn't understand, can you repeat?), *Può parlare più lentamente, per favore?* (Can you speak more slowly, please?), *Come si dice… in italiano?* (How do you say… in Italian?), *Ho perso il treno* (I missed the train), *Ho smarrito il passaporto* (I've lost my passport), *C'è un medico qui vicino?* (Is there a doctor nearby?), *Ho bisogno di aiuto* (I need help), *Chiamate un'ambulanza!* (Call an ambulance!). At a hotel: *Ho una prenotazione a nome Rossi* (I have a reservation under the name Rossi), *A che ora è la colazione?* (What time is breakfast?), *Posso lasciare i bagagli qui?* (Can I leave my luggage here?), *A che ora devo lasciare la camera?* (What time must I leave the room?). The conditional *vorrei* and *potrei* (could I) soften almost any request: *Potrei avere un'altra coperta?* (Could I have another blanket?), *Vorrei cambiare camera, se possibile* (I'd like to change rooms, if possible). Politeness in Italian is carried by these softeners and by *per favore, grazie,* and *scusi* far more than by elaborate grammar.
